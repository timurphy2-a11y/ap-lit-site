# Writing and Editing Guide

This guide is derived from editorial revisions made during Phase 4c (Sculpture sub-domain drafting). It applies to all content writing on this project: Sculpture pages, Science pages, Historical Moment pop-ups, hub page introductions, and editorial passes on existing content.

The organizing principle is precision over elaboration. Content should be exactly as long as the argument requires — no more, no less.

---

## I. Cutting Rules

These patterns appear in first drafts and should be removed in revision.

### 1. Cut throat-clearing openings
Opening sentences that explain what the reader is about to encounter rather than making the argument directly.

> ❌ "Medieval sculpture is not trying to do what Renaissance sculpture will do. It is not interested in..."
> ✓ Start with the argument: "Medieval sculpture serves a total theological program."

### 2. Cut hedging qualifications
Phrases that soften a claim without adding information.

> ❌ "if you look carefully," "strictly speaking," "in many ways," "I feel confident about," "it is worth noting that"
> ✓ Make the claim directly or cut it entirely.

### 3. Cut summarizing final sentences
Sentences that restate what the paragraph has just shown. If the examples made the point, the summary is redundant.

> ❌ "Together, the two *Davids* define the range of what Renaissance sculpture asks the body to do: to stand in its own achieved reality (Donatello) and to hold itself at the threshold of action (Michelangelo)."
> ✓ End at the last new piece of information.

### 4. Cut elaborative closing sentences from Connection sections
Connection sections that make their point and then restate it in a closing flourish should stop at the point of completion.

> ❌ "...the instant when something irreversible has begun and cannot be stopped."
> ✓ End at the last specific claim: "giving way to shame, labor, and mortality."

### 5. Cut the bridge sentence between examples
When two examples already demonstrate a principle, the sentence that explicitly connects them is redundant.

> ❌ "The same principle that makes Daphne's fingers already becoming leaves also governs..."
> ✓ Let the examples speak consecutively without an announced link.

### 6. Cut the scaffolding sentence that announces the connection
Connection sections that open by naming what they're about to argue should drop that opening and go directly to the argument.

> ❌ "The named maker is a Renaissance invention that *Hamlet* takes to its extreme."
> ✓ "Shakespeare's play is full of questions about authorship and attribution..."

### 7. Cut "not just... but also" constructions
These formulate a point as a negative correction before making it positively. State the positive directly.

> ❌ "Bernini was not just a sculptor but an architect, stage designer, and theatrical impresario — and he brought all of those instincts to bear on marble."
> ✓ "Bernini was also an architect, stage designer, and theatrical impresario; he applied all of those varied skills to his sculpture."

### 8. Shift second person to first person plural in general-reader passages
"You" is appropriate when directing a viewer's attention to a specific work (What to Notice sections). In analytical prose addressing a general reader, prefer "we" or impersonal constructions.

> ❌ "you pass beneath the judgment whether you believe it or not"
> ✓ "We pass beneath the judgment whether we believe it or not."

## II. Revision Rules

These patterns should be replaced rather than simply cut.

### 9. Replace em-dashes with colons or semicolons for causal or elaborative relationships
The em-dash implies digression; the colon announces elaboration cleanly.

> ❌ "stands in easy *contrapposto* after the fight is over — relaxed, self-possessed"
> ✓ "stands in easy *contrapposto* after the fight is over: relaxed, self-possessed"

Reserve em-dashes for genuine interruptions, parenthetical asides, or list-like elaborations where the colon would be ambiguous.

### 10. Replace metaphorical payoffs with precise description
Where a paragraph ends with a rhetorical flourish, consider whether precise description serves better.

> ❌ "Michelangelo has made the marble do what marble cannot do: hold a grief larger than any body."
> ✓ "Michelangelo's exquisite skill allows cold marble to hold a grief larger than any actual human body."

This is a judgment call, not an absolute rule — but the instinct should be toward precision.

### 11. Tighten "both... and" closing constructions
Paragraphs that close by symmetrically summarizing two things just argued can usually be collapsed or cut.

> ❌ "The shift from the Renaissance to the Baroque is, in sculptural terms, the same shift visible in the painting: from a world the human figure commands to a world in which the human figure is caught up in something overwhelming."
> ✓ End the paragraph where the last specific example lands, not with the announced summary.

## III. Adding Rules

Concision is not the only goal. These patterns should be added in revision where they are missing from first drafts.

### 12. Add specific textual or visual detail where a claim is asserted without grounding
A general claim about a work or text should be accompanied by the specific image, phrase, or detail that demonstrates it. Do not assert what can be shown.

> ❌ "When Satan crosses Chaos, the reader feels the scale of the Miltonic universe."
> ✓ "When Satan crosses Chaos in Book II on his way to discover the 'pendent' Earth that hangs below Heaven, the reader feels the vertiginous scale of the Miltonic universe."

### 13. Add the specific word that makes the claim irreversible
Where a claim is almost there but slightly soft, one precise word often completes it.

> ❌ "Adam and Eve are changed by their choice"
> ✓ "Adam and Eve are *irrevocably* changed by their choice"

## IV. Structural Rules

These apply at the paragraph and section level.

### 14. Protected sections: do not cut
The following sections carry essential content and should not be reduced in editorial passes:
- **Thread sections** (the main analytical argument per thread)
- **"What to Notice" / "What to Listen For"** sections
- **Thread core arguments** in the YAML `period_summary` fields

### 15. Word count targets (from Phase 4 planning)
These are targets for the editorial pass and for drafting new content:

| Section type | Target |
|---|---|
| Page introduction | ≤200 words |
| Thread section (main argument) | No target — preserve substance |
| "Connection to [anchor text]" | ≤120 words (philosophy); ≤100 words (art and music) |
| "Looking Back / Looking Forward" | ≤200 words (philosophy); ≤100 words (art and music) |
| "What to Notice / Listen For" | No target — preserve substance |

### 16. No redundancy between sections
If the introduction has explained the Baroque sublime, the thread section should not re-explain it. Each section should add new content, not restate what another section has already established.

### 17. No redundancy between hub page and domain pages
Hub page introductions establish the period's broad character. Domain pages should not repeat that material — they should apply it to their specific domain.

## V. Voice and Register

### 18. Write for a student reader who is intelligent but unfamiliar
Assume AP-level reading ability but no prior knowledge of art history or philosophy. Technical terms should be introduced in bold on first use and defined in context, not in parenthetical glosses.

> ✓ "**Hieratic scale** — size indicating spiritual rank rather than physical reality — organizes every figure in the composition."

### 19. Prefer active constructions
Passive voice distances the argument from its subject. Reserve it for cases where the agent is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.

> ❌ "The figure is shown at the moment of maximum muscular effort."
> ✓ "Puget shows the figure at the moment of maximum muscular effort."

### 20. End paragraphs on the strongest word or image, not on a conjunction or abstraction
The last word of a paragraph carries disproportionate weight. Avoid ending on "as well," "too," "also," "in this way," or abstract nouns like "reality," "meaning," "significance."

> ❌ "...which is precisely the shift from Hamlet's questioning to Milton's cosmic drama."
> ✓ "...the shift from Hamlet's questioning to *Paradise Lost*."

## VI. Additional Rules (from Phase 4c editing passes)

### 21. Cut throat-clearing wherever it occurs, not only at openings
Mid-paragraph sentences that announce what the surrounding sentences already demonstrate should be removed. This applies throughout paragraphs, not just at their openings.

> ❌ "This is not incompetence; it is a statement about the relationship between the maker, the material, and the work. The struggle between form and formlessness is itself the subject."
> ✓ Cut the first sentence. The second makes the point directly.

### 22. Integrate parallel examples rather than juxtaposing them
When two examples illuminate the same point, weave them into a single sentence rather than holding them at arm's length as parallel clauses. The connection becomes an argument rather than a comparison.

> ❌ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years; Ahab pursuing a creature that may be beyond reach — both are images of the Romantic will."
> ✓ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years without finishing is a figure of obsessive toil comparable to mad Ahab, driving the *Pequod* across every ocean in pursuit of a creature that may well be beyond human reach."

### 23. Prefer accuracy to rhetorical sharpness when they conflict
A punchy formulation that slightly misrepresents its subject should be revised toward precision even at the cost of elegance.

> ❌ "Duchamp will ask whether a mass-produced urinal is a sculpture at all."
> ✓ "Duchamp will provocatively question the boundaries of fine art by presenting a mass-produced urinal as sculpture."

### 24. Signal interpretive claims as readings, not facts
When a claim about a text is genuinely interpretive rather than descriptive, use "can be read as" or "reads as" rather than "is."

> ❌ "Melville's novel is a book about a man who cannot stop working."
> ✓ "Melville's novel can be read as a book about a man who cannot stop working."

### 25. Make the second term of a comparison more specific than the first
When two things are compared in parallel, the second should land harder — with more specific detail — not at the same level of generality.

> ❌ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years; Ahab pursuing a creature that may be beyond reach."
> ✓ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years without finishing is a figure of obsessive toil comparable to mad Ahab, driving the *Pequod* across every ocean in pursuit of a creature that may well be beyond human reach."

### 26. Add narrative context when a work's meaning depends on sequence of events
When an argument about a text depends on something that happens at a specific point in the narrative, name that moment.

> ❌ "The *Pequod* is a public vessel with a commercial purpose. But what happens aboard is profoundly private."
> ✓ "The *Pequod* is a public vessel with a commercial purpose. But what happens aboard *after Ahab turns the Pequod into an instrument of revenge* is profoundly private."

### 27. When a parallel is strong but not identical, name the difference
If the comparison is an analogy rather than an identity, say so — name how the parallel works and what distinguishes the two terms. Overclaiming a parallel weakens it.

> ❌ "That refusal is what Modernist sculpture, at its best, also performs."
> ✓ "That refusal expressed through spatial rearrangement is a sharp analogue to the impulse that drives much Modernist sculpture."

### 28. Replace "this is not X; it is Y" with the positive claim alone
The negative correction adds nothing when the positive claim is self-sufficient.

> ❌ "This is not calm; it is controlled intensity."
> ✓ "This is controlled intensity."

### 29. Collapse two-sentence elaborations into participial phrases
"X is Y. It does Z" constructions can usually be condensed: "X is Y, doing Z." The participial phrase carries the elaboration without requiring a new sentence.

> ❌ "The Church needed art that would move people — not merely instruct them but overwhelm their senses and produce conviction through feeling."
> ✓ "The Church needed art that would move people, overwhelming their senses and producing conviction through strong feeling."

### 30. Absorb a closing summary into the final substantive sentence
Rather than adding a separate sentence that announces what two things share, fold the shared quality into the last specific claim.

> ❌ "Canova's figures achieve a serenity that looks back to ancient Greece. They have absorbed the Baroque's illusionistic ambitions but disciplined them."
> ✓ "Canova's figures achieve a serenity that looks back to ancient Greece, absorbing the Baroque's illusionistic ambitions but disciplining them to quieter purpose."

### 31. One precise adjective over a subordinate clause
Where a claim requires a clause to complete it, ask whether a single well-chosen adjective does the same work more cleanly.

> ❌ "caught at the precise moment when something irreversible has begun and cannot be stopped"
> ✓ "caught at the instant of irrevocable motion"

### 32. Cut "a sculpture that X also Y" closing formulations
When a sentence closes by announcing a second implication of what was just argued, fold it into the argument or cut it.

> ❌ "A sculpture that announces the autonomous individual also announces the autonomous city."
> ✓ Let the *David*'s placement in the Piazza della Signoria carry that implication without announcing it.

*This guide should be updated as new patterns emerge during Phase 4 drafting. When a revision reveals a principle not captured here, add it.*
