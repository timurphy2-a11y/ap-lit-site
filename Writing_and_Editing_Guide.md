# Writing and Editing Guide

The organizing principle is precision over elaboration. Content should be exactly as long as the argument requires — no more, no less.

**Word-count targets were retired in August 2026.** The structural changes to the period pages (Connection sections behind a disclosure link, visual elements on philosophy pages, images inline rather than pooled in a gallery) removed the pressure the caps were managing. Length is now governed by the rules below, not by a number.

---

## I. Cutting Rules

These patterns appear in first drafts and should be removed in revision.

### 1. Cut throat-clearing openings
Opening sentences that explain what the reader is about to encounter rather than making the argument directly.

> ❌ "Medieval sculpture is not trying to do what Renaissance sculpture will do. It is not interested in..."
> ✓ Start with the argument: "Medieval sculpture serves a total theological program."

### 2. Cut hedging qualifications
Phrases that soften a claim without adding information.

> ❌ "if you look carefully," "strictly speaking," "in many ways," "I feel confident about," "it is worth noting that"
> ✓ Make the claim directly or cut it entirely.

### 3. Cut summarizing final sentences
Sentences that restate what the paragraph has just shown. If the examples made the point, the summary is redundant.

> ❌ "Together, the two *Davids* define the range of what Renaissance sculpture asks the body to do: to stand in its own achieved reality (Donatello) and to hold itself at the threshold of action (Michelangelo)."
> ✓ End at the last new piece of information.

### 4. Cut elaborative closing sentences
Any subsection that makes its point and then restates it in a closing flourish should stop at the point of completion.

> ❌ "...the instant when something irreversible has begun and cannot be stopped."
> ✓ End at the last specific claim: "giving way to shame, labor, and mortality."

### 5. Cut the bridge sentence between examples
When two examples already demonstrate a principle, the sentence that explicitly connects them is redundant.

> ❌ "The same principle that makes Daphne's fingers already becoming leaves also governs..."
> ✓ Let the examples speak consecutively without an announced link.

### 6. Cut the scaffolding sentence that announces the argument
Sections that open by naming what they're about to argue should drop that opening and go directly to the argument.

> ❌ "The named maker is a Renaissance invention that *Hamlet* takes to its extreme."
> ✓ "Shakespeare's play is full of questions about authorship and attribution..."

### 7. Cut "not just... but also" constructions
These formulate a point as a negative correction before making it positively. State the positive directly.

> ❌ "Bernini was not just a sculptor but an architect, stage designer, and theatrical impresario — and he brought all of those instincts to bear on marble."
> ✓ "Bernini was also an architect, stage designer, and theatrical impresario; he applied all of those varied skills to his sculpture."

### 8. Avoid second person in analytical prose
Second person ("you," "your") is prohibited in analytical writing. It is either too informal or implies a direct address that doesn't belong. The exception is genuinely instructional contexts, where direct address is appropriate: "Notice the hands," "Look at the Virgin's face." On this site those contexts are exactly two: **"What to Notice" / "What to Listen For"** sections, and the **`**Prompt:**`** lines beneath Gallery and Hero entries. Body prose inside a Gallery entry is analytical, not instructional, and second person is prohibited there.

In analytical prose, replace second person with first person plural ("we," "our"), third person, or impersonal constructions:

> ❌ "Your place in this chain is not something you chose or can change."
> ✓ "One's place in this chain is not something one chose or can change."

## II. Revision Rules

These patterns should be replaced rather than simply cut.

### 9. Replace em-dashes with colons or semicolons for causal or elaborative relationships
The em-dash implies digression; the colon announces elaboration cleanly.

> ❌ "stands in easy *contrapposto* after the fight is over — relaxed, self-possessed"
> ✓ "stands in easy *contrapposto* after the fight is over: relaxed, self-possessed"

Reserve em-dashes for genuine interruptions, parenthetical asides, or list-like elaborations where the colon would be ambiguous.

### 10. Replace metaphorical payoffs with precise description
Where a paragraph ends with a rhetorical flourish, consider whether precise description serves better.

> ❌ "Michelangelo has made the marble do what marble cannot do: hold a grief larger than any body."
> ✓ "Michelangelo's exquisite skill allows cold marble to hold a grief larger than any actual human body."

This is a judgment call, not an absolute rule — but the instinct should be toward precision.

### 11. Tighten "both... and" closing constructions
Paragraphs that close by symmetrically summarizing two things just argued can usually be collapsed or cut.

> ❌ "The shift from the Renaissance to the Baroque is, in sculptural terms, the same shift visible in the painting: from a world the human figure commands to a world in which the human figure is caught up in something overwhelming."
> ✓ End the paragraph where the last specific example lands, not with the announced summary.

## III. Adding Rules

Concision is not the only goal. These patterns should be added in revision where they are missing from first drafts.

### 12. Add specific detail where a claim is asserted without grounding
A general claim about a work or text should be accompanied by the specific image, phrase, or detail that demonstrates it. Do not assert what can be shown.

> ❌ "When Satan crosses Chaos, the reader feels the scale of the Miltonic universe."
> ✓ "When Satan crosses Chaos in Book II on his way to discover the 'pendent' Earth that hangs below Heaven, the reader feels the vertiginous scale of the Miltonic universe."

### 13. Add the specific word that makes the claim irreversible
Where a claim is almost there but slightly soft, one precise word often completes it.

> ❌ "Adam and Eve are changed by their choice"
> ✓ "Adam and Eve are *irrevocably* changed by their choice"

## IV. Structural Rules

These apply at the paragraph and section level.

### 14. No redundancy between sections
If an introduction has explained a concept, the body should not re-explain it. Each section should add new content, not restate what another section has already established.

### 15. No redundancy between overview and detail
Overview-level writing establishes the broad frame. More specific writing should apply that frame, not restate it.

## V. Voice and Register

### 16. Write for an intelligent but unfamiliar reader
Assume strong reading ability but no prior subject knowledge. Technical terms should be introduced in bold on first use and defined in context, not in parenthetical glosses.

> ✓ "**Hieratic scale** — size indicating spiritual rank rather than physical reality — organizes every figure in the composition."

### 17. Prefer active constructions
Passive voice distances the argument from its subject. Reserve it for cases where the agent is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.

> ❌ "The figure is shown at the moment of maximum muscular effort."
> ✓ "Puget shows the figure at the moment of maximum muscular effort."

### 18. End paragraphs on the strongest word or image, not on a conjunction or abstraction
The last word of a paragraph carries disproportionate weight. Avoid ending on "as well," "too," "also," "in this way," or abstract nouns like "reality," "meaning," "significance."

> ❌ "...which is precisely the shift from Hamlet's questioning to Milton's cosmic drama."
> ✓ "...the shift from Hamlet's questioning to *Paradise Lost*."

## VI. Additional Rules

### 19. Cut throat-clearing wherever it occurs, not only at openings
Mid-paragraph sentences that announce what the surrounding sentences already demonstrate should be removed.

> ❌ "This is not incompetence; it is a statement about the relationship between the maker, the material, and the work. The struggle between form and formlessness is itself the subject."
> ✓ Cut the first sentence. The second makes the point directly.

### 20. Integrate parallel examples rather than juxtaposing them
When two examples illuminate the same point, weave them into a single sentence rather than holding them at arm's length as parallel clauses. The connection becomes an argument rather than a comparison.

> ❌ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years; Ahab pursuing a creature that may be beyond reach — both are images of the Romantic will."
> ✓ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years without finishing is a figure of obsessive toil comparable to mad Ahab, driving the *Pequod* across every ocean in pursuit of a creature that may well be beyond human reach."

### 21. Prefer accuracy to rhetorical sharpness when they conflict
A punchy formulation that slightly misrepresents its subject should be revised toward precision even at the cost of elegance.

> ❌ "Duchamp will ask whether a mass-produced urinal is a sculpture at all."
> ✓ "Duchamp will provocatively question the boundaries of fine art by presenting a mass-produced urinal as sculpture."

### 22. Signal interpretive claims as readings, not facts
When a claim about a text is genuinely interpretive rather than descriptive, use "can be read as" or "reads as" rather than "is."

> ❌ "Melville's novel is a book about a man who cannot stop working."
> ✓ "Melville's novel can be read as a book about a man who cannot stop working."

### 23. Make the second term of a comparison more specific than the first
When two things are compared in parallel, the second should land harder — with more specific detail — not at the same level of generality.

> ❌ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years; Ahab pursuing a creature that may be beyond reach."
> ✓ "Rodin working on the *Gates of Hell* for thirty-seven years without finishing is a figure of obsessive toil comparable to mad Ahab, driving the *Pequod* across every ocean in pursuit of a creature that may well be beyond human reach."

### 24. Add narrative context when a work's meaning depends on sequence of events
When an argument about a text depends on something that happens at a specific point in the narrative, name that moment.

> ❌ "The *Pequod* is a public vessel with a commercial purpose. But what happens aboard is profoundly private."
> ✓ "The *Pequod* is a public vessel with a commercial purpose. But what happens aboard *after Ahab turns the Pequod into an instrument of revenge* is profoundly private."

### 25. When a parallel is strong but not identical, name the difference
If the comparison is an analogy rather than an identity, say so — name how the parallel works and what distinguishes the two terms. Overclaiming a parallel weakens it.

> ❌ "That refusal is what Modernist sculpture, at its best, also performs."
> ✓ "That refusal expressed through spatial rearrangement is a sharp analogue to the impulse that drives much Modernist sculpture."

### 26. Replace "this is not X; it is Y" with the positive claim alone
The negative correction adds nothing when the positive claim is self-sufficient.

> ❌ "This is not calm; it is controlled intensity."
> ✓ "This is controlled intensity."

### 27. Collapse two-sentence elaborations into participial phrases
"X is Y. It does Z" constructions can usually be condensed: "X is Y, doing Z." The participial phrase carries the elaboration without requiring a new sentence.

> ❌ "The Church needed art that would move people — not merely instruct them but overwhelm their senses and produce conviction through feeling."
> ✓ "The Church needed art that would move people, overwhelming their senses and producing conviction through strong feeling."

### 28. Absorb a closing summary into the final substantive sentence
Rather than adding a separate sentence that announces what two things share, fold the shared quality into the last specific claim.

> ❌ "Canova's figures achieve a serenity that looks back to ancient Greece. They have absorbed the Baroque's illusionistic ambitions but disciplined them."
> ✓ "Canova's figures achieve a serenity that looks back to ancient Greece, absorbing the Baroque's illusionistic ambitions but disciplining them to quieter purpose."

### 29. One precise adjective over a subordinate clause
Where a claim requires a clause to complete it, ask whether a single well-chosen adjective does the same work more cleanly.

> ❌ "caught at the precise moment when something irreversible has begun and cannot be stopped"
> ✓ "caught at the instant of irrevocable motion"

### 30. Cut closing formulations that announce a second implication
When a sentence closes by announcing a second implication of what was just argued, fold it into the argument or cut it.

> ❌ "A sculpture that announces the autonomous individual also announces the autonomous city."
> ✓ Let the *David*'s placement in the Piazza della Signoria carry that implication without announcing it.

### 31. Avoid second person when describing conditions that apply to people generally
Rule 8 bans second person throughout analytical prose. This applies with particular force in passages that describe how a historical, social, or philosophical framework applied to human beings generally — second person implies the reader is personally subject to those conditions.

> ❌ "Your place in this chain is not something you chose or can change. Your task is not to reinvent yourself."
> ✓ "One's place in this chain is not something one chose or can change. The human task is not self-reinvention."

### 32. Ground claims about texts in specific sequential evidence, not just thematic parallel
When a text offers a sequence of moments that complicates a simple thematic claim, show the sequence rather than asserting the parallel. Where a character does something that looks one way in one scene and another way in the next, the sequence is the argument.

> ❌ "Hamlet's graveyard scene is saturated with the Medieval conviction that the body is temporary."
> ✓ "Hamlet literally enacts a memento mori when he contemplates Yorick's skull — with no mention of the afterlife. But a scene later he accepts that there is 'special providence in the fall of a sparrow.' The Medieval and Renaissance are in genuine tension within the same scene sequence, not just as opposing worldviews."

### 33. When a parallel is established by examples, don't restate it with a confirming quotation
If two examples already demonstrate a structural parallel, a quotation that confirms it is redundant — and usually less precise, since quotations tend to broaden what the example has narrowed.

> ❌ "The Medieval painter disappears into the devotional program; the courtier disappears into the king's service. What Hamlet cannot do — what his Renaissance consciousness refuses — is disappear. 'I know not seems,' he insists. His grief is *his*, not a performance of grief in a role assigned by the court."
> ✓ "The Medieval painter disappears into the devotional program, just as Rosencrantz and Guildenstern disappear into court function."

Stop when the parallel is established.

### 34. Cut figurative language in analytical or instructional prose unless it adds precision direct statement cannot achieve
Metaphor and figurative language in expository or instructional writing should earn their place by doing analytical work, not by creating atmosphere or rhetorical warmth. If the figurative phrase can be replaced by a direct statement without loss of meaning, replace it.

> ❌ "...deliberately alive with more meaning than any one reading can exhaust."
> ✓ "A poem worth reading carefully almost always contains tension, contradiction, or ambiguity that resists easy explanation."

This is distinct from Rule 10 (which addresses metaphorical payoffs at paragraph endings). This applies to figurative language anywhere in analytical or instructional prose.

### 35. Compress lists of questions by merging those that address the same underlying concern
When a series of interpretive questions contains two or more that probe the same aspect of a text or speaker, merge them into one. Separate questions that share an underlying concern create the impression of more analytical coverage than actually exists, and compression produces tighter, more useful content.

> ❌ "What is the speaker's emotional and psychological condition at the start of the poem? How does the speaker's condition, understanding, or perspective change — or fail to change — over the course of the poem?"
> ✓ "What is the speaker's emotional and psychological condition at the start of the poem? How does that condition change over the course of the poem?"

The merged version asks the same thing in less space and loses nothing.

### 36. Anchor abstractions in the concrete phrase that earns them
When an abstract claim is introduced, ground it in a concrete descriptor or gerund rather than stating it in isolation. An abstraction that is *felt* as well as understood does more work than one that is merely accurate.

> ❌ "A poem is a movement of thought, feeling, or perception from one state to another."
> ✓ "A poem is a record of a mind in motion, shifting thought, feeling, or perception from one state to another."

### 37. Cut sentences that pre-announce a distinction the following examples already make
When examples immediately follow and do the work of a framing contrast, the contrast sentence is redundant scaffolding. This applies with particular force to "not X; it is Y" constructions (see Rule 26), but extends to any sentence whose sole function is to name a distinction that the surrounding content demonstrates without announcement.

> ❌ "The central question is not 'what techniques does this poem use?' but 'how does this poem change?'" [followed immediately by examples showing the change]
> ✓ Cut the framing contrast; let the examples carry it.

### 38. Cut evaluative qualifiers from method claims
In instructional prose that describes habits of attention, strip evaluative qualifiers from statements about what poems do or what readers should notice. The habits apply to all poems, not only excellent ones.

> ❌ "Most poems *worth reading carefully* have at least one moment where something changes."
> ✓ "Most poems have at least one moment of significant change."

### 39. Cut meta-claims about what matters more; let structure enact priority
When a series of questions or examples already demonstrates an analytical hierarchy (e.g., *why* before *where*), don't add a sentence announcing which matters more. The structure makes the argument; the announcement is throat-clearing at the method level.

> ❌ "Identifying that moment is less important than understanding what changes and what the change costs or produces." [followed by questions that ask *why* and *what it produces*]
> ✓ Cut the meta-claim; let the questions enact the priority.

### 40. Protected sections: do not cut
These sections carry essential content and are not reduced in editorial passes:
- **Thread sections** (the main analytical argument per thread)
- **"What to Notice" / "What to Listen For"** sections
- **Thread core arguments** in the YAML `period_summary` fields

### 41. Define by what a thing does, not by what it lacks
Stacked negations ("no harmony, no bass line, no chords, no accompaniment") characterize a period
by subtraction from a later one, which flatters the later period and tells the reader nothing about
what is actually happening. State the positive organizing principle, then let one negation mark the
contrast if it still earns its place.

> ❌ "There is no harmony in the modern sense, no bass line, no chords, no accompaniment."
> ✓ "A single melodic line carries the whole musical argument, its shape unsupported by anything beneath it."

This extends Rule 1 (whose ❌ example is itself a stacked negation) and Rule 26.

## VII. Typography

### 42. Titles of works
Italicize works published as standalone volumes, even short ones (*Nature*, *Existentialism Is a Humanism*). Book-length works stay italic even if first serialized (*Notes from Underground*, Schiller's *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man*). Put works published within a journal, collection or larger work in quotation marks ("An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?", "A Defence of Poetry", "Benito Cereno"). Italicize periodicals (*Rheinische Zeitung*). In music, italicize standalone compositions (motets, organa, masses, operas, symphonies); put songs and excerpts in quotation marks ("Erlkönig"; *Messiah*: "Hallelujah Chorus"). Numbered or generic titles stay roman (Symphony No. 9). Use double quotation marks, with commas and periods inside.
