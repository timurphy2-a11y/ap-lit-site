## Unit 00 — The High Middle Ages (Medieval)


### Painting


#### 1. **Annunciation with St. Margaret and St. Ansanus** — Simone Martini  
*Hero · id: `hero-martini`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The gold is not sky
- **Analysis:** The gold behind Gabriel and the Virgin is not a sky but a sign of divine presence. Both figures are lit evenly from no identifiable source and cast no shadow; they seem to float rather than stand on solid ground. Even the space between them is symbolic — the vase of lilies, the words of the angel's greeting inscribed across the gold — rather than architectural.
- **Prompt:** Look for a horizon line or a light source. You will not find either — and that absence is the whole point.


#### 2. **The Maestà** — Duccio · 1311  
*Inline work-plate · id: `duccio-maesta`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Rank, not distance
- **Analysis:** Duccio stacks the heavenly court in tiers rather than in depth. The enthroned Virgin is rendered far larger than the saints and angels ranked around her — her scale announces her rank, not her nearness to us. There is no floor receding to a horizon; the gold ground refuses physical space altogether, so the eye reads position as spiritual hierarchy.
- **Prompt:** Notice how the rows stack *upward* instead of shrinking into the distance.


#### 3. **The Wilton Diptych — Right Panel** — Anonymous · 1395  
*Inline work-plate · id: `wilton-right`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Light without a source
- **Analysis:** The Virgin and Child stand among eleven angels on a ground of burnished gold. The light has no source and throws no shadow — every face is lit with the same even radiance. What glows here is not sunlight but the sacred itself; the ultramarine blue and gold leaf do the work that modeled light and shade will do in later centuries.
- **Prompt:** Look for a single light source. You won't find one — the gold *is* the light.


#### 4. **The Wilton Diptych — Left Panel** — Anonymous · 1395  
*Inline work-plate · id: `wilton-left`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The vanished hand
- **Analysis:** Richard II kneels, presented by three saints. The surface is smooth as enamel and the brushwork is invisible; the panel reads as a jewel rather than as a record of the hand that made it. The painter's individuality is deliberately effaced in favor of a flawless devotional finish — craft in service of the sacred, not of the maker's fame.
- **Prompt:** Try to find a single visible brushstroke.


### Sculpture


#### 1. **Last Judgment Tympanum** — Gislebertus  
*Hero · id: `hero-tympanum`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Bodies as spiritual states
- **Analysis:** Begin with scale: Christ dominates from his central mandorla while the souls weighed by the archangel Michael occupy a register below. The damned are contorted and elongated, fingers reaching desperately upward — a giant hand clamps down on a head — while the saved stand upright, calm, and orderly. Every body is readable as a spiritual condition, its size and posture assigned by rank rather than anatomy.
- **Prompt:** Find Gislebertus's signature at the base of the Christ figure — "Gislebertus hoc fecit," a maker's mark beneath the feet of Christ in judgment.


#### 2. **Royal Portal** — Unknown · 1145  
*Inline work-plate · id: `chartres-portal`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Becoming stone
- **Analysis:** Notice how the column figures are absorbed into the architectural shafts — barely separated from the stone itself, their bodies elongated to match the proportions of the columns. Kings, queens, patriarchs, and prophets stand as presences, not portraits, arranged to represent salvation history. The building is using them as much as they are inhabiting it.
- **Prompt:** The three west doorways organize the whole facade around a hierarchy — to approach the cathedral is to walk through prophecy toward the eternal.


### Music


#### 1. **Ave Maris Stella** — Anonymous · Plainchant hymn · Medieval · 3–4 min  
*Hero · id: `sdJ55DPsWhI`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Pure monophony
- **Analysis:** Listen for the purity of the single unaccompanied line — no harmony, no bass, no rhythm section. Notice how the melody rises and falls with the Latin words rather than following a regular beat, expanding on the important syllables and moving simply through the rest. The effect is timeless and meditative, existing outside the pulse-driven framework of virtually all later Western music.
- **Prompt:** The hymn addresses the Virgin Mary as “Star of the Sea” — a single voice turned wholly toward devotion, the sonic baseline against which every later period will be heard.


#### 2. **Dies Irae** — Anonymous · Gregorian chant · 13th century  
*Inline listening · id: `h3FED3omlDA`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Monophony made dramatic
- **Analysis:** The same monophonic principle applies here — one melodic line, no harmony — but the melody is far more dramatic and memorable. This is the chant that has haunted Western music for centuries, quoted in everything from Berlioz's *Symphonie fantastique* to modern film scores. Even at its most vivid, it never abandons the single-line texture: all the drama lives in the shape of one voice.
- **Prompt:** Follow the melody alone — its power comes entirely from its contour, not from any accompaniment beneath it.


#### 3. **Viderunt Omnes** — Pérotin · c. 1198 · Early polyphony  
*Inline listening · id: `Q2JvIyStzNA`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · The first simultaneous voices
- **Analysis:** This is the crucial transitional piece: for the first time you can hear multiple voices singing different notes *simultaneously*. Sustained bass notes hold beneath faster-moving upper voices, producing an extraordinary, almost otherworldly sound — the first glimmers of polyphony emerging from monophony. And because simultaneous pitches now exist, so does the very possibility of consonance and dissonance.
- **Prompt:** Listen to how the held lower notes and the moving upper lines rub against and then resolve into each other — harmony, and with it the whole question of tension and release, being born.

## Unit 01 — Renaissance & Reformation


### Painting


#### 1. **The Creation of Adam** — Michelangelo Buonarroti  
*Hero · id: `hero-adam`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The measurable gap
- **Analysis:** The human figure now occupies a physically convincing space. Adam's body has weight, volume, and anatomical reality that would be unthinkable in medieval painting — muscle and bone, not floating symbol. The gap between the two reaching fingers dramatizes the Renaissance idea that the human being and the divine are separate figures in a measurable space, reaching toward each other across a felt distance.
- **Prompt:** Measure the distance between the fingertips with your eye. That charged, empty inch is the whole argument of the picture.


#### 2. **The School of Athens** — Raphael · 1511  
*Inline work-plate · id: `school-of-athens`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Everything converges
- **Analysis:** Find the vanishing point at the dead center of the composition, between Plato and Aristotle. Every architectural line — the coffered vaults, the floor tiles, the receding arches — converges on that single point, pulling your eye into a deep, rationally organized space. The philosophers of antiquity stand on a solid, measurable floor, gathered in an ideal humanist architecture where reason itself is given a stage.
- **Prompt:** Trace the ceiling arches with your finger — they all aim at the same spot. That spot is *you*, the positioned viewer.


#### 3. **The Alba Madonna** — Raphael · 1510  
*Inline work-plate · id: `alba-madonna`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Geometry as grace
- **Analysis:** Raphael resolves the difficult circular (tondo) format through a stable pyramidal composition: the Virgin's body forms a broad triangle whose apex is her head, anchoring the three figures in serene equilibrium. She sits directly on the ground in a real landscape with a distant horizon — no gold, no hierarchy of scale, only weighted bodies in measurable space. Harmony here is not decoration; it is the Renaissance conviction that the world is intelligible.
- **Prompt:** Notice how the round frame never feels awkward — the figures are engineered to fill it.


#### 4. **The Last Supper** — Leonardo da Vinci · 1498  
*Inline work-plate · id: `last-supper`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Light from the left
- **Analysis:** Light falls consistently from the left, matching the real windows of the refectory it was painted for, so that the painted room seems to extend the actual one. Those shadows confirm the solidity of every surface — table, wall, drapery, hands. Leonardo binds this naturalistic light to one-point perspective centered on Christ's head, making the calm at the center of a sudden emotional storm both a geometric and a luminous fact.
- **Prompt:** Follow the shadows: they all agree on where the light is. Nothing floats.


#### 5. **The Ambassadors** — Hans Holbein the Younger · 1533  
*Inline work-plate · id: `ambassadors`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Legibility and its limit
- **Analysis:** Take inventory of the objects on the lower shelf before looking at the figures — instruments of navigation, measurement, and music, the tools of the rational humanist world, each surface (silk, fur, wood, metal) rendered so precisely the brushwork vanishes. Then find the grey shape stretched across the foreground and try to resolve it. The perspective that organizes everything else cannot organize this shape; you have to abandon your own vantage point, step to the side, and only then does the skull snap into focus.
- **Prompt:** The flawless surface that renders the world also hides a death's-head in plain sight. Legibility has a limit.


### Sculpture


#### 1. **David** — Michelangelo Buonarroti  
*Hero · id: `hero-david`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The instant before
- **Analysis:** Scale registers first — over seventeen feet of marble, engineered to be read from below. The hands are slightly too large for the body, a deliberate distortion that emphasizes David's grip on the sling. The face turns left, brow furrowed, eyes fixed on a point where Goliath presumably stands. Every muscle is articulated — the veins in the hand, the tension across the shoulders, the weight shifted onto the right leg — yet the body is not yet moving. This *David* holds the instant of decision: the will fully committed, the act not yet begun.
- **Prompt:** The most concentrated image of human agency the Renaissance produced — coiled potential held in perfect suspension.


#### 2. **David** — Donatello · 1440  
*Inline work-plate · id: `donatello-david`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Power at rest
- **Analysis:** Notice the absence of effort, heroism, or drama. The boy simply stands, the giant's head beneath his heel, an expression of mild unconcern on his face. Cast in bronze — the material of ancient civic monuments and imperial statues — this claim of imperial dignity is made not for a general or a saint but for a slight, nude adolescent. It is a strange and disquieting image of power, and the first freestanding nude since antiquity.
- **Prompt:** The victory is already over. Donatello sculpts the eerie calm of the aftermath, not the heroics of the fight.


#### 3. **Pietà** — Michelangelo Buonarroti · 1499  
*Inline work-plate · id: `pieta`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The signed sash
- **Analysis:** Look at the Virgin's face — serenely, impossibly young (Michelangelo said purity does not age) — then at her lap, engineered wider than anatomy allows to hold the full weight of the adult body draped across it. Now find the strap crossing her robe: it carries Michelangelo's signature, the only work he ever signed. Christ's body conveys the specific weight of corporeal death with a tenderness that makes the marble seem warm.
- **Prompt:** The one sculpture Michelangelo ever signed — carved into the Virgin's sash, a genius asserting authorship in the heart of Rome.


### Music


#### 1. **Sicut Cervus** — Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina · c. 1580s · 4 min  
*Hero · id: `0yd5EE0hAB8`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Imitation, the signature of polyphony
- **Analysis:** Try to follow a single voice as it enters with the melody, then listen for how the other voices take up that same melody in turn — this technique is called *imitation*, and it is the signature sound of Renaissance polyphony. Each of the four voices enters separately, then overlaps and interweaves with the rest. Notice the overall balance and serenity: moments of tension exist, but they always resolve smoothly, and no single voice ever dominates.
- **Prompt:** This is the purest example of Renaissance polyphony — serene, luminous, and perfectly balanced, four equal voices held in equilibrium.


#### 2. **Spem in Alium** — Thomas Tallis · c. 1570 · Motet in 40 parts  
*Inline listening · id: `CkL1hdL40i0`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Polyphony at its monumental extreme
- **Analysis:** Tallis takes polyphony to its monumental extreme: forty *independent* voices build gradually from a single line into an overwhelming, luminous wall of sound. Listen to how it scales — from intimate entries in one corner of the choir, passed around the eight five-voice choirs, to the full forty singing at once. The sheer density shows how polyphony can grow from an intimate conversation into something approaching the sublime, without any single line ever being lost.
- **Prompt:** Forty separate melodic lines, not one of them a mere accompaniment — the Renaissance ideal of independent, equal voices carried as far as it can possibly go.


#### 3. **Ave Maria…virgo serena** — Josquin des Prez · c. 1485 · Motet  
*Inline listening · id: `s-pVbpV4yuk`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Imitation as structure
- **Analysis:** Listen for the voices entering one at a time in clear imitation, each taking up the same melodic idea, then shifting between different combinations — duets, trios, the full choir. The piece is built almost entirely from this disciplined imitative procedure: a structure of strict rules that nonetheless yields enormous variety and expressive grace, much as a sonnet's fixed form becomes a source of rhetorical power rather than a constraint on it.
- **Prompt:** The rules of counterpoint are not a cage here — Josquin's ingenuity lives entirely within them, and is inseparable from them.

## Unit 02 — The Baroque


### Painting


#### 1. **The Supper at Emmaus** — Caravaggio  
*Hero · id: `hero-emmaus`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The beam of recognition
- **Analysis:** Notice where the light comes from and how much of the canvas is swallowed by shadow. A single dramatic beam illuminates the instant of recognition — Christ blessing the bread — while the background recedes into near-total darkness. The disciples are caught mid-motion, one flinging his arms wide, the other about to push back his chair, so the miracle erupts into the viewer's own space.
- **Prompt:** Find the light source, then everything it chooses to leave in darkness. That selection is the drama.


#### 2. **Las Meninas** — Diego Velázquez · 1656  
*Inline work-plate · id: `las-meninas`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Where do you stand?
- **Analysis:** Velázquez builds a real but unmeasurable room and then makes your own position the puzzle. He stands at a huge canvas looking out at us, and a mirror on the back wall reflects the king and queen — who occupy the very spot where we are standing. Figures turn and glance in different directions across a space that dissolves upward into shadow, so the composition never resolves into a single, stable vantage.
- **Prompt:** Ask who is being painted, and where you are standing. The picture refuses to answer.


#### 3. **The Calling of Saint Matthew** — Caravaggio · 1600  
*Inline work-plate · id: `calling-matthew`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · One shaft, one summons
- **Analysis:** A single shaft of light enters from the upper right and cuts across a dark, ordinary room to fall on Matthew at his counting table. The beam echoes the line of Christ's pointing hand, so that light and gesture together perform the summons. Everything outside that diagonal — most of the canvas — stays in shadow, and the sacred enters the mundane world with no softening glow.
- **Prompt:** Trace the beam back to its edge. Caravaggio tells you exactly what to see and buries the rest.


#### 4. **Self-Portrait** — Rembrandt van Rijn · 1659  
*Inline work-plate · id: `rembrandt-self`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Paint you can feel
- **Analysis:** Look closely at the highlighted flesh — the forehead, the nose, the collar — where the paint is thick and sculptural, built up in ridges that catch actual light on the canvas surface. The shadows, by contrast, are painted thinly and transparently. The aging face is constructed from visible material, so you see both a man and the physical stuff of his making at once.
- **Prompt:** Move your eye from the lit ridges to the thin shadows. The paint stops hiding and becomes the subject.


### Sculpture


#### 1. **Apollo and Daphne** — Gian Lorenzo Bernini  
*Hero · id: `hero-daphne`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Stone caught mid-transformation
- **Analysis:** Begin with the surfaces: Bernini has carved bark growing up Daphne's thighs, her toes rooting into the stone below her feet, her fingers branching into actual leaves — all from a single block of marble. The composition pulls your eye upward and outward in a spiral: Apollo's reaching arm, Daphne's recoiling torso, the explosion of leaves at the top. There is no stable front; the group was designed to be walked around, offering a different dramatic moment from every angle.
- **Prompt:** The fixed stone seems to *contain motion* — you are watching a body become a tree at the exact instant it happens.


#### 2. **David** — Gian Lorenzo Bernini · 1624  
*Inline work-plate · id: `bernini-david`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The action shot
- **Analysis:** Find the face: it is Bernini's own, according to his biographer, and it conveys something very different from the serene confidence of Michelangelo's version. This David bites his lip, his brow furrowed, his whole body torqued into a spiral committed to an action he cannot retract — the instant before the sling releases. The figure's twist throws his energy out into the viewer's space; you instinctively step back from where the stone he is about to fling would fly.
- **Prompt:** Donatello sculpts the aftermath, Michelangelo the decision — Bernini sculpts the violent middle of the act itself.


#### 3. **Milo of Croton** — Pierre Puget · 1682  
*Inline work-plate · id: `puget-milo`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Virtuosity as anguish
- **Analysis:** The Greek athlete who tried to split an oak with his bare hands was trapped when the tree closed around his fingers, then devoured by a wolf. Puget shows the instant of maximum muscular effort that is also maximum helplessness. The body is anatomically hyper-specific — every tendon and straining muscle described — and it is precisely that virtuosic marble specificity that makes the helplessness devastating. The polished stone renders both the strength and its total futility.
- **Prompt:** The same marble mastery that makes Bernini's Daphne miraculous makes Puget's Milo unbearable — technique in the service of overwhelming force.


#### 4. **The Ecstasy of Saint Teresa** — Gian Lorenzo Bernini · 1652  
*Inline work-plate · id: `ecstasy-teresa`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · A chapel as theater
- **Analysis:** Look for the hidden window above and behind the figures — the source of the real light that falls on the gilded rays. Then look at the Cornaro family portraits in their theater boxes on the side walls: Bernini has made us members of the audience at Teresa's vision. Teresa and the angel float on a cloud of marble, the saint's body slack and her drapery churning, so that the whole chapel — architecture, sculpture, light, spectators — becomes a single unified environment rather than an object on a pedestal.
- **Prompt:** You are not looking at a statue in a room; you have been seated inside the drama, in the same theater as the carved marble audience.


### Music


#### 1. **Toccata and Fugue in D Minor, BWV 565** — Johann Sebastian Bach · c. 1704 · first 3–4 min  
*Hero · id: `HL0drraRHJ0`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Freedom, then rigor
- **Analysis:** Notice the dramatic shift between the two sections. The famous opening is the toccata — free, improvisatory, almost wild, pure expressive freedom. Then the fugue begins with a single, simple theme stated alone; listen for that theme as it is picked up by additional voices, one at a time, each entry adding another layer of complexity. The contrast between the toccata's freedom and the fugue's intellectual rigor is the Baroque's central tension in miniature.
- **Prompt:** One piece, two temperaments — improvisatory abandon giving way to strict counterpoint. The whole Baroque, compressed into a few minutes.


#### 2. **The Four Seasons: “Winter,” first movement** — Antonio Vivaldi · 1725 · Solo violin concerto  
*Inline listening · id: `tJAQI7PVofQ`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · The concerto principle
- **Analysis:** The concerto principle is vivid here: a solo violin set against the full string orchestra, creating dialogue, contrast, and drama. Listen for the alternation between the soloist and the ensemble — the individual voice stepping forward, then rejoining or being answered by the collective. This tension between one and many is the Baroque's defining textural innovation, and the direct ancestor of the operatic aria.
- **Prompt:** Follow the solo violin as it separates from and returns to the orchestra — the individual against the collective, made audible.


#### 3. **Messiah: “Hallelujah Chorus”** — George Frideric Handel · 1741 · Oratorio chorus  
*Inline listening · id: `usfiAsWR4qU`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Dramatic contrast
- **Analysis:** Notice how the texture shifts constantly between moments when all voices sing the same rhythm together (homophony) and moments when they overlap in polyphonic imitation. The dramatic contrasts — the full choir blazing, then suddenly thinning to a few voices — are prototypically Baroque: the sonic equivalent of chiaroscuro, brilliant light set against sudden shadow, jubilation staged as sheer opposition of forces.
- **Prompt:** Baroque drama lives in the juxtapositions — loud against soft, full against sparse, all voices against one.


#### 4. **Dido and Aeneas: “Dido's Lament”** — Henry Purcell · 1689 · Ground-bass aria  
*Inline listening · id: `uGQq3HcOB0Y`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · The ground bass
- **Analysis:** Listen for the ground bass: a short, chromatically descending line that repeats eleven times beneath Dido's vocal melody, unwavering as fate. The descending line is word painting for death, and the harmonic dissonances it creates — as Dido's vocal line chafes against the inexorable bass — produce an ache that has moved audiences for three hundred years. This is the passacaglia principle in action: a fixed, repeating foundation beneath freely varying human feeling, the Baroque's emotional extremity at its most concentrated.
- **Prompt:** A fixed bass sustaining the chaos of mortal variation — Milton's cosmology in sonic form: God's order persists, unaltered, beneath the Fall.

## Unit 03 — The Enlightenment


### Painting


#### 1. **The Swing** — Jean-Honoré Fragonard  
*Hero · id: `hero-swing`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Pleasure as composition
- **Analysis:** Notice the pastel palette — pinks, greens, creamy whites — and the soft, flattering light that bathes the whole scene. The composition is playful and asymmetrical, organized around a flirtatious dynamic among three figures: a young woman on a swing, the admirer positioned to look up at her, and the older man working the ropes behind. A slipper flies off mid-air. The fabrics shimmer with exquisitely rendered texture, and the sacred stakes of the Baroque have shrunk to the management of desire within a social game.
- **Prompt:** The whole cosmos of the Baroque has contracted to a garden of flirtation. Notice how completely — almost comically — the stakes have shrunk.


#### 2. **The Pilgrimage to Cythera** — Jean-Antoine Watteau · 1717  
*Inline work-plate · id: `pilgrimage`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · A social tableau in motion
- **Analysis:** Read the painting as a sequence of couples strung across an idealized garden, staged like figures at a country assembly. The composition curves gently from the lovers still seated at right to those already drifting toward the boat at left. Everyone is defined by their pairing and their place in the social choreography — but look closely and the mood is wistful: they are *leaving* the island of love, not arriving, and that undertow of melancholy beneath the charm is precisely what anticipates Austen's irony.
- **Prompt:** Follow the line of couples from right to left. It is a social scene choreographed as carefully as a ball.


#### 3. **Young Girl Reading** — Jean-Honoré Fragonard · 1770  
*Inline work-plate · id: `young-girl-reading`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Light that flatters
- **Analysis:** Soft, even light falls across the girl, her lemon-yellow dress, and the cushion behind her, with no dramatic beam and no plunging shadow. Illumination here is not theological but decorative — it makes silk, skin, and ribbon uniformly lovely. The quiet, self-possessed absorption of a young woman reading in private is exactly the kind of ordinary social moment the Rococo, and Austen, take as their true subject.
- **Prompt:** No beam, no darkness — just flattering, even light. Notice how the mood becomes intimate rather than cosmic.


#### 4. **The Secret Message** — François Boucher · 1767  
*Inline work-plate · id: `secret-message`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Effortless artifice
- **Analysis:** Look at how the paint renders silk, lace, foliage, and skin — fluid, light, decorative, with no visible struggle in the brushwork. An intimate domestic moment (a whispered message between two women in a garden) is treated with the same jewel-like refinement Boucher gave to mythological scenes. The effect of naturalness is achieved through extraordinary artifice; the ease is the performance.
- **Prompt:** The touch looks effortless — which is precisely the sophisticated illusion the Rococo surface is built to create.


### Sculpture


#### 1. **Voltaire Seated** — Jean-Antoine Houdon  
*Hero · id: `hero-voltaire`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · A real man in an ideal's costume
- **Analysis:** Begin with the face: the eyes are sharp, slightly narrowed, hovering between amusement and challenge — the sardonic intelligence that had discomfited kings and bishops for sixty years. The body is draped in a Roman toga, the period's conventional signal of civic virtue, but the specificity of the aging face — the loose skin at the throat, the forward inclination of a mind perpetually engaged — refuses the timelessness the classical drapery implies. This is a real man, with a real history, wearing the costume of an ideal.
- **Prompt:** The tension between the universal and the particular is the work's central drama — accuracy in the service of revealing the mind within.


#### 2. **Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss** — Antonio Canova · 1793  
*Inline work-plate · id: `canova-psyche`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Idealization made total
- **Analysis:** Look at how little the two bodies betray of individual identity — these are not portraits but perfected types, the human form lifted toward an ideal. The composition is built on a gentle diagonal, Cupid's wings and arms forming a frame that draws Psyche's reviving body up into his kiss. The marble is polished until it seems to become skin; the drama of the Baroque has quieted into something restrained, tender, and self-contained. Compared to Bernini's writhing Daphne, this is emotion held under perfect rational control.
- **Prompt:** Where Houdon fixes a specific mind in time, Canova lifts the body out of time entirely — the universal over the particular.


### Music


#### 1. **Eine kleine Nachtmusik, K. 525 — first movement** — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · 1787 · 6 min  
*Hero · id: `UhPBT0dA_oA`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Textbook sonata form
- **Analysis:** Listen for the bright, confident opening theme, then the shift to a more lyrical second theme — these two contrasting ideas are the *exposition*. The development section fragments and recombines them, creating harmonic instability, before the recapitulation brings back the opening material in a satisfying return. The balanced, symmetrical phrases — musical sentences that come in even, proportional groups — are the sonic equivalent of Austen's precisely balanced prose.
- **Prompt:** Statement, development, resolution — the same formal control that carries an Austen novel from introduction through complication to its surprising-yet-inevitable close.


#### 2. **Piano Concerto No. 21 in C, K. 467 — Andante** — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · 1785 · Solo piano & orchestra  
*Inline listening · id: `5Y4Fkxg7WcA`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · One voice, gently answered
- **Analysis:** The second movement shows how Classical music handles deep emotion within restrained formal structures: the melody is achingly beautiful but never excessive, feeling shaped by form. Notice, too, how the piano and orchestra move between collaboration and gentle contest — a single clear voice heard above and alongside the collective. This is the concerto as a sonic image of the individual in society.
- **Prompt:** The soloist's line stays clearly audible above its accompaniment — Elizabeth Bennet's consciousness, with the other characters arranged around her.


#### 3. **Overture to Don Giovanni, K. 527** — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · 1787 · Opera overture  
*Inline listening · id: `G1YS61pu8Rc`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Dissonance deferred, then resolved
- **Analysis:** Listen for the dramatic slow introduction — dark, foreboding, harmonically unstable — before the bright sonata-form movement that follows. The two are in violent contrast: tragedy and comedy occupying the same six minutes. That opening dissonance is not resolved here; the introduction returns, note for note, at the opera's finale, when the ghost of the murdered Commendatore arrives to drag Don Giovanni to hell. Mozart embeds the whole moral reckoning inside the overture.
- **Prompt:** Tension introduced at the start, held across the entire evening, and resolved only at the very end — dissonance in the service of a larger architecture.


#### 4. **Symphony No. 94 (“Surprise”) — Andante** — Joseph Haydn · 1791 · Symphony movement  
*Inline listening · id: `lLjwkamp3lI`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · A joke about expectation
- **Analysis:** This is Classical wit at its most concentrated: a gentle, predictable melody, phrased in tidy symmetrical units — and then, without warning, a single sudden fortissimo chord. The joke only works because the form has trained you to expect the opposite. Haydn plays with the listener's expectations within the very framework of cadence and phrase that gives the movement its shape.
- **Prompt:** The surprise depends on the rules — you have to know what should happen for the disruption to land. Austen's deceptive cadences work exactly the same way.

## Unit 04 — Romanticism


### Painting


#### 1. **Wanderer above the Sea of Fog** — Caspar David Friedrich  
*Hero · id: `hero-wanderer`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Dwarfed by immensity
- **Analysis:** Notice the scale relationship: the human figure is solid and present but dwarfed by the mist, rock, and sky that surround him. His back is turned to us — we see what he sees, but we also see him seeing it. The gap between his smallness and nature's immensity is the painting's subject. Nature is no longer a garden or a backdrop; it is the vast, ungovernable primary reality against which a solitary human consciousness measures itself.
- **Prompt:** His back is turned so that we see with him, not at him — and the immensity he faces refuses to be measured or mastered. This is the sublime.


#### 2. **The Raft of the Medusa** — Théodore Géricault · 1819  
*Inline work-plate · id: `raft-medusa`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Bodies against an indifferent sea
- **Analysis:** Read the composition as two surging diagonals: a pyramid of straining bodies climbs from the dead and dying in the foreground toward a single figure waving a scrap of cloth at a barely-visible ship on the horizon. The survivors are piled, reaching, and collapsing on a makeshift raft against an indifferent ocean that dwarfs them. Hope and despair are built into the geometry itself — the desperate upward push against the vast, uncaring sea.
- **Prompt:** Follow the pyramid of bodies up to the waving figure — then find the tiny ship on the horizon. The sea holds all the power.


#### 3. **The Slave Ship** — J.M.W. Turner · 1840  
*Inline work-plate · id: `slave-ship`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Where sky and sea dissolve
- **Analysis:** A blazing, blood-red sunset consumes most of the canvas, and solid forms dissolve into swirling color until the boundary between sea and sky nearly vanishes. The ship tilts in a churning storm while the horrific human cargo — the drowning enslaved people thrown overboard — is rendered almost illegibly amid the turbulence, half-swallowed by the elemental fury. Turner makes light and paint themselves carry a moral catastrophe that exceeds what any single legible figure could convey.
- **Prompt:** Try to find the horizon line. It has dissolved — sea, sky, and atrocity have become a single field of turbulent light.


#### 4. **Liberty Leading the People** — Eugène Delacroix · 1830  
*Inline work-plate · id: `liberty`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Energy as subject
- **Analysis:** Look at the brushwork in the smoke, the flag, and the tumbled bodies at the base of the composition — bold, rapid, and visibly energetic, conveying the chaos and passion of revolution rather than describing it neatly. Delacroix applies Romantic surface energy to a political subject: the allegorical figure of Liberty surges forward over the barricade, and the paint surges with her. The handling itself carries the urgency.
- **Prompt:** The brushwork doesn't describe the revolution from a safe distance — it enacts its turbulence. Surface energy *is* the meaning.


### Sculpture


#### 1. **The Burghers of Calais** — Auguste Rodin  
*Hero · id: `hero-burghers`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Alone, together
- **Analysis:** Find the hands first — among the most expressive elements in the group. One figure's hands hang with extraordinary heaviness, fingers slightly open, as if the decision to hold anything has been abandoned; another grips the oversized key to the city with a mix of purpose and dread. Then look at the faces: each is different, each private, none turned toward the others. These six men who offered their lives to spare their city are together in their ordeal and alone in it at the same time. Rodin gives them no heroic bodies — they are heavy, tentative, burdened, courage and grief occupying the same figure at once.
- **Prompt:** Designed to stand at ground level among ordinary people, not on a plinth — grief that is human rather than monumental, its power dependent on proximity.


#### 2. **The Gates of Hell** — Auguste Rodin · 1880–1917  
*Inline work-plate · id: `gates-hell`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Bodies returning to the ground
- **Analysis:** Find the *Thinker* at the top — Rodin's original figure is not pondering philosophically but physically straining, every muscle engaged, as if thought itself were an act of violent effort. Then look to the edges of the doors, where figures begin to dissolve into the bronze ground, bodies losing their definition as they return to the undifferentiated material from which they emerged. Worked and reworked for thirty-seven years and never finished, the gates make their own incompletion the point — the boundary between form and raw material deliberately refused.
- **Prompt:** The finished form and the raw material bleed into each other — the struggle of making is left visible as the subject.


#### 3. **La Marseillaise — Departure of the Volunteers of 1792** — François Rude · 1836  
*Inline work-plate · id: `marseillaise`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Purpose below, passion above
- **Analysis:** Find the contrast between the figures at the bottom of the relief — warriors preparing for battle, their bodies in active, purposeful motion — and the central winged figure of Liberty above them, whose open mouth and forward surge communicate something beyond purpose: pure passionate force. Carved in high relief on the Arc de Triomphe, high above the street, this is public sculpture at its most monumental, inseparable from the civic architecture it crowns. Its meaning is collective and nationalist, the Romantic body in its exhilarated rather than anguished mode.
- **Prompt:** Where Rodin drops his figures to ground level, Rude lifts revolutionary energy high onto the city's symbolic architecture.


### Music


#### 1. **Symphony No. 5 in C Minor, Op. 67 — first movement** — Ludwig van Beethoven · 1808 · 7 min  
*Hero · id: `7eOaIiHB58U`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Four notes, pursued obsessively
- **Analysis:** The famous four-note opening (da-da-da-DUM) is not just a catchy phrase; it is the raw material from which the entire movement is built. Listen for the way those four notes are fragmented, transposed, extended, and combined as the movement unfolds. Classical sonata form is still the skeleton, but the flesh is Romantic intensity — a single idea pursued with obsessive, relentless developmental energy that pushes the inherited form to its limits.
- **Prompt:** A single motif driving an entire movement — the sound of form being stretched by emotional force. This is Classical structure becoming Romantic argument.


#### 2. **La Mer: “From Dawn to Noon on the Sea”** — Claude Debussy · 1905 · Orchestral movement  
*Inline listening · id: `IoENgt1h4_A`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · The orchestra as a single instrument
- **Analysis:** Debussy's *La Mer* sits at the boundary between Romanticism and Modernism: the vastly expanded orchestra creates shifting, shimmering textures that evoke the sea not through representation but through a kind of sonic impressionism. Listen for how no single melodic line dominates — instead, layers of orchestral color merge and dissolve into one immersive field of sound. The connection to Melville's ocean is almost too perfect; this is the sound of "meditation and water" wedded together.
- **Prompt:** Not a tune with accompaniment but a whole shimmering surface — the Romantic orchestra become one massive, oceanic instrument.


#### 3. **Prelude to Tristan und Isolde** — Richard Wagner · 1859 · Opera prelude  
*Inline listening · id: `IaZZVRd_WeU`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Yearning that never resolves
- **Analysis:** Listen for the sense of unresolved yearning — the music constantly reaching toward a resolution that only arrives hours later in the opera. The harmonies are richer and more ambiguous than anything in the Classical period, and the emotional tension builds without release. Every phrase seems about to settle, then slips away instead. This is dissonance pushed past the point where the inherited rules can contain it.
- **Prompt:** Wait for the chord to resolve. It won't — that withholding, sustained across an entire opera, is the whole point.


#### 4. **Der Erlkönig** — Franz Schubert · 1815 · Lied for voice & piano  
*Inline listening · id: `XoBo8dlPcQo`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · One figure, four voices
- **Analysis:** Listen for the way a single continuous, galloping, relentless piano figure conveys the experience of four characters at once: narrator, father, child, and the supernatural Elf King, whose seductive murmuring contrasts with the father's desperate reassurances and the child's mounting terror. A single sung line and a single accompanying figure carry an entire terrifying drama — the unbroken piano ostinato is the small-scale idée fixe, the relentless idea that cannot be abandoned.
- **Prompt:** One galloping figure, never pausing, holding four voices and a whole night's dread together — coherence through relentless iteration.

## Unit 05 — Modernism


### Painting


#### 1. **Les Demoiselles d'Avignon** — Pablo Picasso  
*Hero · id: `hero-demoiselles`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Seen from everywhere at once
- **Analysis:** Notice how the five figures are shown from multiple angles simultaneously — frontal and profile views coexist on the same face, spatial planes shift abruptly, and background and foreground compress into a single fractured surface. The two faces at right borrow the sharp planes of African masks. This is not a window onto a world; it is a flat surface asserting its own logic — the opening move of the Cubist revolution.
- **Prompt:** Look for a stable viewpoint or a coherent floor. There is none — you are seeing from everywhere and nowhere at once.


#### 2. **A Bar at the Folies-Bergère** — Édouard Manet · 1882  
*Inline work-plate · id: `manet-bar`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · A space that won't resolve
- **Analysis:** Start with the barmaid facing us, then look at her reflection in the mirror behind her — and notice it doesn't line up. The reflected back of her figure is shifted far to the right, where a top-hatted customer faces her, though no such customer stands before us. Manet flattens the modeling and lets the spatial logic contradict itself, so the coherent, single-viewpoint space of the Renaissance quietly comes apart. The picture refuses to resolve into one consistent world.
- **Prompt:** Line up the barmaid with her reflection. You can't — and that impossibility is the painting's real subject.


#### 3. **Composition VII** — Wassily Kandinsky · 1913  
*Inline work-plate · id: `kandinsky`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Light without a world
- **Analysis:** There is no recognizable subject here at all — what you see is color, line, and energy operating without reference to the visible world. Luminosity is not borrowed from any depicted sun or window; it is produced directly by the collision of hues on the canvas. Kandinsky treats color the way a composer treats sound, so the 'light' of the painting is generated by the paint itself rather than illustrating the lighting of a three-dimensional scene.
- **Prompt:** Stop hunting for objects. The glow here is made by color against color — light with no world behind it.


#### 4. **The Dance** — Henri Matisse · 1910  
*Inline work-plate · id: `matisse-dance`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · The figure reduced to rhythm
- **Analysis:** Five figures circle in a ring against nothing but a band of blue and a band of green — the human body reduced to its most elemental: flat, outlined, rhythmic. There is no modeling, no measurable space, no anatomical detail beyond what the movement requires. Pattern and rhythm matter more than accuracy, and the flat planes of pure color announce the canvas as a surface rather than a window. The painting is about the pulse of the dance, carried entirely by shape and color.
- **Prompt:** No floor, no depth, no shading — only flat color and the rhythm of the ring. The surface has become the subject.


### Sculpture


#### 1. **Bird in Space** — Constantin Brancusi  
*Hero · id: `hero-bird`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · An idea of flight
- **Analysis:** Resist the instinct to look for the bird. Instead, follow the form upward: the base widens into a compressed torso shape, then tapers into a long ascending shaft, then ends in a fine point. Brancusi arrived here through years of progressive reduction — stripping away wings, feathers, beak, every recognizable feature — until only the essential gesture of motion through air remained. The polished bronze reflects the room around it, so you cannot quite locate where the sculpture ends and the reflections begin; the boundary between the work and its environment is deliberately unstable.
- **Prompt:** In 1926 a US customs official refused to classify this as art, taxing it as industrial metal. Brancusi sued — and a federal court had to decide, on the record, what counts as sculpture.


#### 2. **City Square** — Alberto Giacometti · 1948  
*Inline work-plate · id: `giacometti-square`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Present but barely there
- **Analysis:** Notice the scale: the figures are small enough to observe from above, which immediately makes you a kind of god or surveillance camera looking down on a city scene. Their elongation is most visible from the side — thinner than any human body could be, as if the sculptor has subtracted from them until only the gesture of being upright and moving remains. Five figures cross a flat bronze base that could be the pavement of any city, near one another yet connecting with none. The work does not represent urban isolation; it produces it.
- **Prompt:** This is Rodin's ground-level gesture pushed to its limit — the street itself made into sculpture, and you another figure moving through it, equally unnoticed.


#### 3. **Guitar** — Pablo Picasso · 1912  
*Inline work-plate · id: `picasso-guitar`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Built, not carved
- **Analysis:** Resist looking at it as a guitar. Instead, notice the construction: flat planes of sheet metal bent and joined at angles, the wire strings, the visible seams and edges. Nothing is hidden or finished smooth. The sound-hole, which on a real guitar is a void, projects *outward* as a cylinder — the negative made positive. This is the first major Western sculpture built rather than carved or cast, a three-dimensional Cubist object that shows exactly how it is put together. It is a guitar the way a Cubist portrait is a face.
- **Prompt:** The cheap industrial material and visible seams are the point — everything a marble surface was designed to suppress.


#### 4. **The Large Glass — The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even** — Marcel Duchamp · 1915–1923  
*Inline work-plate · id: `duchamp-glass`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Look through it, not just at it
- **Analysis:** Look *through* the work as well as at it — it was designed so that whatever appears behind the glass becomes part of the composition. Made on two panes with paint, varnish, lead wire, and dust, it is at once painting, sculpture, and architectural element. Then find the cracks: the glass shattered in transport in 1926, and Duchamp declared the damage an improvement, an accidental contribution no intentional craft could produce. Where his earlier *Fountain* asked whether the artist's hand was necessary, the *Large Glass* asks whether intention itself is the limit of meaning.
- **Prompt:** Chance — a crack in transit — is accepted as a collaborator. Intention is no longer the boundary of the work.


#### 5. **Hudson River Landscape** — David Smith · 1951  
*Inline work-plate · id: `smith-hudson`*

- **Eyebrow:** Close looking · Drawing in three dimensions
- **Analysis:** Find the gestural lines of welded steel that trace the horizon and the riverbank — this is drawing in three dimensions, a landscape you can walk around. Smith made it by welding together lines drawn from sketches he took on a train journey along the Hudson, so the finished work is simultaneously a drawing, a landscape, and a sculptural object, occupying a category of its own creation. A factory worker's skills — cutting, grinding, welding — are brought directly into art, the industrial labor high culture preferred to keep out of the gallery made fully visible.
- **Prompt:** Neither quite drawing nor quite sculpture nor quite landscape — a work that invents the category it belongs to.


### Music


#### 1. **The Rite of Spring: “Augurs of Spring”** — Igor Stravinsky · 1913 · from ~3:00  
*Hero · id: `dcwB67IoefA`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Rhythm as rupture
- **Analysis:** Listen for the famous pounding chords and notice how their accents fall in irregular, unpredictable places. This is asymmetrical rhythm as a structural principle: the beat groupings shift constantly, so the music never settles into a pattern the body can anticipate. The chords are built from simple triads, but stacked and accented until they feel violent and disorienting. Compare this to the balanced, symmetrical phrases of Mozart and the rupture is immediately audible; its 1913 premiere caused a riot.
- **Prompt:** Try to tap your foot to a steady beat — the music refuses to let you. Rhythm itself has become the disruptive subject.


#### 2. **Black, Brown and Beige: “Black”** — Duke Ellington · 1943 · first 5–6 min  
*Hero · id: `8HZ4jiiOQmc`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · The jazz ensemble
- **Analysis:** Listen for how jazz texture works at the level of the ensemble: individual soloists emerge from the collective, improvise, and return. The relationship between soloist and ensemble is fluid and democratic in a way that neither Classical homophony nor Baroque counterpoint achieves. The connection to Ellison's narrative method is direct — the narrator's voice riffs, improvises, circles back, and breaks into different registers just as a jazz soloist does. It is this texture, more than any European formal innovation, that Ellison drew on as the model for his narrator's voice.
- **Prompt:** Follow one soloist as it steps out of the ensemble and folds back in. That fluid exchange is the sound of Invisible Man's voice.


#### 3. **The Rite of Spring: Introduction to Part I** — Igor Stravinsky · 1913 · Orchestral introduction  
*Inline listening · id: `02tkp6eeh40`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Voices that never merge
- **Analysis:** Listen for what does *not* happen: there is no melody in the conventional sense, no harmonic forward motion, no sense of the music going somewhere. Instead, follow individual voices as they enter — the opening bassoon, then the other woodwinds joining one by one — and notice how each repeats its own pattern without resolving into anything larger. Debussy, who heard the piece before its premiere, called it "an extraordinarily savage affair, primitive with every modern convenience." The primitivism is the point, and the sophistication of the means is exactly what makes it so unsettling.
- **Prompt:** Independent, repeated voices layering without ever merging — texture as accumulation, like organisms emerging one by one from the earth.


#### 4. **Pierrot Lunaire, No. 8: “Nacht” (Night)** — Arnold Schoenberg · 1912 · Chamber song  
*Inline listening · id: `1gafF5sbnB0`*

- **Eyebrow:** Listen for · Floating in darkness
- **Analysis:** Listen for the vocalist's *Sprechstimme* — a technique suspended between speaking and singing — which makes language itself sound fragmented and uncanny. There is no tonal center, no resolution: the small, strange ensemble (here, a low ostinato figure) circles without ever landing. The feeling is of floating in darkness — the sonic equivalent of the narrator's underground room before the lights come on.
- **Prompt:** No home key to return to, no chord that settles — the sound of an identity with no fixed center, in a world that refuses to see it clearly.
