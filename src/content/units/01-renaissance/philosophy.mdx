# Philosophical Context: Renaissance and Reformation

## Introduction

Renaissance thought developed through competing movements. Italian humanists recovered and edited classical texts; Christian philosophers joined ancient sources to theology; reformers contested the authority of the Church; mathematicians and natural philosophers challenged inherited accounts of the heavens. Each movement changed the relation between inherited authority and individual judgment.

*Hamlet* registers the resulting pressure. The play's characters inhabit Christian, humanist, courtly, and political frameworks that offer incompatible instructions. Thought becomes difficult because several systems remain plausible at once.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do human beings stand in the order of things?*

<BioLink id="petrarch">Petrarch</BioLink>'s *Secretum* stages a dialogue between two versions of the self. "Franciscus" defends his love of Laura, desire for literary fame, and attachment to worldly achievement; "Augustine" exposes those attachments as obstacles to spiritual freedom. Petrarch accepts much of Augustine's diagnosis without completing the conversion Augustine demands. The dialogue ends with its conflict unresolved. A Christian moral framework remains authoritative, but recognition no longer guarantees surrender.

Petrarch's divided self introduces a central Renaissance problem: knowledge of the good may intensify self-consciousness without producing action. Humanist education can diagnose the will, give it historical examples, and refine its language while leaving it divided.

<BioLink id="pico">Pico della Mirandola</BioLink> gives human freedom a cosmic form in the *Oration on the Dignity of Man* (1486). Pico retains the hierarchy that later scholars call the **Great Chain of Being**, extending from material existence toward divine intelligence. What changes is humanity's relation to that scale. God assigns the human creature no single fixed place and grants the freedom to descend toward merely material life or ascend through reason and contemplation.

<PullQuote unit="01-renaissance" attribution="Giovanni Pico della Mirandola · Oration on the Dignity of Man · 1486">
  We have given you, O Adam, no visage proper to yourself, nor endowment properly your own, in order that whatever place, whatever form, whatever gifts you may, with premeditation, select, these same you may have and possess through your own judgment and decision.
</PullQuote>

Pico transforms the hierarchy rather than destroying it. The medieval scale assigns; Pico turns the same scale into a path of moral and spiritual movement. His project remains mystical and Christian. He studied Plato, Kabbalah, Islamic philosophy, and other traditions because he believed that apparently different schools preserved fragments of one divine truth.

<BioLink id="castiglione">Baldassare Castiglione</BioLink>'s *Book of the Courtier* (1528) moves self-formation into social life. The dialogue's speakers define the ideal courtier through education, physical skill, conversation, artistic judgment, and the ability to advise a ruler. Castiglione calls the art of concealing effort **sprezzatura**: disciplined performance should appear natural and unforced. The courtier creates a persuasive public self, but the performance remains dependent on rank, audience, and power.

### Connection to *Hamlet*

Hamlet's "What a piece of work is a man" speech moves along Pico's scale from reason, angelic apprehension, and godlike capacity to the "quintessence of dust." The speech shares Pico's conceptual range while reversing his confidence in ascent. Hamlet recognizes capacities that should make human beings admirable and experiences those capacities as empty.

The play also tests Castiglione's world of performed identity. Claudius presents murder as orderly succession; Polonius performs wisdom through maxims; Osric turns courtly refinement into parody; Hamlet adopts an "antic disposition" and attempts to control how others read him. Performance can reveal truth, as in the play-within-the-play, or become the method by which power conceals it.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do human beings know what they know, and how far can knowledge take them?*

Renaissance inquiry developed from medieval practices of argument, translation, mathematical reasoning, and observation. Humanists changed those practices through **philology**, the close study of language, manuscripts, and historical context. Comparing copies could expose corruption in an inherited text; recovering Greek could challenge conclusions based on Latin translation; printing could distribute a correction or controversy across Europe.

<div class="plate-pair right vcenter">
<div class="pair-text">

<BioLink id="copernicus">Copernicus</BioLink> worked within ancient astronomy while reorganizing it. His heliocentric model joined mathematical calculation to dissatisfaction with the complexity of existing models. The earth became a moving planet, although the physical explanation of that motion remained incomplete.

<BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink> combined mathematical argument with instruments and observation. The telescope showed an irregular lunar surface, phases of Venus, and moons orbiting Jupiter. These findings challenged the division between a corruptible earth and perfect, unchanging heavens. His rebuke to the Aristotelians captures the change in evidentiary priority: "come either with arguments or demonstrations, and bring us no more texts and authorities, for our disputes are about the sensible world, and not one of paper."

<BioLink id="descartes">Descartes</BioLink>'s *Discourse on the Method* (1637) extends the challenge inward. He subjects received opinion and sensory experience to methodical doubt until the activity of doubting supplies one secure proposition: *cogito ergo sum*. The method seeks certainty, but it reaches that foundation by suspending confidence in almost everything else.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern social and spiritual life?*

At the Diet of Worms in 1521, <BioLink id="luther">Luther</BioLink> refused to retract teachings unless they could be disproved through Scripture or clear reason. The familiar "Here I stand" wording is probably a later addition, but the confrontation remains decisive. Luther's conscience claims obedience to a higher authority than pope or council. Scripture judges the institution that had claimed the right to interpret it.

Galileo's confrontation with the Inquisition presents a related conflict over natural knowledge. Institutional judgment compelled him to recant the earth's motion. The phrase *Eppur si muove*, "And yet it moves," belongs to later legend, but its survival expresses the argument that a verdict cannot alter the physical world it describes.

<BioLink id="machiavelli">Machiavelli</BioLink> supplies a less reassuring account of authority. Earlier political writing often described the virtues a ruler ought to possess. *The Prince* asks what actions actually preserve a state among people who do not consistently act well:

<PullQuote unit="01-renaissance" attribution="Niccolò Machiavelli · The Prince, chapter 15 · 1513">
  There is such a distance between how one lives and how one ought to live that anyone who abandons what is done for what ought to be done achieves his downfall rather than his preservation.
</PullQuote>

Machiavelli evaluates cruelty and deception through political necessity rather than conventional moral reputation. He contemplates situations when preserving a state may require actions usually condemned. Authority becomes a craft that demands judgment about circumstance, consequence, and appearance.

His image of **Fortuna** revises <BioLink id="boethius">Boethius</BioLink>'s Wheel of Fortune. Boethius treats Fortune's turning as evidence that worldly goods cannot provide stable happiness. Machiavelli compares Fortune to a flooding river. A ruler cannot prevent the flood, but prudent action during calm weather can build dikes and channels that limit its destruction. Boethius teaches detachment from Fortune's gifts; Machiavelli teaches preparation against Fortune's force.

### Connection to *Hamlet*

One reading of the opening court scene makes Claudius a disturbingly effective Machiavellian ruler. He manages grief, succession, diplomacy, and the threat from Norway in a single public performance. His competence intensifies the contradiction between political effectiveness and moral legitimacy.

The prayer scene places Machiavellian possession against Christian repentance. Claudius asks whether he can be pardoned while retaining "those effects for which I did the murder: / My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen." Hamlet, watching unseen, refuses to kill because he wants the act to satisfy a moral and theological standard. Claudius has acted effectively and cannot surrender the result; Hamlet insists on acting rightly and loses the opportunity to act.

The play complicates any opposition between heroic conscience and corrupt authority. Conscience can resist injustice, delay necessary action, or rationalize cruelty. Political skill can preserve public order while concealing private crime.

## Looking back: Inheritance and self-invention

Renaissance humanists helped construct the period now called the Middle Ages. Petrarch and later writers described an intervening age of decline between classical antiquity and their own hoped-for renewal. Leonardo Bruni gave this historical story an influential tripartite form.

That act of periodization served an argument. A culture claiming rebirth needed an earlier loss from which to recover. The caricature remains historically misleading, but belief in renewal had practical force: it directed the search for manuscripts, the study of ancient languages, and artistic attempts to equal the past.

Humanists redirected education toward language, history, moral philosophy, and civic life. Pico made inherited hierarchy a field of spiritual movement. Luther appealed from ecclesiastical office to Scripture. Copernicus and Galileo challenged particular cosmological authorities through mathematics and observation. Each transformation depended on methods, institutions, and questions inherited from earlier centuries.

## Looking forward: Reform and regulation

The same methods that expanded inquiry could also regulate it. Print spread Luther's attack and Catholic replies. Humanist rhetoric trained defenders of competing confessions. Schools and religious orders used classical education to strengthen institutional discipline. The Council of Trent renewed Catholic life while clarifying the boundaries of acceptable doctrine, worship, and representation.

The Baroque inherits that tension. Human freedom, sensory evidence, religious conviction, and political power have all grown more forceful, yet none can govern the others securely. <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>, writing in the next generation, will place the human creature between the infinitely large and the infinitely small: capable of thought, yet unable to master the universe thought reveals.
