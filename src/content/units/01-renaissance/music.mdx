## Introduction

Renaissance music is the first Western music that sounds recognizably modern to us: ordered, balanced, and governed by rules that produce a feeling of serene harmony. Where medieval music was predominantly **monophonic**, a single melodic line sung in unison, Renaissance composers made polyphony their central art, writing for four, five, and six independent voices.

Medieval plainchant existed to serve the liturgy and to suppress the body's claims in favor of spiritual discipline. Renaissance music keeps that sacred setting but adds a humanist ambition: music should move the listener, stir the emotions, and illuminate the meaning of words. Humanists found in ancient Greek writers the claim that music could shape a listener's character, and composers set out to find the technical means to do it.

## Texture & Voices

*How many voices are active, and how do they relate to each other?*

The defining texture of Renaissance music is **polyphony** — multiple independent melodic lines sounding simultaneously. In a four-voice **motet**, a sacred choral setting of a Latin text, each voice (soprano, alto, tenor, bass) has its own melody, and the composer fits these melodies together harmonically while keeping each one distinct. The singing is unaccompanied, which is where the term *a cappella*, "in the chapel style," comes from. The equal, distinct voices parallel Renaissance perspective painting, in which individual figures each hold their own place within one rationally ordered space.

But the texture has a cost, and the period knew it. Sing any one of <BioLink id="josquin">Josquin</BioLink>'s lines alone and every word is clear; sing six of them together and the words dissolve into one another. Renaissance composers held two convictions that threatened to contradict each other: that many independent voices are beautiful, and that the words must be clearly heard and understood. At the Council of Trent, convened to answer the Protestant Reformation, bishops complained that church polyphony buried its text, and in the years that followed, reformers pressed composers to make every word intelligible.

### Connection to *Hamlet*

The play inherits the Renaissance ideal of balanced, rational discourse: the humanist conviction that truth emerges from an exchange of perspectives. Hamlet himself is a master of dialogue, debate, and rhetorical self-examination. But the play strains against the polyphonic ideal. Hamlet speaks roughly 1,500 lines, the largest part Shakespeare wrote, and as the play goes on the other characters become instruments of his obsession or targets of his contempt. In the recorder scene (3.2) he turns that image on Guildenstern, who has been sent to sound him out: "You would play upon me," he says, and refuses to be played.

## Consonance & Dissonance

*How does the music handle tension and resolution?*

Renaissance composers treated dissonance with great care. It was permitted, even valued for its expressive potential, but only under strict conditions: it had to arrive smoothly, by step or held over from a consonant chord; it had to be brief; and it had to resolve promptly to a consonance.

Behind that control sits the period's real technical discovery. Medieval composers wrote independent lines and accepted whatever harmonies fell out where those lines crossed; the vertical result was often a by-product. Renaissance theorists, above all Gioseffo Zarlino in 1558, drew on ancient Greek ideas about number and proportion to explain the **triad** — the three-note chord that still underlies almost all Western music — and made harmony a governing science rather than an accident. Melody now has to accommodate itself to the chord. That single subordination is most of why <BioLink id="josquin">Josquin</BioLink> sounds modern to us and <BioLink id="machaut">Machaut</BioLink> sounds strange.

The control was not universal. Outside the church, in the **madrigal**, a secular part-song setting Italian or English poetry, composers pushed hard the other way. <BioLink id="gesualdo">Carlo Gesualdo</BioLink>, a prince answerable to neither patron nor paying audience, wrote chromatic progressions so unstable that composers did not approach them again until the nineteenth century. The rules described here are the rules of the sacred mainstream, not of the whole period.

### Connection to *Hamlet*

The play inherits a Renaissance ideal of rational order, but its content — madness, murder, existential doubt — pushes against that ideal in ways that anticipate the Baroque. Hamlet's dissonances (his antic disposition, his cruelty to Ophelia, his philosophical despair) are far more extreme and far less neatly resolved than the polyphonic rules would allow. The play is written in the formal language of Renaissance drama, blank verse and the inherited plot of revenge tragedy, but its emotional content strains that form almost to breaking. Its most disturbing music makes the point: in 4.5 Ophelia, mad, sings scraps of ballads before the king and queen, a lament for a dead man and a bawdy song about a lost maidenhead, and the scene never resolves them.

## Structure & Freedom

*How does the music relate to inherited formal structures?*

Renaissance music is governed by the strict rules of **counterpoint**, the craft of combining independent melodies, which dictate how those melodies can move in relation to each other. The rules codify what the ear of the period heard as harmonious. Composers found their variety inside these constraints: in how voices were grouped and regrouped, in which voice led an imitation, in where the music came to rest. The constraint works like a sonnet's fourteen lines, a limit that sharpens what can be said within it.

Within that discipline, Renaissance composers developed a practice called **word painting** — shaping the music to reflect and intensify the meaning of the text. A melody might descend on the word "falling" or rise on "heaven"; harmony could darken for grief.


### Connection to *Hamlet*

*Hamlet* is written mostly in **iambic pentameter**, a line of five iambs, each an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one, and <BioLink id="shakespeare">Shakespeare</BioLink> varies it constantly. "To be, or not to be, that is the question" runs to eleven syllables, ending on an unstressed beat that leaves the line hanging. He also steps outside the meter entirely: when Hamlet feigns madness with Polonius in 2.2 he speaks prose ("Words, words, words"), and he returns to verse when he is alone. The variations within the line match what Renaissance composers did within the rules of counterpoint. The shifts into prose go further, into a freedom the period's music found only outside the church, in the madrigal.

## Looking Back

Polyphony was not invented here. Composers at Notre-Dame in Paris were stacking voices above chant by 1200, and <BioLink id="machaut">Machaut</BioLink> built formidably complex motets in the fourteenth century. What changes in the Renaissance is that polyphony becomes the ordinary condition of music and that its voices become equal. Where "Ave Maris Stella" offered a single line, and Machaut set a fragment of chant in long notes in his lowest voice as a scaffold for the others, <BioLink id="palestrina">Palestrina</BioLink> gives four melodies the same weight.

## Looking Forward

Around 1600, Italian composers turned away from equal voices toward **monody**: a single solo voice over a bass line and chords, built to carry the feeling of a text. <BioLink id="monteverdi">Claudio Monteverdi</BioLink> defended a "second practice" in which the words govern the music and a dissonance may arrive unprepared when the text demands it. The Baroque concerto, which sets a soloist against an ensemble, grows from the same preference for one voice standing out from the rest; the fugue keeps Renaissance imitation alive inside a stronger harmonic drive.
