## Introduction

The Classical movement in music values the same qualities <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> values in prose: clarity, proportion, balance, wit, and the elegant management of feeling within rational structures. Behind that shift is a social fact. As wealth moved from inherited land to accumulated money, a new middle-class public appeared with the means to buy tickets, scores, and instruments — and it wanted music it could actually follow. The Enlightenment principle that the best institution is the one doing the greatest good for the greatest number had a direct artistic corollary: the best music is the music that pleases the greatest number.

Everything this page calls clarity and transparency follows from that demand. Where Baroque music was elaborate, convoluted, and written for connoisseurs, Classical music strips back to clean lines, a single hearable tune, and forms whose logic a listener can track. The period had its own word for the goal, and it was <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s favourite: *natural*. A melody that unwinds in its own time and can be sung is natural; a worked-out fugue is contrived. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> wrote both the politics and the music articles for the *Encyclopédie*, on the view that they were the same subject.

## Texture & Voices

*How many voices are active, and how do they relate to each other?*

Classical music overwhelmingly favors **homophony** — a single clear melody supported by an accompaniment. The dense polyphony of the Renaissance and the elaborate counterpoint of the Baroque give way to a texture in which one voice leads and the others support. This is the musical equivalent of the shift from the Baroque's multiple competing dramatic forces to the Classical era's focus on a single, clearly articulated narrative line.

The Classical solo concerto extends this logic into the realm of political ideology. With one voice (the soloist) heard alongside, and sometimes pitted against, the collective (the orchestra), the concerto became an almost perfect sonic metaphor for the Enlightenment's view of the individual in society: a single consciousness navigating, collaborating with, and occasionally resisting the pressures of the group.

The concerto's purest version of that image is the **cadenza**: the orchestra stops altogether and the soloist plays alone — unaccompanied, virtuosic, briefly answerable to nobody — before the ensemble returns and the movement resumes. <BioLink id="mozart">Mozart</BioLink> improvised his own and never wrote them down, which is why performers still have to supply their own today. The individual voice, by design, never fixed.

### Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*

Elizabeth Bennet's perspective organizes the novel the way a Classical melody organizes a musical movement: other voices — Darcy, Jane, Mr. Bennet, Wickham — contribute, respond, and complicate, but the primary line of consciousness is always hers. The texture is transparent; the reader always knows whose perception is shaping events, just as Classical music always lets the melody be heard clearly above its accompaniment. The concerto form further sharpens this comparison: Elizabeth's refusals — of Collins, of Darcy's first proposal, of the entire social logic that treats marriage as a transaction — are the soloist's departures from what the orchestra expects and demands.

## Consonance & Dissonance

*How does the music handle tension and resolution?*

Classical music uses dissonance with precision and restraint. Moments of harmonic tension serve a structural purpose: they create the sense of departure in the development section of a sonata, building anticipation for the satisfying return to the home key in the recapitulation. Dissonance is a dramatic tool, but it is always in service of a larger architecture of resolution. The emotional range is real — <BioLink id="mozart">Mozart</BioLink> can be heartbreaking — but feeling is always shaped by form, never allowed to overwhelm it.

The largest example is not on this page. <BioLink id="mozart">Mozart</BioLink> opens the overture to *Don Giovanni* with a dark, unresolved sequence and then simply declines to resolve it — the same music returns three hours later, at the very end of the opera, when the murdered Commendatore arrives to drag Don Giovanni to hell. Tension introduced in the first minute and paid off in the last.

### Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*

The novel's central dissonance — Elizabeth's prejudice and Darcy's pride — creates real tension, difficult misunderstanding, genuine pain. Yet the novel's architecture ensures that this tension resolves; the misperceptions are corrected, the characters grow, and the resolution feels both surprising and inevitable. Like <BioLink id="mozart">Mozart</BioLink>, <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> makes formal satisfaction feel like emotional truth rendered with seeming ease and simplicity.

## Structure & Freedom

*How does the music relate to inherited formal structures?*

One of the Classical era's defining achievements is the **cadence** — a musical punctuation mark that signals the end of a phrase or section and creates a sense of arrival.

There are three kinds, and they work like punctuation. An **open** cadence is a comma — it pauses, but the sentence is not finished. A **closed** cadence is a full stop. A **deceptive** cadence is a colon: it arrives somewhere that sounds like an ending, then obliges the music to carry on. Cadences matter more here than in Baroque music for a textural reason. Where voices overlap continuously they rarely rest together, so the joins stay hidden; homophonic melody pauses and breathes, and one can hear where its sentences end.

Where Baroque music tends toward continuous forward motion (one idea flowing into the next without strong stopping points), Classical music deploys cadences to organize its material into clear phrases with beginnings, middles, and ends. The cadence gives Classical music its quality of *narrative* in contrast to the Baroque's more circular, ongoing energy of perpetual motion.

This cadential language made possible the Classical era's great formal invention: **sonata form**, which organizes a movement into three sections. The **exposition** presents two contrasting themes in two different keys. The **development** fragments, combines, and transforms those themes in unstable harmonic territory, multiplying "open" cadences that defer resolution. The **recapitulation** restates both themes in the home key, delivering the large-scale closed cadence the whole movement has been building toward. The form is satisfying because it creates expectations and then fulfills them or even, in the hands of a master like <BioLink id="haydn">Haydn</BioLink> or <BioLink id="mozart">Mozart</BioLink>, wittily subverts them.


### Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*

<BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s plots follow the same grammatical logic. The novel sets up social and romantic expectations with great clarity (exposition), complicates them through misunderstanding and misdirection (development), and resolves them in ways the reader did not predict and cannot argue with (recapitulation). <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> even uses the deceptive cadence: Wickham appears to resolve Elizabeth's need for a partner, but lands in the wrong key entirely, prolonging the tension rather than ending it. <BioLink id="haydn">Haydn</BioLink>'s "Surprise" Symphony, below, is that wit at its most concentrated. The composer plays with the listener's expectations within the same formal framework that <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> manipulates at every level of structure in her novels.

## Looking Back

The shift has a physical form. A harpsichord can only be suddenly loud or soft; the piano — *harpsichord with soft and loud*, as its full name had it — can swell and fade like a voice. An instrument built for counterpoint gives way to one built for singing. <BioLink id="bach">Bach</BioLink>'s dense polyphony and <BioLink id="handel">Handel</BioLink>'s dramatic contrasts have given way to <BioLink id="mozart">Mozart</BioLink>'s transparent textures and balanced phrases. Baroque complexity is all on the surface. Classical complexity lies beneath it: the texture seems simple, even light, but any lapse in execution is instantly exposed by that very transparency. A similar shift occurs in the transition from <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink>'s periodic sentences to <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s clean prose — both demand total mastery, but reveal it in opposite ways.

## Looking Forward

Romanticism will take these forms and stretch them. What changes first is simply scale — of orchestras, of movements, of what a composer feels entitled to ask of a listener's patience. What changes underneath is harder to hear and more consequential, and the Romanticism page takes it up: whether the form exists to hold the feeling, or the feeling to fill the form. <BioLink id="beethoven">Beethoven</BioLink> is where the balance tips, and he was already at work while <BioLink id="haydn">Haydn</BioLink> was still writing.
