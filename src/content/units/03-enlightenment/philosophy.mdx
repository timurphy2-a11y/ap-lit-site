# Philosophical Context: The Enlightenment

## Introduction

The Enlightenment was not a single doctrine or a unanimous celebration of reason. It included rationalists and empiricists, reformers and skeptics, Christians, deists, materialists, and atheists. Its participants disagreed about human nature, religion, political authority, and the limits of knowledge. They shared no complete program, but many placed a new burden of proof on claims protected by antiquity, office, revelation, or custom.

This change made criticism public and judgment revisable. A political institution had to explain its legitimacy; an account of nature had to answer to observation and argument; a moral judgment had to survive perspectives beyond the judge's own. *Pride and Prejudice* brings that demand into social life. Its central intelligence belongs to a woman who thinks for herself and discovers that independence without self-correction can harden into prejudice.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do human beings stand in the order of things?*

<BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> defines enlightenment as emergence from **self-incurred immaturity**, dependence on another's direction when a person possesses the capacity to use reason independently. The obstacle is not a natural lack of intelligence. It is the failure of courage and resolve that allows a book, official, cleric, or guardian to assume responsibility for judgment.

<PullQuote attribution="Immanuel Kant · 'An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?' · 1784">
Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's own understanding without the guidance of another.
</PullQuote>

Kant's call does not describe an accomplished condition. Enlightenment is a practice through which people become capable of public reasoning. His essay also preserves institutional obedience: a person may have to follow commands while serving in an official role yet remain free, as a scholar addressing the reading public, to criticize that role and propose reform. Autonomy therefore develops within a social world rather than through escape from every structure.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> offers a different account of human development. In the *Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality Among Mankind* (1755), pre-social human beings possess **amour de soi**, a concern for their own preservation moderated by natural pity. They also possess **perfectibility**, the open-ended capacity to change in response to circumstance. Perfectibility makes language, cooperation, and reflection possible; it also makes new forms of dependence and inequality possible.

Rousseau uses **amour-propre** for self-concern shaped by comparison and the desire for recognition. Once people measure themselves through the eyes of others, appearing can separate from being. Esteem becomes scarce because one person's superiority depends on another's inferiority. Amour-propre can produce achievement, sociability, and sensitivity to judgment, but it also feeds vanity, resentment, concealment, and domination. Rousseau's critique concerns a historically developed form of social selfhood rather than a simple claim that nature is good and society is evil.

<BioLink id="smith">Adam Smith</BioLink> places social judgment at the foundation of moral life. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), **sympathy** is an imaginative effort to enter another person's situation. It never gives direct access to another mind. Observers reconstruct what another person might feel, then compare those sentiments with what seems fitting to the circumstances.

Self-judgment develops through the same process. Smith's **impartial spectator** is an imagined fair-minded observer whose perspective checks immediate self-interest. Moral maturity requires a person to distinguish the pleasure of receiving praise from the worthiness of deserving it. The social gaze that Rousseau treats as a source of comparison can, in Smith's account, become an instrument of conscience when it is disciplined by fairness.

<BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Mary Wollstonecraft</BioLink> exposes the exclusions within accounts of rational agency. *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) argues that virtue must rest on reason and independence for women as well as men. Women trained to value beauty, obedience, and male approval will develop the very vanity and cunning later used to justify their subordination. Dependence produces the conduct offered as evidence that dependence is natural.

Wollstonecraft directs part of her argument against Rousseau's *Émile*. Rousseau designs Sophie's education around the needs of Émile: she should please, support, and moderate a man rather than cultivate equal intellectual and moral independence. Wollstonecraft accepts that education forms character and rejects the sex-specific limit Rousseau places upon that principle. A universal account of virtue cannot deny women the education required to practice it.

### Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*

Elizabeth Bennet begins with a Kantian strength: she refuses to let rank determine belief. Lady Catherine's title, Darcy's wealth, and Collins's clerical office do not compel her assent. Her independence becomes unreliable when injured pride begins selecting the evidence it will credit. Wickham's attention confirms the judgment she wants to make; Darcy's insult makes contrary evidence easier to dismiss.

Rousseau and Smith provide complementary readings of the error. Elizabeth's delight in her own discernment turns judgment into a form of *amour-propre*. Darcy's letter then forces her to imagine conduct from perspectives beyond her own. "Till this moment I never knew myself" marks neither humiliation before authority nor sudden certainty. It marks the discovery that independent judgment remains answerable to evidence and revision.

The novel extends the problem across gender and class. Mrs. Bennet's anxiety reflects the economic danger created by entail and limited female independence, even when her conduct makes that anxiety comic. Wickham makes social appearance an instrument of deception. Darcy's rank encourages him to confuse inherited position with deserved esteem. Austen's moral world is social in Smith's sense: character becomes visible through conduct observed over time, yet every observer must account for the distortions of interest, vanity, and incomplete knowledge.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do human beings know what they know, and how far can knowledge take them?*

<BioLink id="bacon">Francis Bacon</BioLink> supplied an influential image for the new suspicion of inherited authority. People often assume that beliefs surviving from antiquity must have survived because they were best. Bacon argues that time resembles a river:

<PullQuote attribution="Francis Bacon · 'The Advancement of Learning' · 1605">
Time seemeth to be of the nature of a river or stream, which carrieth down to us that which is light and blown up, and sinketh and drowneth that which is weighty and solid.
</PullQuote>

The image does not prove that inherited knowledge is false. It reverses the presumption that survival establishes truth. Tradition becomes evidence to investigate rather than a verdict that ends investigation. Enlightenment writers inherit that polemical change while applying it to texts, institutions, natural philosophy, and histories of their own past.

<BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink>'s *Principia* offered a model of explanatory reach. Mathematical laws joined terrestrial and celestial motion within one account. The achievement depended on observation, calculation, inherited problems, and contributions from many investigators; it did not arise from reason operating without history or evidence. <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink>'s instruction to "follow Nature" translates the period's admiration for order into an aesthetic claim: judgment should discover principles embodied in successful works and in the larger patterns they imitate.

The *Encyclopédie*, edited by <BioLink id="diderot">Denis Diderot</BioLink> and Jean le Rond d'Alembert, made knowledge an institutional project. Its articles joined philosophy and natural inquiry to manufacturing, tools, and mechanical arts. Cross-references allowed an apparently neutral organization of subjects to expose contradictions and redirect readers toward controversial arguments. The work's publication under censorship also demonstrated that organizing knowledge redistributes authority: an encyclopedia decides which practices deserve preservation, which distinctions matter, and how one field connects to another.

<BioLink id="hume">David Hume</BioLink> applies empiricism to the assumptions that make empirical knowledge possible. A billiard ball strikes another, and the second moves. Observation supplies sequence and repeated conjunction; it does not reveal a necessary power binding the events. The expectation that similar causes will produce similar effects comes from **custom or habit**. Repetition trains the imagination to anticipate what usually follows.

Hume's argument does not prevent natural inquiry. It denies that reason can prove the uniformity of nature without circularity. Inductive practice continues because human beings are constituted to form expectations from experience. Hume's **mitigated skepticism** therefore joins intellectual modesty to ordinary confidence: conclusions remain corrigible and inquiry stays within the limits of experience.

At the end of Book I of the *Treatise of Human Nature*, philosophical reflection leaves Hume unable to justify beliefs that ordinary life requires. His response is memorable:

<PullQuote attribution="David Hume · 'A Treatise of Human Nature' · 1739–1740">
I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends.
</PullQuote>

Conversation and play do not refute skepticism. They show that natural belief and social practice resume when abstract doubt reaches an impasse. Philosophy returns afterward with less claim to absolute certainty.

The period's religious arguments also changed the standards of knowledge. **Deism** grounds belief in a creator upon reason and the observable order of nature rather than a particular revelation, church, or miracle. Nature appears public and universal in a way disputed scriptures do not. A perfect creator, some deists argued, would establish laws sufficient to govern creation rather than repeatedly suspend them through miraculous repair.

Deism was neither traditional Christianity nor atheism, and it did not define the entire Enlightenment. Christian, Jewish, and other religious thinkers also used criticism, historical scholarship, and natural philosophy. The conflict concerned the authority of revelation, the interpretation of providence, and the kinds of evidence religious belief could require.

The Lisbon earthquake forced those questions into public view. <BioLink id="voltaire">Voltaire</BioLink>'s *Candide* attacks an optimism capable of preserving itself through every disaster. Pangloss does more than offer a bad explanation. His system can discourage intervention. When Jacques the Anabaptist falls into Lisbon harbor after saving a sailor, Candide prepares to follow him. Pangloss stops the rescue by proving that the harbor was formed expressly for Jacques to drown there. Explanation becomes a reason not to act.

The command to cultivate the garden answers that fatalism. Voltaire does not replace one universal system with another. The survivors accept limited knowledge, shared labor, and responsibility for suffering close enough to address. Practical action matters precisely because no theory has converted every evil into a necessary good.

Smith brings the limit back to moral perception. Sympathy depends on imaginative reconstruction rather than direct access to another consciousness. Bias, social rank, personal interest, and partial evidence can distort it. Moral knowledge remains possible, but its method requires repeated observation, comparison of perspectives, and correction.

### Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*

The novel begins with confident causal stories. Elizabeth treats Darcy's manner as evidence of settled character; Darcy treats the behavior of several Bennets as evidence that connection with the family will produce degradation; the neighborhood converts fragments of conduct into reputations. Each inference moves beyond what has actually been observed.

Darcy's letter does not provide mathematical proof. It supplies testimony, names, chronology, and claims that Elizabeth can compare with remembered conduct. She revises her judgment through a Humean and Smithian discipline: expectations remain necessary, but they must answer to fuller experience and competing perspectives. The result is neither skepticism about character nor confidence in first impressions. It is fallible knowledge earned over time.

Hume's return to conversation after philosophical crisis offers a useful analogy. Elizabeth cannot solve the general problem of knowing another mind before continuing to dine, walk, converse, and choose. The drawing room remains a place of judgment after certainty has failed. Greater care, not withdrawal from social life, becomes the available virtue.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern political and social life?*

<BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink>'s *Two Treatises of Government* attacks Robert Filmer's claim that political authority descends from Adam's paternal power into hereditary monarchy. Locke begins instead from natural equality. No person is born with a political title to rule another. The **law of nature**, accessible through reason, obliges people not to harm others in life, health, liberty, or possessions.

Political society supplies known laws, impartial judges, and power to enforce decisions. Individuals authorize government to secure rights that remain vulnerable in the state of nature. The transfer is conditional. A government that systematically violates its trust places itself in a state of war against the people and may be resisted or replaced.

Locke develops concepts also used by <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>, including natural rights, the state of nature, and social authorization, but changes their operation. Hobbes makes undivided sovereignty the necessary condition of peace. Locke subjects political power to natural law, institutional division, and continuing public purposes. Hobbes's sovereign may be a monarch or an assembly; Locke's argument should not be reduced to an answer to monarchy alone.

<BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s distinction between public and private reason offers a less revolutionary account of criticism. "Private" reason is the use of understanding within a role such as soldier, official, or cleric; institutions may require obedience for coordinated action. "Public" reason is the argument a scholar addresses to the reading world. Kant permits obedience and criticism to coexist, trusting publication and debate to make gradual reform possible.

Voltaire demonstrates this public use of reason before Kant names it. When the Protestant merchant Jean Calas was executed in Toulouse in 1762 for the alleged murder of his son, Voltaire did not begin with certainty about Calas's innocence. He investigated testimony, procedure, and motive; the evidence changed his judgment. He then used pamphlets, correspondence, publicity, and elite networks to challenge the verdict. The conviction was overturned posthumously in 1765.

Voltaire's campaign matters because self-correction precedes advocacy. He did not defend Calas by treating his first impression as moral insight. He made a public case strong enough to reverse an institution's judgment. The episode also exposes a limit within Voltaire himself: his defense of toleration coexisted with vicious generalizations about Jews and Judaism. Enlightenment criticism could identify prejudice in an institution without freeing the critic from prejudice.

<BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink> applies the demand for legitimacy to male authority over women. Social arrangements deny women education and economic independence, then describe the resulting dependence as natural. Political and domestic authority must answer to the same standard: power cannot become legitimate merely through age, custom, or the interests of those who possess it.

The Atlantic revolutions tested claims of universal freedom. The American Declaration drew on natural-rights language while political rights remained restricted and slavery endured. The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen announced equality before the law while legislators disputed the status of women, free people of color, enslaved people, and colonial subjects. Olympe de Gouges and Wollstonecraft exposed gendered exclusions through the language of rights itself.

The Haitian Revolution forced the broadest claim. Beginning in 1791, enslaved people in Saint-Domingue acted on their own traditions of resistance as well as circulating revolutionary arguments. Their struggle compelled France to abolish slavery in its colonies in 1794 and culminated in Haitian independence in 1804. Universal rights acquired political force when people excluded from their announced scope claimed those rights without waiting for permission.

These revolutions did not follow a simple sequence from philosophical reason to political liberation. War, property, religion, race, empire, local organization, and competing interests shaped their courses. Revolutionary governments could extend rights and exercise coercion. The Terror emerged amid foreign war, civil conflict, counterrevolution, faction, and emergency government; it should not be treated as reason's inevitable conclusion.

### Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*

Austen's characters inhabit structures of class, gender, property, and family authority without reducing moral judgment to obedience. Lady Catherine treats rank and ancestry as self-justifying. Her demand that Elizabeth reject Darcy resembles Filmer's logic in miniature: inherited position should settle the future without the consent of those governed by it. Elizabeth refuses because Lady Catherine possesses social power but no moral title to make the decision.

Mr. Bennet shows the opposite failure. He possesses legal and paternal authority but retreats into amusement rather than using it responsibly. Mr. Collins converts dependence into servility, borrowing Lady Catherine's opinions because proximity to rank substitutes for judgment. Darcy begins with legitimate social power over an estate and dependents; the novel evaluates that authority through conduct rather than title alone.

The marriage plot makes consent indispensable while exposing the pressures surrounding it. Charlotte Lucas's choice reflects limited economic alternatives; Lydia's vulnerability reveals failures of education and guardianship; Elizabeth can refuse Collins and Darcy because her family grants her more latitude than many women possessed. The novel does not imagine choice outside institutions. It asks when a decision made within unequal conditions remains thoughtful, responsible, and free.

## Looking back: The Enlightenment constructs its past

Enlightenment writers inherited the Renaissance practice of creating a usable history. Renaissance humanists had described an intervening Middle Age so that their own work could appear as recovery and rebirth. Voltaire and later writers selected a different Renaissance: rational, anticlerical, and already moving toward Enlightenment criticism.

That construction illuminated real conflicts while distorting their setting. <BioLink id="pico">Pico</BioLink> was a Christian mystic as well as a defender of human freedom; <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink>'s natural philosophy developed within institutions and arguments inherited from earlier centuries; Renaissance humanism included religious renewal as well as criticism of clerical authority. The Enlightenment invented the Renaissance it needed, just as Renaissance humanists had invented the Middle Ages they needed.

The strongest continuity from the Baroque lies in the problem of authority. <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>, <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>, and <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> had asked how freedom, knowledge, and obedience could coexist. Locke, Hume, Rousseau, Smith, Kant, and Wollstonecraft alter the answers while retaining the conflict. Public reason expands; certainty does not.

## Looking forward: Reason, feeling, and authenticity

Romanticism inherits Enlightenment problems rather than simply rejecting reason for passion. Hume makes imagination and habit necessary to ordinary knowledge. Smith makes sympathetic imagination central to moral judgment. Rousseau joins social criticism to natural feeling, autobiographical self-examination, and suspicion of civilization. Kant will give imagination a larger role in aesthetic judgment.

Rousseau provides the clearest bridge. His analysis of *amour-propre* asks whether social performance has separated appearance from an authentic self. His *Confessions* turns that question into autobiographical experiment; his educational and political works ask what institutions might form freer people. Romantic writers will intensify the claims of nature, feeling, imagination, and individual experience while remaining indebted to Enlightenment debates about freedom and self-knowledge.
