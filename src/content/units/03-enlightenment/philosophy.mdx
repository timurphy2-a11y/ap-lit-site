# Philosophical Context: The Enlightenment

## Introduction

After the Baroque's drama — its wars, its vertigo, its agonized negotiations between faith and reason — the Enlightenment can feel like someone opening the curtains. The light comes in. The temperature drops. Reason, which <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> had declared insufficient and <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> had yoked to the service of absolute power, is rehabilitated as the instrument of progress, freedom, and human flourishing. <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s motto for the age — *Sapere aude*, "dare to know" — captures the spirit: stop being afraid of your own intelligence. Think for yourself.

This is the intellectual world in which <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> writes, and it is essential to understand that she is an Enlightenment thinker even though she is writing during the Romantic period. Her novels do not celebrate emotional intensity, visionary experience, or rebellion against social norms. They celebrate judgment — the capacity to see clearly, to weigh evidence, to correct one's errors, and to act well within social structures that are imperfect but navigable. Elizabeth Bennet is, in this sense, <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s ideal Enlightenment subject: someone who dares to think for herself, who refuses to defer to the judgments of others simply because they hold positions of authority, but who also learns — painfully — that her own judgment is not infallible.

But the Enlightenment is not only about reason in the abstract. Two of the period's most penetrating thinkers — <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> and <BioLink id="smith">Adam Smith</BioLink> — turn the Enlightenment's analytical power onto social life itself, asking how the experience of living among others shapes (and distorts) who we are and how we judge. Their insights connect to <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s fiction more directly than almost anything else in the period.

### A Note on the Readings

Some of the readings in this unit illuminate the *period* — the political and scientific landscape of the eighteenth century. Others illuminate the *novel* — the specific dynamics of social perception, moral judgment, and self-knowledge that <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> dramatizes. Both kinds are valuable, but they serve different purposes. <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> and <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink> give you the Enlightenment's foundations; <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>, <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink>, <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>, <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink>, and <BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink> give you the tools to read *Pride and Prejudice*.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

The Enlightenment's answer is, by comparison with what came before and after, remarkably confident. Human beings are rational agents. We are capable of understanding the laws of nature (<BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink> has proved this), of governing ourselves through just institutions (<BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> will explain how), and of moral progress through the exercise of reason. We are not <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s trembling reeds, suspended between abysses; we are thinking beings who can — if we have the courage — take charge of our own lives and our own societies.

<BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s "What is Enlightenment?" (1784) articulates this confidence with memorable clarity. "Immaturity," <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> argues, is not a condition imposed on us by nature — it is a condition we impose on ourselves through laziness and cowardice. We let others think for us because thinking for ourselves is difficult and frightening. Enlightenment is simply the decision to grow up — to use your own reason, even when it is hard, even when it leads to conclusions that make you uncomfortable.

<PullQuote attribution="Immanuel Kant, What is Enlightenment?, 1784">
  Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without guidance from another.
</PullQuote>

But the Enlightenment's portrait of the human being is richer and darker than <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s rallying cry alone suggests. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755) offers a devastating counter-narrative. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> agrees that human beings are naturally good — but he argues that civilization has corrupted us, and the instrument of that corruption is something he calls *amour-propre*: the need to be esteemed by others, the compulsion to see ourselves through others' eyes.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> distinguishes *amour-propre* from *amour de soi-même* — a natural, healthy self-regard that every creature possesses, the simple desire for self-preservation. *Amour de soi* is innocent; it asks nothing of other people. *Amour-propre* is social and comparative; it can only exist in a world where people observe and rank each other. It emerges, <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> argues, at the moment when human beings first gather together and begin to perform: "Each began to look at the others and to want to be looked at, and public esteem was valued." From that moment, "being and appearing became two entirely different things" — and from that split emerged "imposing pomp, deceitful cunning, and all their attendant vices." Reason itself, far from being our salvation, is what produces this disease: "It is reason that gives rise to *amour-propre*, and it is reflection that strengthens it."

<BioLink id="smith">Adam Smith</BioLink>, writing just four years after <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>, offers a portrait of social self-consciousness that is equally penetrating but less despairing. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> argues that our capacity to imagine ourselves in another person's position — what he calls "sympathy" — is the foundation of all moral life. We understand others by imaginatively placing ourselves in their situation; we judge ourselves by imagining how a fair-minded observer would view our conduct. <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> calls this imagined observer the "impartial spectator" — an internalized perspective that functions as the basis of conscience. We cannot see our own faces directly, <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> observes; we need a mirror. In the same way, we cannot judge our own sentiments directly; we need to see ourselves through the eyes of others.

Where <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> sees the social gaze as primarily corrupting, <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> sees it as potentially ennobling — but only if we learn to care about *deserving* approval rather than merely *receiving* it. The person who simply craves praise is governed by vanity (<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *amour-propre*). The person who wants to be genuinely worthy of praise — who consults the impartial spectator honestly — is on the path to moral maturity.

<BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink> extends the analysis to women's position in this social world. If *amour-propre* corrupts everyone, it is especially destructive for women, who are educated to value appearance over substance, charm over competence, and the approval of men over the development of their own rational faculties. Women kept in dependence and ignorance will inevitably become what their circumstances make them — not because of any deficiency in their nature but because they have been denied the opportunity to develop the moral resources (reason, independence, honest self-examination) that genuine virtue requires.

**Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*:** <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s novel is a laboratory for testing <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s diagnosis and <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink>'s remedy. Every character in the book is caught in the web of *amour-propre* — performing, comparing, judging, and being judged. Mrs. Bennet's obsession with advantageous marriages is *amour-propre* at its most anxious. Wickham's charm is a masterclass in managing appearances — being and appearing have split entirely apart in him, and his social performance conceals a rotten character. Darcy's pride is *amour-propre* in its aristocratic form: his sense of his own worth is inseparable from his consciousness of his social rank relative to others.

Elizabeth's journey is the novel's great test case. She prides herself on her perceptiveness — her ability to see through appearances and judge character accurately. But her judgment of Darcy has been shaped by *amour-propre* all along: she liked Wickham because he flattered her, and she disliked Darcy because he wounded her pride. The moment she recognizes this — "Till this moment I never knew myself" — is the moment she finally consults her impartial spectator honestly. It is an Enlightenment awakening: not just the correction of a factual error, but the discovery that her own moral perception has been distorted by the very social self-consciousness <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> diagnosed. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s genius is to show that this distortion is not a permanent condition. Elizabeth can learn. Darcy can learn. The impartial spectator can be cultivated. But the cultivation requires a specific kind of courage — the willingness to see yourself as you really are, not as you wish to appear.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The Enlightenment's answer: very far indeed — in the domain of nature. <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink> has shown that the universe operates according to mathematical laws that human reason can discover. <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink>'s "Essay on Criticism" — written in heroic couplets whose very form embodies rational order — argues that the rules of good judgment are the rules of nature itself: "First follow Nature, and your judgment frame / By her just standard, which is still the same." Knowledge is not merely possible; it is a moral obligation.

But the Enlightenment's most interesting thinkers recognize that moral knowledge — knowledge of other people, knowledge of yourself — is a different and more treacherous enterprise than scientific knowledge. <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink>'s poem also warns against the overconfidence of those who mistake partial knowledge for complete understanding. <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> distinguishes carefully between the *public* use of reason (which must always be free) and the *private* use of reason (which may legitimately be constrained by one's duties within institutions). Even within the Enlightenment's celebration of reason, there is an awareness that knowledge must be disciplined and that judgment requires humility.

<BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink>'s account of sympathy makes this epistemological challenge vivid. We understand other people's feelings not through direct access but through an act of imagination — we construct, in our own minds, a version of what we think they feel. But this means that our understanding of others is always partly a fiction, shaped by our own experiences, biases, and limitations. Sympathy is the best instrument we have for moral knowledge, but it is inherently imperfect. We can get other people wrong — and we will, especially when *amour-propre* is distorting our perception, when we are motivated by vanity or wounded pride to see what flatters us rather than what is true.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> pushes this point further: reason itself, the Enlightenment's proudest instrument, can become an obstacle to genuine understanding. "It is philosophy that isolates him," <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> writes of the civilized man. The very capacity for reflection that distinguishes us from animals also separates us from the natural sympathy that would otherwise connect us to others. The philosopher can rationalize his way out of compassion; the uneducated person, closer to natural feeling, rushes to help.

**Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*:** The novel is a sustained exploration of how intelligent people go wrong in their judgments — and how they can learn to go right. Elizabeth trusts first impressions and the evidence of surfaces; Darcy trusts social categories and inherited assumptions. Wickham is plausible and charming; his narrative is believed because it flatters Elizabeth's existing prejudice against Darcy. The novel insists that moral knowledge — real knowledge of other people and of yourself — requires not just intelligence but something closer to what <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> describes: the patient, honest exercise of sympathetic imagination, disciplined by the willingness to revise your conclusions when the evidence demands it. Elizabeth is brilliant, but brilliance is not enough. She must learn to see past the distortions of her own *amour-propre* — and that requires not more reason but a different, more honest way of using it.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

The Enlightenment produces the most influential theories of political legitimacy in Western history, and understanding them is essential to understanding the period even where they do not connect directly to <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s fiction. <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> argues that human beings possess natural rights — to life, liberty, and property — that no government may violate, and that political authority is legitimate only when it rests on the consent of the governed. This is a direct response to <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> (whom students have encountered in the Baroque unit): where <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> argued that the only escape from the war of all against all is submission to an absolute sovereign, <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> insists that sovereign power is limited by natural law and that a government that violates its citizens' rights may legitimately be overthrown. These ideas will fuel the American and French Revolutions and permanently reshape the political landscape of the modern world.

But the individual-authority thread in the Enlightenment is not only about political revolution. <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s distinction between public and private reason offers a more nuanced picture. As a citizen, you may be obligated to obey — the soldier must drill, the taxpayer must pay. But as a *scholar* — that is, as a thinking person addressing the public — you must always be free to criticize, to question, to propose reforms. The Enlightenment does not simply overthrow authority; it reimagines authority as something that must justify itself before the tribunal of reason.

<BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink> applies this logic to the authority of men over women. The subordination of women is not justified by nature, she argues, but sustained by artificial arrangements — lack of education, economic dependence, a culture that rewards women for being ornamental rather than rational. This is authority without legitimacy, and it corrupts both the rulers and the ruled. <BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink>'s analysis draws on both <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> and <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink>: the system of male authority over women produces a world in which women are trapped in *amour-propre* — valued for their appearance rather than their character — and denied the opportunity to develop the impartial spectator that would allow them to judge and act independently.

**Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*:** <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s world is thick with structures of authority — class, gender, family, economic dependence — and her characters must navigate them constantly. The novel does not advocate overthrowing these structures (<BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> is not a revolutionary), but it subjects them to rigorous scrutiny. Lady Catherine de Bourgh represents authority based on rank alone, stripped of any rational or moral justification — and she is ridiculous. Mr. Bennet represents the failure of paternal authority exercised without responsibility. Mr. Collins represents authority internalized as servility — a man who has made himself a mirror of his patroness's *amour-propre*. Darcy must learn that his social authority means nothing without moral authority earned through conduct. Elizabeth asserts her right to judge for herself — to be, in <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s terms, a public reasoner — even when every social pressure tells her to defer.

The novel's resolution depends on the possibility that individuals can, through honest self-examination, earn the authority that mere social position cannot confer. This is the Enlightenment's deepest promise: not that authority will disappear, but that it can be made legitimate — grounded in character, judgment, and mutual respect rather than in rank, wealth, or the performance of status.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From the Baroque:** The contrast is one of emotional temperature as much as intellectual content. <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s anguished human being, suspended between infinities, becomes <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s self-governing rational agent. <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>'s terrified subjects, surrendering their freedom to an absolute sovereign, become <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink>'s citizens, consenting to government that protects their natural rights. The Baroque's sense that the universe is overwhelming and that reason alone cannot save us gives way to the Enlightenment's faith that reason, properly exercised, can illuminate everything it touches. The darkness lifts — or seems to. But <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>, from within the Enlightenment itself, is already diagnosing a new kind of darkness: the social corruption that civilization produces, the gap between being and appearing that *amour-propre* opens up in every human heart.

**Toward Romanticism:** The Romantics will take <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s diagnosis and run with it. If civilization corrupts, perhaps the answer is a return to nature, to feeling, to the truths that social life obscures. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> himself — who appears in this unit as a social critic and moral psychologist — will reappear in the Romanticism unit as something very different: the man who wrote the *Confessions*, who insisted on radical emotional honesty, and who opened the door to an entirely new understanding of what it means to be human. The Enlightenment's faith in rational self-improvement will give way to the Romantic conviction that the deepest truths are accessed through imagination and passion, not analysis. But notice what carries over: both periods are intensely interested in the problem of authenticity — in the gap between what we are and what we appear to be. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> and <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink>, despite their vast differences, are both writers for whom that gap is the central moral problem.
