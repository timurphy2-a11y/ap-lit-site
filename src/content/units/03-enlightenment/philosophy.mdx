# Philosophical Context: The Enlightenment

## Introduction

<BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s definition of Enlightenment — *Sapere aude*, dare to use your own understanding — captures the period's confidence but not its complexity. The Enlightenment's most interesting thinkers know that moral knowledge is more ambiguous than physics. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* diagnoses *amour-propre* — the corrosive impulse to see yourself through others' eyes. <BioLink id="smith">Adam Smith</BioLink> argues in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* that moral judgment depends on sympathetic imagination, not calculation. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s *Pride and Prejudice* is a laboratory for testing these ideas: a novel in which every character is caught in the web of social self-consciousness, and in which the path to genuine understanding requires not more reason but a more honest way of using it.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

<BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s answer is stirring: human beings are rational agents, capable of thinking for themselves — if they have the courage. "Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without guidance from another." The Enlightenment human being is defined by this potential for self-governance.

<PullQuote attribution="Immanuel Kant, What is Enlightenment?, 1784">
  Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without guidance from another.
</PullQuote>

But the Enlightenment's portrait of the human being is richer and darker than <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s rallying cry alone suggests. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755) offers a devastating counter-narrative. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> agrees that human beings are naturally good — but he argues that civilization has corrupted us, and the instrument of that corruption is *amour-propre*: the need to be esteemed by others, the compulsion to see ourselves through others' eyes.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> distinguishes *amour-propre* from *amour de soi-même* — a natural, healthy self-regard, the simple desire for self-preservation. *Amour de soi* is innocent; it asks nothing of other people. *Amour-propre* is social and comparative; it can only exist in a world where people observe and rank each other. It emerges at the moment when human beings first gather together and begin to perform: "Each began to look at the others and to want to be looked at." From that moment, "being and appearing became two entirely different things."

<BioLink id="smith">Adam Smith</BioLink>, writing just four years after <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>, offers a portrait of social self-consciousness that is equally penetrating but less despairing. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> argues that our capacity to imagine ourselves in another person's position — what he calls "sympathy" — is the foundation of all moral life. We judge ourselves by imagining how a fair-minded observer would view our conduct. <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> calls this imagined observer the "impartial spectator" — an internalized perspective that functions as the basis of conscience.

Where <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> sees the social gaze as primarily corrupting, <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> sees it as potentially ennobling, but only if we learn to care about *deserving* approval rather than merely *receiving* it.

<BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink> extends the analysis to women's position in this social world. If *amour-propre* corrupts everyone, it is especially destructive for women, who are educated to value appearance over substance and the approval of men over the development of their own rational faculties.

**Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*:** Every character is caught in the web of *amour-propre*. Wickham's charm is a masterclass in managing appearances — being and appearing have split entirely apart in him. Elizabeth prides herself on her perceptiveness, but her judgment of Darcy has been shaped by *amour-propre* all along: she liked Wickham because he flattered her, and she disliked Darcy because he wounded her pride. The moment she recognizes this — "Till this moment I never knew myself" — is the moment she finally consults her impartial spectator honestly.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The Enlightenment's answer: very far indeed — in the domain of nature. <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink> has shown that the universe operates according to mathematical laws. <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink>'s "Essay on Criticism" argues that the rules of good judgment are the rules of nature itself: "First follow Nature, and your judgment frame / By her just standard, which is still the same." Knowledge is not merely possible; it is a moral obligation.

But moral knowledge — knowledge of other people, knowledge of yourself — is a different and more treacherous enterprise than scientific knowledge. <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> distinguishes carefully between the *public* use of reason (which must always be free) and the *private* use (which may legitimately be constrained by institutional duties). Even within the Enlightenment's celebration of reason, there is an awareness that judgment requires humility.

<BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink>'s account of sympathy makes this epistemological challenge vivid. We understand other people's feelings not through direct access but through an act of imagination — we construct, in our own minds, a version of what we think they feel. But this means our understanding of others is always partly a fiction, shaped by our own biases. Sympathy is the best instrument we have for moral knowledge, but it is inherently imperfect.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> pushes the point further: reason itself can become an obstacle to genuine understanding. "It is philosophy that isolates him," <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> writes of the civilized man. The very capacity for reflection that distinguishes us from animals also separates us from the natural sympathy that would otherwise connect us to others.

**Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*:** The novel is a sustained exploration of how intelligent people go wrong in their judgments. Elizabeth trusts first impressions; Darcy trusts social categories. The novel insists that moral knowledge requires not just intelligence but something closer to what <BioLink id="smith">Smith</BioLink> describes: the patient, honest exercise of sympathetic imagination, disciplined by the willingness to revise your conclusions when the evidence demands it.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

The Enlightenment produces the most influential theories of political legitimacy in Western history. <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> argues that human beings possess natural rights — to life, liberty, and property — and that political authority is legitimate only when it rests on the consent of the governed. This is a direct response to <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>: where <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> argued that the only escape from the war of all against all is submission to an absolute sovereign, <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> insists that sovereign power is limited by natural law and that a government that violates its citizens' rights may legitimately be overthrown.

<BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s distinction between public and private reason offers a more nuanced picture. As a citizen, you may be obligated to obey — the soldier must drill, the taxpayer must pay. But as a *scholar* — a thinking person addressing the public — you must always be free to criticize and propose reforms. The Enlightenment does not simply overthrow authority; it reimagines authority as something that must justify itself before the tribunal of reason.

<BioLink id="wollstonecraft">Wollstonecraft</BioLink> applies this logic to the authority of men over women. The subordination of women is not justified by nature, she argues, but sustained by artificial arrangements — lack of education, economic dependence, a culture that rewards women for being ornamental rather than rational.

**Connection to *Pride and Prejudice*:** <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s world is thick with structures of authority — class, gender, family, economic dependence. The novel does not advocate overthrowing these structures, but it subjects them to rigorous scrutiny. Lady Catherine represents authority based on rank alone — and she is ridiculous. Mr. Collins represents authority internalized as servility. Elizabeth asserts her right to judge for herself, even when every social pressure tells her to defer.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From the Baroque:** <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s anguished human being, suspended between infinities, becomes <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>'s self-governing rational agent. <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>'s terrified subjects become <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink>'s citizens, consenting to government that protects their natural rights. The Baroque's sense that reason alone cannot save us gives way to the Enlightenment's faith that reason, properly exercised, can illuminate everything it touches. But <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>, from within the Enlightenment itself, is already diagnosing a new kind of darkness: the gap between being and appearing that *amour-propre* opens up in every human heart.

**Toward Romanticism:** The Romantics will take <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s diagnosis and extend it with a return to nature, to feeling, to the intuitive truths that social life obscures. But notice what carries over: both periods are intensely interested in the problem of authenticity, in the gap between what we are and what we appear to be. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink> and <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink>, despite their vast differences, are both writers for whom that gap is a central moral problem.
