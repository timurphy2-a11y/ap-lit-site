## Introduction

Modernism is really a convenient blanket term for a collection of *modernisms*, a series of radical breaks with the conventions that had governed European painting since the Renaissance. The coherent picture plane, the illusion of depth, the unified point of view, the smooth narrative surface: all of these are dismantled, questioned, or abandoned between roughly 1880 and 1950. This is the visual equivalent of <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s experiments with the form of the novel: fragmenting perspective, defamiliarizing the reader's understanding of American society, insisting that the old ways of seeing are inadequate to the complexity of modern experience.

The turn does not begin with Picasso. In the closing decades of the nineteenth century, <BioLink id="cezanne">Cézanne</BioLink> and <BioLink id="vangogh">Van Gogh</BioLink> had already started dismantling the conventions Renaissance perspective had established four centuries earlier — Cézanne fracturing solid form into planes of shifting color, Van Gogh loading the brushstroke itself with a psychological charge no smooth surface could carry. Three threads trace what happens once that turn completes: the fracture of pictorial space, the abandonment of light as a stable phenomenon, and the emergence of the painted surface as a subject in its own right.

## Figure & Space

*How is the human figure placed within the pictorial space?*

The unified, rational space of the Renaissance is progressively shattered by the development of Modern painting. <BioLink id="manet">Manet</BioLink> — working two decades before this unit's own period begins, but already unsettling the single coherent viewpoint the Renaissance had established — flattened his figures, eliminating the smooth tonal modeling that gave bodies their three-dimensional illusion, and built the *Bar at the Folies-Bergère* around a reflection that cannot be reconciled with the woman casting it. <BioLink id="cezanne">Cézanne</BioLink> pushed further: rather than record a scene from one fixed position, he painted objects and figures as planes of color seen from several slightly different angles at once, arguing in effect that no single vantage point is more valid or true than another. <BioLink id="picasso">Picasso</BioLink> saw a large Cézanne retrospective in 1907, months before finishing his own *Demoiselles*, and Georges <BioLink id="braque">Braque</BioLink> built early Cubism directly out of Cézanne's faceted landscapes. Cézanne is routinely called the movement's father; Cubism pursues his fracturing of form to its logical conclusion. Picasso and Braque broke figures and objects into shards, showing multiple viewpoints at once on a single flat surface. The figure is no longer whole, no longer occupying a coherent space. It is fragmented, reassembled, sometimes unrecognizable.

<BioLink id="monet">Monet</BioLink> reaches the same crisis from the opposite direction by dissolving the distinction between figure and ground, water and flower and sky. Cubism dismantles the object itself; the late *Water Lilies* dismantle the viewer's sense of stable relation to the object. It is a refusal of Renaissance space as total as Cubism's, arrived at by a painter who had spent fifty years working from direct observation and never claimed to be breaking anything.

### Connection to *Invisible Man*

<BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s narrator cannot be seen whole by the society around him. This is not merely a social observation but a formal principle: the novel itself fragments its protagonist across multiple identities, roles, and perspectives — college student, factory worker, Brotherhood orator, underground man — none of which adds up to a complete, coherent self visible from a single vantage point. This is Cubism applied to character. Just as <BioLink id="picasso">Picasso</BioLink>'s figures are seen from everywhere and nowhere simultaneously, <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s narrator is defined by the multiple, contradictory ways others see him (or refuse to see him). The "invisibility" of the title is not merely a metaphor for racism but a formal condition, the condition of a self that cannot be unified because the social space it inhabits is itself fractured and incoherent.

Cubism's refusal to privilege a single viewpoint is also a refusal of authority, the authority of the Renaissance tradition that said there was one correct way to see. In <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s novel, no single ideology (the college's accommodationism, the Brotherhood's Marxism, Ras the Exhorter's nationalism) is presented as the correct viable frame of meaning and authority. The narrator's journey is precisely the discovery that every frame is partial, and that the only honest position is the one that acknowledges its own partiality.

## Light & Shadow

*How does the painting use light — its source, quality, and emotional effect?*

Modernism dismantles the old systems of illumination. The Impressionists broke light into its component colors, applying pure pigments in small strokes that blend at a distance into luminous color; <BioLink id="vangogh">Van Gogh</BioLink> took that broken color and charged it with feeling rather than observation. Light is transformed from steady, unified force into a shimmering, unstable phenomenon that changes from moment to moment.

The more radical step is to paint light with no source at all. The two abstract artists here who take it describe what they are doing not as a retreat from reality but as a revelation of a deeper realism, a pictorial means of communicating emotion, consciousness, and spirit. <BioLink id="afklint">Hilma af Klint</BioLink> reached that position first, working from 1906 inside a spiritualist practice she kept out of public view. <BioLink id="kandinsky">Kandinsky</BioLink> reached it independently around 1912–13, then wrote a book about it, exhibited it, and taught it, ultimately achieving greater (and somewhat unjustified) acclaim as the pioneer of abstraction.

In Mondrian's grids or Rothko's color fields, light is not depicted but *produced* by the interaction of colors on the canvas. The painting does not display the lighting conditions of a presumed three-dimensional world; paint as medium generates its own luminosity.


### Connection to *Invisible Man*

<BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s novel opens and closes in a basement illuminated by 1,369 light bulbs wired into the power grid of Monopolated Light & Power — stolen light, repurposed light, light that is simultaneously a defiant assertion of visibility and a confession of marginality. This image condenses the entire Modernist problem of illumination: the old sources of light do not illuminate the narrator, so he must create his own. <BioLink id="kandinsky">Kandinsky</BioLink>'s claim that abstraction reveals what the eye cannot see doubles as <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s claim about his own narrator's invisibility. The Impressionist insight that light is not a stable, objective phenomenon but something that shifts and fragments depending on conditions becomes, in <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s hands, a political and existential insight: who gets illuminated, by what sources, and at whose expense are not neutral questions.

The novel's movement between visibility and invisibility, between moments of searing clarity and passages of surreal obscurity (the Liberty Paints factory, the hospital machine, the Harlem riot), recapitulates the Modernist dismantling of unified, rational illumination. Light in *Invisible Man* is partial, contested, and unreliable — exactly as it is in Modernist painting.

## Brushwork & Surface

*What is the physical character of the paint itself?*

In many of its iterations, Modernism openly embraces the reality that a painting is a flat surface covered with paint, and pretending otherwise is a kind of willful self-deception. The surface of the painting becomes the subject. <BioLink id="rembrandt">Rembrandt</BioLink> had hinted at it and <BioLink id="manet">Manet</BioLink> forced it into the open; <BioLink id="vangogh">Van Gogh</BioLink> made it impossible to ignore. His brushstrokes are thick, visible, and directional, laid down with an urgency that has nothing to do with accurately describing a cypress tree or a starlit sky and everything to do with recording the hand that made them.

<BioLink id="matisse">Matisse</BioLink> reaches the same conclusion by the opposite route. Van Gogh emphasizes the surface of the canvas by covering it in evidence of the artist's touch; Matisse insists on it by refusing the modeling and atmospheric recession that would let a viewer read a painting as a window into a three-dimensional world. <BioLink id="monet">Monet</BioLink>'s late panels offer a third version — worked and reworked across more than a decade until the surface carries the whole record of its own making.

Abstract Expressionists like Pollock dripped and flung paint, making the physical act of painting visible as the work's primary content. The artist's gesture — the record of a body moving through time — replaces the depicted scene. This is the ultimate reversal of medieval anonymity: the individual artist's hand is now everything.

### Connection to *Invisible Man*

<BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s novel is relentlessly self-conscious about its own surface — its language, its shifting registers, its visible artifice. The narrator's language moves between the rhetoric of sermons, jazz riffs, political oratory, naturalistic description, and surrealist hallucination; the shifts themselves are never disguised, the way Van Gogh never disguised a brushstroke as anything other than paint. There is always an awareness that someone is *telling* this story, performing it, and that the performance is part of the meaning. This is the literary equivalent of the Modernist insistence that the canvas is a canvas, the paint is paint, and the act of making is not something to be hidden behind a transparent surface. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>, like the Abstract Expressionists, makes the act of artistic creation — the energy, the improvisation, the risk — visible as content.

## Looking Back

Romanticism had already begun dissolving the Renaissance's confident, measurable space — <BioLink id="turner">Turner</BioLink>'s storms and <BioLink id="friedrich">Friedrich</BioLink>'s fog are early instances of a coherent world coming apart. Modernism completes what Romanticism started and then goes further: not content to overwhelm the rational world with sublime feeling, it dismantles the very idea that painting owes the viewer a stable, unified space at all. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s narrator, emerging from his underground room to affirm that he still has a socially responsible role to play, is reaching for something beyond the fragmentation — not a return to old certainties, but a new kind of wholeness that can acknowledge its own complexity.

## Looking Forward

There is no forward from here; this is the final period the course covers. What the whole arc has traced — from the medieval gold ground's flat certainty, through the Renaissance's rational window, the Baroque's dramatic extremes, the Enlightenment's social intelligence, and Romanticism's encounter with the sublime, to Modernism's dismantling of every inherited convention at once — is the six-century argument this site exists to make visible. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s narrator ends the novel still underground, still without answers, but unwilling to remain invisible. That refusal is where the course leaves him, and where it leaves the reader.
