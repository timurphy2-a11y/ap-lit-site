# Philosophical Context: Modernism

## Introduction

Modernism begins in wreckage. The philosophical certainties that had sustained European intellectual life for centuries — God as the guarantor of meaning, reason as the instrument of progress, the individual as a coherent self capable of knowing and governing itself — have all, by the early twentieth century, come under devastating attack. Nietzsche declares God dead. Einstein dismantles the Newtonian framework of absolute space and time. Freud reveals that the rational, self-knowing subject is a fiction: beneath the surface of consciousness lies the unconscious, a realm of repressed desires and irrational drives that shapes our behavior without our knowledge or consent. And then the First World War — industrialized slaughter on an unprecedented scale — strips away whatever remained of the Enlightenment's faith that science, reason, and civilization were leading humanity toward a better world.

The Eilenberger excerpt from *Time of the Magicians* captures the resulting crisis with precision: every philosophical school of the 1920s agreed that the Kantian foundations had been undermined. Space, time, human nature, the transparency of consciousness, the civilizing power of culture — none of these could any longer be taken for granted. Max Scheler's summary, which appears on the epigraph page of your Modernism packet, states the situation plainly: "Ours is the first period when man has become completely and totally problematical to himself, when he no longer knows what he is, but at the same time knows that he knows nothing."

This is the world of Ellison's *Invisible Man*: a world in which every system that claims to explain who you are and what you should do — racial ideology, political ideology, educational institutions, nationalist mythology — turns out to be a machine for making you invisible. The novel's narrator begins by believing in the systems and ends by rejecting them all, retreating underground to think. His journey recapitulates, in compressed and specifically American form, the philosophical trajectory of the entire modern period.

But *Invisible Man* is not only a Modernist novel in the European philosophical sense; it is a novel written from within a specifically Black American intellectual tradition. Washington and Du Bois — whose debate over the proper path for Black Americans in the decades after Reconstruction defined the terms of Black political and intellectual life in the early twentieth century — provide the immediate context for the narrator's journey. The novel begins in a world shaped by Washington's vision of accommodation and industrial education, moves through a version of Du Bois's more radical critique, and ultimately finds both insufficient. Understanding both thinkers is essential to understanding what the narrator is rejecting — and what he is trying, in his underground exile, to find.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

Every previous period had an answer. The Medieval world said: a creature of God, fixed in a hierarchy. The Renaissance said: a self-creating being of infinite potential. The Baroque said: a thinking reed, suspended between infinities. The Enlightenment said: a rational agent, capable of self-governance. Romanticism said: a feeling being, defined by imagination, passion, and the encounter with the sublime.

Modernism says: we don't know. And that not-knowing is not a temporary gap to be filled by further inquiry — it is the condition itself.

Nietzsche's "Parable of the Madman" is the foundational text of this crisis. The madman announces that God is dead — and that we have killed him. But this is not a celebration. It is a catastrophe. God, in Nietzsche's framing, was not merely a theological proposition; he was the structure that held everything together — meaning, morality, the sense that the universe was oriented toward something. Without that structure, the madman asks, "are we not straying, as through an infinite nothing? Do we not feel the breath of empty space?" The questions are rhetorical. The answer is yes. And the people in the marketplace — the casual atheists who never believed much anyway — do not understand what has happened. The madman has come too early. The event has not yet reached their ears.

Freud's *Civilization and Its Discontents* extends the crisis inward. If Nietzsche destroyed the external guarantor of meaning, Freud destroys the internal one. The self is not a unified, rational agent making free decisions — it is a battleground between competing drives, shaped by childhood experiences it cannot remember, governed by an unconscious it cannot access. And civilization, far from being the fulfillment of human potential, is a system of repression that produces neurosis as its inevitable byproduct. We are not what we think we are. The Enlightenment's confident rational subject turns out to be a comfortable fiction.

Du Bois, writing from within the American racial crisis, articulates a version of this fractured selfhood that is at once more specific and more devastating. His concept of "double consciousness" — the sense of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" — describes a condition in which the self is not merely uncertain (as in the European Modernists) but actively split by the social reality of race in America. The Black American, Du Bois argues, lives with "two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" — the desire to be both Black and American in a society that has made those identities antagonistic. Students who have read Rousseau on *amour-propre* — the corrupting compulsion to see yourself through others' eyes — will recognize something structurally similar in Du Bois, but intensified by the specific violence of racial hierarchy: the gaze through which you are forced to see yourself belongs to a society that denies your full humanity.

**Connection to *Invisible Man*:** The novel's narrator is, from the first page, a human being in crisis. "I am an invisible man," he announces — not because of any physical condition but because "people refuse to see me." Every institution he encounters — the Southern Black college, the Brotherhood, the Harlem political scene — offers him an identity, a role, a place in a system. And every one of these identities turns out to be a version of invisibility: a way of not seeing him as he actually is. Du Bois's double consciousness is the narrator's starting condition — the experience of being looked at without being seen — but Ellison pushes beyond Du Bois. The narrator discovers that even the systems designed to liberate him (the Brotherhood, Black nationalism) reproduce the same structure of invisibility. The novel dramatizes, through the specific experience of Black American life in the mid-twentieth century, the Modernist discovery that the "human position" is not a given but a problem — and that the systems designed to answer the question of who you are may be the very things that prevent you from finding out.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The Enlightenment said: reason can illuminate everything. Romanticism said: reason is necessary but insufficient; imagination reaches truths that analysis cannot. Modernism says: the instruments of knowledge themselves are broken.

Einstein's theory of relativity (1905) does not merely revise Newton; it reveals that the fundamental categories through which we understand the physical world — space and time — are not absolute but relative, dependent on the observer's frame of reference. Wittgenstein's *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1922) pushes the problem into language itself. His seven propositions attempt to draw the boundary of what can be meaningfully said — and the conclusion is devastating in its compression: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." The deepest questions — about ethics, meaning, the nature of existence — fall outside the boundary. They are not answered incorrectly; they cannot be asked at all, at least not in the language of logical propositions.

The Eilenberger passage traces how these individual disruptions — Einstein, Darwin, Freud, the war — converge into a general crisis of foundations. The Kantian system, which had provided the philosophical scaffolding for the Enlightenment and much of the nineteenth century, depended on assumptions about space, time, human nature, and rational consciousness that had all been undercut. The philosophers of the 1920s were not arguing about details; they were trying to figure out whether philosophy itself was still possible.

**Connection to *Invisible Man*:** The novel is full of characters who claim to possess knowledge — Dr. Bledsoe knows how power works, Brother Jack knows the laws of history, Ras the Exhorter knows the truth of racial identity — and every one of them is wrong, or at least dangerously partial. The narrator's education is an education in the insufficiency of every available system of knowledge. The Brotherhood's ideology, which promises to explain everything through dialectical materialism, turns out to be as blind to the narrator's actual humanity as the Southern racial ideology it claims to oppose. Ellison insists, through his narrator's painful journey, that the systems we build to make the world legible are also systems that make certain people — and certain truths — invisible.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

The Modernist period witnesses the most extreme and destructive forms of authority in human history. Totalitarianism — in its fascist and communist forms — represents the absolute subordination of the individual to the state, enforced through propaganda, surveillance, and industrialized violence. The First World War had already demonstrated what happens when the machinery of modern civilization is turned to the purpose of killing. The Holocaust reveals the ultimate consequences of a system that denies the humanity of those it designates as other. These are not abstractions; they are the historical ground on which Modernist philosophy and literature stand.

Freud's analysis in *Civilization and Its Discontents* offers one framework for understanding how this happens. Civilization requires the renunciation of instinctual drives — aggression, desire, the pursuit of pleasure — and this renunciation produces a chronic, low-grade unhappiness that is the price of social order. But Freud also suggests something darker: that the aggressive drives civilization represses do not disappear. They are redirected — toward outsiders, toward scapegoats, toward the designated enemies of the social order. The very mechanisms that make civilization possible also make atrocity possible.

Nietzsche's madman had warned that the death of God would not lead to liberation but to a new and more dangerous kind of authority: "Must we ourselves not become gods simply to appear worthy of it?" The totalitarian ideologies of the twentieth century can be read as attempts to fill the void left by God's death — to provide meaning, purpose, and structure through politics rather than religion. They demand absolute faith and absolute submission, and they punish dissent with absolute violence.

Washington and Du Bois give this thread its specifically American dimension. Washington's Atlanta Exposition Address (1895) makes a case for accommodation — for Black Americans to accept, at least temporarily, the existing structures of racial authority and to pursue economic self-improvement within them. "Cast down your bucket where you are," Washington urges. The speech is strategic, pragmatic, and deeply controversial: it asks Black Americans to defer their claims to full political and social equality in exchange for economic opportunity and white goodwill. Du Bois's response, in the opening chapter of *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), rejects this bargain. He argues that Washington's program demands a psychic self-mutilation — the surrender of the very aspirations (higher education, political rights, civic dignity) that make full human flourishing possible. The debate between them is not merely political; it is a debate about the individual's proper relationship to an unjust authority: do you work within the system and hope to transform it gradually, or do you insist on your full humanity even when the system is designed to deny it?

**Connection to *Invisible Man*:** Every authority the narrator encounters demands that he subordinate his individual identity to a collective project. The college president, Dr. Bledsoe — a figure who echoes Washington's strategy of accommodation — demands obedience to the institution and the performance of racial deference. Bledsoe has mastered the art of appearing to submit while actually wielding enormous power behind the scenes; he is Washington's pragmatism pushed to its cynical extreme. The Brotherhood demands obedience to ideology and the sacrifice of individual perception to "the discipline" — a different kind of authority, ostensibly liberatory, but equally insistent that the narrator become a function rather than a person. Ras the Exhorter demands obedience to racial solidarity and the rejection of any identity that does not serve the cause. The novel does not treat these authorities as equivalent — some are more sympathetic than others, and the specific dynamics of American racial politics are not interchangeable with European totalitarianism — but it insists that all of them share a common operation: they render the individual invisible by insisting that he become a symbol, a function, or a tool. The narrator's decision to go underground — to refuse all available roles — is an act of Modernist resistance: the insistence on existing as a particular, irreducible self in a world that has no place for such a thing.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From Romanticism:** Romanticism celebrated the individual imagination as the highest human faculty — the poet as visionary, the self as unique and irreducible. Modernism inherits this intensity but discovers that the self is far more complicated, more fractured, and more vulnerable than the Romantics imagined. Emerson's transparent eyeball, merging ecstatically with nature, becomes Ellison's invisible man, unseen by a society that looks right through him. Ahab's Romantic obsession — the individual will hurled against an inscrutable universe — anticipates the Modernist condition, but Ahab at least had a whale to chase. The Modernist individual confronts something worse: systems so pervasive and abstract that there is nothing to harpoon.

**The arc of the course:** Standing at the end of this five-unit journey, students can look back and trace the full trajectory of each thread. The human position moves from medieval certainty (a creature of God in a fixed hierarchy) through Renaissance freedom, Baroque vertigo, Enlightenment confidence, and Romantic intensity to Modernist crisis. Knowledge moves from revelation to empiricism to the limits of reason to the primacy of imagination to the collapse of foundations. The individual's relationship to authority moves from submission to defiance to surrender to consent to rebellion to — in Ellison's narrator — a wary, hard-won, provisional autonomy. The narrator ends underground, alone, thinking. He has not solved anything. But he has refused to be invisible. That refusal is, in the context of the course's long arc, an act of extraordinary courage — a single individual insisting on his own existence against the weight of every system that would deny it.
