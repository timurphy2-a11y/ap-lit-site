# Philosophical Context: Modernism

## Introduction

Modernism begins in wreckage. The philosophical certainties that had sustained European intellectual life for centuries — God as the guarantor of meaning, reason as the instrument of progress, the individual as a coherent self capable of knowing and governing itself — have all, by the early twentieth century, come under devastating attack. <BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink> declares God dead. Einstein dismantles the Newtonian framework of absolute space and time. <BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink> reveals that the rational, self-knowing subject is a fiction: beneath the surface of consciousness lies the unconscious, a realm of repressed desires and irrational drives that shapes our behavior without our knowledge or consent. The First World War, bringing industrialized slaughter on an unprecedented scale, strips away whatever remained of the Enlightenment's faith that science, reason, and civilization were leading humanity toward a better world.

The Eilenberger excerpt from *Time of the Magicians* captures the resulting crisis with precision: every philosophical school of the 1920s agreed that the Kantian foundations had been undermined. Space, time, human nature, the transparency of consciousness, the civilizing power of culture — none of these could any longer be taken for granted. Max Scheler's summary states the situation plainly: "Ours is the first period when man has become completely and totally problematical to himself, when he no longer knows what he is, but at the same time knows that he knows nothing."

This crisis is the basic intellectual context of <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s *Invisible Man*: a world in which every explanatory system (racial and political ideology, educational institutions, nationalist mythology) has been revealed as machinery for the production of invisibility. The journey of the novel's narrator recapitulates, in compressed and specifically American form, the philosophical trajectory of the entire modern period.

Yet *Invisible Man* is not only a Modernist novel in the European philosophical sense; it is a novel deliberately situated within a specifically Black American intellectual tradition. <BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink> and <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink>, whose debate over the proper path for Black Americans in the decades after Reconstruction defined the terms of Black political and intellectual life in the early twentieth century, provide the immediate context for the narrator's journey. The novel begins in a world shaped by <BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink>'s vision of accommodation and industrial education, moves through an exploration of <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink>'s more radical critique, and ultimately finds both insufficient. Understanding both thinkers is essential to understanding the intellectual heritage that the narrator is rejecting with his withdrawal into underground exile.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

Every previous period had a stable response to this question. The Medieval world answered: a creature of God, fixed in a hierarchy; the Renaissance, a self-creating being of infinite potential. The Baroque replied: a thinking reed, suspended between infinities. The Enlightenment responded: a rational agent, capable of self-governance. Romanticism countered: a feeling being, defined by imagination, passion, and the encounter with the sublime.

Modernism says plainly: we don't know. That "not-knowing" is not a temporary gap to be filled by further inquiry — it is the condition itself.

<BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink>'s "Parable of the Madman" announces this crisis. The madman runs into the marketplace declaring that God is dead — and that *we* have killed him. This is not atheist triumphalism. It is a diagnosis of catastrophe. God, in <BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink>'s framing, was not merely a theological proposition; he was the organizing structure of the whole Western world — the ground of meaning, morality, and the conviction that the universe was oriented toward something. Without that ground, the madman asks, "are we not straying, as through an infinite nothing?" But Nietzsche's madman does not stop at demolition. In the same breath, he demands creation: new festivals of atonement must be invented, new sacred games. The destruction of the old order carries an obligation to build something in its place. This double movement — catastrophic loss and anguished creation — is the Modernist cultural impulse in miniature.

<BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink>'s *Civilization and Its Discontents* extends the crisis inward. If <BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink> destroyed the external guarantor of meaning, <BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink> devastates the internal one. The self is not a unified, rational agent; it is a battleground between competing drives, shaped by childhood experiences it cannot remember, governed by an unconscious it cannot access. The Enlightenment's confident rational subject turns out to be a comfortable fiction.

<BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink>, writing from within the experience of racialized otherness in America, articulates a version of this fractured selfhood that is more specific and more devastating. His concept of "double consciousness" — the sense of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" — describes a condition in which the self is not merely uncertain but actively split by the social reality of race in America. Students who have read <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> on *amour-propre* will recognize something structurally similar in <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink>, intensified by the specific violence of racial hierarchy. The White majority gaze through which Black Americans are forced to see themselves belongs to a society that denies their full humanity.

<PullQuote attribution="W.E.B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk, 1903">
  One ever feels his two-ness — an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder.
</PullQuote>

**Connection to *Invisible Man*:** "I am an invisible man," the narrator announces — not because of any physical condition but because "people refuse to see me." Every institution he encounters — the college, the Brotherhood, Harlem politics — offers him an identity, and every one turns out to be a version of invisibility: a way of not seeing him as he actually is. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink> pushes beyond <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink>. The narrator discovers that even the systems designed to liberate him reproduce the same structure of invisibility.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

If the Enlightenment asserted that reason can illuminate everything, Modernism replies with the revelation that the instruments of knowledge themselves are broken.

Einstein's theory of relativity (1905) does not merely revise <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink>; it reveals that the fundamental categories through which we understand the physical world — space and time — are not absolute but relative, dependent on the observer's frame of reference. <BioLink id="wittgenstein">Wittgenstein</BioLink>'s *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1922) pushes the problem into language itself. His seven propositions attempt to draw the boundary of what can be meaningfully said — and the conclusion is devastating: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." The deepest questions — about ethics, meaning, the nature of existence — fall outside the boundary. They cannot be asked at all, at least not in the language of logical propositions.

The Eilenberger passage traces how these individual disruptions — Einstein, Darwin, <BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink>, the war — converge into a general crisis of foundations. The Kantian system, which had provided the philosophical scaffolding for the Enlightenment and much of the nineteenth century, depended on assumptions about space, time, human nature, and rational consciousness that had all been undercut. The philosophers of the 1920s were trying to figure out whether philosophy itself was still possible.

**Connection to *Invisible Man*:** The novel is full of characters claiming to possess knowledge — Dr. Bledsoe knows how power works, Brother Jack knows the laws of history, Ras the Exhorter knows the truth of racial identity. Every one of them is wrong, or at least dangerously partial. The Brotherhood's ideology, which promises to explain everything, turns out to be as blind to the narrator's humanity as the Southern racial ideology it claims to oppose.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

The Modernist period witnesses the most extreme and destructive forms of authority in human history. Totalitarianism — in its fascist and communist forms — represents the absolute subordination of the individual to the state. The First World War demonstrates the destructive power of the machinery of modern civilization turned to the purpose of killing. The enormity of the Holocaust reveals the ultimate consequences of a system that weaponizes antisemitism to justify mass murder on an unimaginable scale.

<BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink>'s analysis offers one framework for understanding how this happens. Civilization requires the renunciation of instinctual drives, and this renunciation produces a chronic unhappiness that is the price of social order. But the aggressive drives civilization represses do not disappear. They are redirected — toward outsiders, toward scapegoats, toward the designated enemies of the social order. The very mechanisms that make civilization possible also open the door to atrocity.

<BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink>'s madman had warned that the death of God would not lead to liberation but to a new and more dangerous kind of authority. The totalitarian ideologies of the twentieth century can be read as attempts to fill the void left by God's death — to provide meaning, purpose, and structure through politics rather than religion.

<BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink> and <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink> give this thread its specifically American dimension. <BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink>'s Atlanta Exposition Address (1895) makes a case for accommodation — for Black Americans to accept, at least temporarily, the existing structures of racial authority and to pursue economic self-improvement within them. "Cast down your bucket where you are," <BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink> urges. The speech is strategic, pragmatic, and deeply controversial: it asks Black Americans to defer their claims to full political and social equality in exchange for economic opportunity and white goodwill. <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink>'s response, in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), rejects this bargain. He argues that <BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink>'s program demands a psychic self-mutilation — the surrender of the very aspirations that make full human flourishing possible. The debate between them is not merely political; it is a debate about the individual's proper relationship to an unjust authority.

**Connection to *Invisible Man*:** Every authority the narrator encounters demands that he subordinate his individual identity to a collective project. Bledsoe — <BioLink id="washington">Washington</BioLink>'s pragmatism pushed to its cynical extreme — demands obedience to the institution and the performance of racial deference. The Brotherhood demands obedience to ideology. Ras demands obedience to racial solidarity. The novel insists that all of them share a common operation: they render the individual invisible by insisting that he become a symbol, a function, or a tool. The narrator's decision to go underground is an act of Modernist resistance: the insistence on existing as a particular, irreducible self in a world that has no place for such a thing.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From Romanticism:** Romanticism celebrated the individual imagination as the highest human faculty. Modernism inherits this intensity but discovers that the self is far more complicated, more fractured, and more vulnerable than the Romantics imagined. <BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink>'s transparent eyeball, merging ecstatically with nature, becomes <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s invisible man, unseen by a society that looks right through him. Ahab's Romantic obsession anticipates the Modernist condition, but Ahab at least had a whale to chase. The Modernist individual confronts something worse: systems so pervasive and abstract that there is nothing to harpoon.

**The arc of the course:** The human position moves from medieval certainty through Renaissance freedom, Baroque vertigo, Enlightenment confidence, and Romantic intensity to Modernist crisis. Knowledge moves from revelation to empiricism to the limits of reason to the primacy of imagination to the collapse of foundations. The individual's relationship to authority moves from submission to defiance to surrender to consent to rebellion to — in <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s narrator — a wary, hard-won, provisional autonomy. The narrator ends underground, alone, thinking. He has not solved anything. But he has refused to be invisible.
