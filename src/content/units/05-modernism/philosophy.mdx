# Philosophical Context: Modernism

## Introduction

Modernism develops amid broken institutions and competing explanations. <BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink> questions the source of value after the decline of religious authority. <BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink> divides the self into conscious and unconscious processes. <BioLink id="einstein">Einstein</BioLink> and <BioLink id="godel">Gödel</BioLink> revise assumptions about physical measurement and formal proof. <BioLink id="wittgenstein">Wittgenstein</BioLink> tests the boundary between meaningful propositions and the problems language cannot settle. <BioLink id="sartre">Sartre</BioLink> and <BioLink id="camus">Camus</BioLink> ask what freedom and responsibility remain when no external authority guarantees a human purpose. <BioLink id="dubois">Du Bois</BioLink> examines a self forced to see itself through the contemptuous judgments of a racial order.

*Invisible Man* places these problems inside American history. Its narrator meets educators, employers, doctors, political organizers, nationalists, and opportunists who claim to know what he is. Their explanations contain partial truths, yet each becomes destructive when it substitutes a type for a person. The narrator's task is larger than rejecting false ideas. He must acknowledge the facts of his history without letting those facts become an essence imposed by others; he must exercise freedom without pretending that freedom erases race, power, or obligation.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being when no inherited description commands general agreement?*

<BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink>'s madman announces that "God is dead" to people who do not yet understand the consequences. The declaration describes a cultural event rather than a biological or metaphysical report. Christian moral language continues after belief in its divine foundation weakens, but inherited values can no longer justify themselves by invoking an authority everyone accepts. The madman therefore asks who human beings must become to prove worthy of what they have done.

Nietzsche's problem concerns responsibility for creating and testing values. It does not establish a direct path from secularization to fascism, and it does not predict one inevitable political result. Modern movements would later appropriate Nietzsche selectively and distort his work. His diagnosis remains central because it removes the comfort of believing that an inherited moral vocabulary can carry authority without continued judgment.

<BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink> destabilizes the sovereign self from within. Dreams, symptoms, slips of the tongue, repression, and repetition suggest that conscious reasons do not exhaust the motives shaping action. Psychoanalysis claims indirect access to unconscious conflict through interpretation; it does not simply declare the mind unknowable. The self becomes a negotiated structure in which desire, prohibition, memory, aggression, and social expectation remain in tension.

<BioLink id="sartre">Jean-Paul Sartre</BioLink> rejects the idea that a human being possesses a fixed essence that determines every action. Human existence combines **facticity**, the conditions already true of a person, with **transcendence**, the capacity to project possibilities and act beyond an established description. Facticity includes a body, past actions, social position, history, and circumstances that cannot be wished away. Transcendence does not erase those conditions; it names the freedom exercised within them.

**Bad faith** occurs when a person falsifies that relation. Sartre's waiter performs his role with exaggerated precision, behaving as though "waiter" were his complete being rather than an occupation. The error lies neither in working nor in inhabiting a social role. It lies in pretending that the role removes freedom and responsibility. The opposite distortion treats freedom as unlimited and refuses the facts that make any action situated.

The distinction requires special care in a novel about racism. The narrator does not freely invent the racial order that classifies and endangers him. Racism is part of his facticity: a material structure of law, custom, violence, economic power, and other people's perception. His freedom consists neither in denying Black identity nor in transcending history through will. He must act without accepting any imposed racial script as the whole truth of his person.

Sartre's account of **the Look** examines the moment when another person's perception turns someone into an object. A man peering through a keyhole remains absorbed in what he sees until he hears someone behind him. He suddenly experiences himself as the other person sees him: exposed and fixed by a judgment he cannot control.

Sartre later described the young Jean Genet caught stealing and named a thief as being "pinned like a butterfly" to an identity. The label converts one act into an alleged essence. Genet's later choices cannot be reduced to that accusation, but the image captures the force of being assigned a self by another person's word.

<BioLink id="dubois">W. E. B. Du Bois</BioLink> had described a historically specific and more sustained form of divided self-consciousness in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903):

<PullQuote attribution="W. E. B. Du Bois · 'The Souls of Black Folk' · 1903">
It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity.
</PullQuote>

Du Bois and Sartre should remain distinct. Sartre analyzes a general structure of being seen by another consciousness. Du Bois describes the historical pressure of a racial order that repeatedly makes Black Americans measure themselves through a hostile public gaze. Rousseau's *amour-propre* remains a useful earlier comparison because it shows identity becoming dependent upon social judgment, but Du Bois gives the problem its racial, national, and political form.

### Connection to *Invisible Man*

The narrator is often watched, displayed, and interpreted, yet rarely recognized as a person. The Battle Royal makes him hypervisible as entertainment while disregarding his pain and ambition. The college values him as the image of a grateful student. The Brotherhood renames him and converts his voice into an instrument of doctrine. Ras demands that racial loyalty determine his political identity. Rinehart appears to escape every fixed role, but his endless disguises make identity interchangeable rather than free.

Invisibility and hypervisibility become related conditions: other people can stare at a racial type while failing to see the individual before them. The narrator's underground withdrawal interrupts that process, but it does not complete his freedom. His hibernation gives him time to narrate and judge his experience; the epilogue leaves open whether he can return to public life without surrendering to another identity prepared in advance.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*What can human beings know when observation, language, and formal systems possess internal limits?*

<BioLink id="einstein">Einstein</BioLink>'s special theory of relativity revises measurements of distance, duration, and simultaneity. Observers moving relative to one another may assign different spatial and temporal measurements to the same events. Relativity does not mean that every account is equally true or that physical law depends upon personal opinion. Its central principles are invariant: the laws of physics take the same form for inertial observers, and the speed of light in a vacuum remains constant.

This combination of changed perspective and invariant structure is more useful than the familiar slogan that "everything is relative." Einstein transformed classical physics while retaining a commitment to a lawful and intelligible universe. He later resisted interpretations of quantum mechanics that appeared to make physical reality fundamentally indeterminate.

<BioLink id="wittgenstein">Ludwig Wittgenstein</BioLink> pursued limits within language. The *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* seeks the logical form that allows propositions to picture facts. Its final proposition states:

<PullQuote attribution="Ludwig Wittgenstein · 'Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus' · 1921">
Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.
</PullQuote>

The sentence marks a boundary around factual propositions, leaving ethics, value, and the meaning of life outside what the book believes logic can state. Wittgenstein then compares his own propositions to a ladder that must be discarded after it has been climbed. His later philosophy abandons much of the *Tractatus*'s framework and studies the varied uses of language within forms of life. The philosopher who drew a single boundary concluded that language works through practices more diverse than his first system allowed.

<BioLink id="godel">Kurt Gödel</BioLink> establishes a different limit. His first incompleteness theorem applies to consistent, effectively axiomatized formal systems strong enough to express elementary arithmetic. Such a system contains statements that cannot be proved or disproved within it. His second theorem shows, under related conditions, that the system cannot prove its own consistency.

Gödel's result concerns mathematical proof, not political organizations or every form of human knowledge. Its application to *Invisible Man* must remain an analogy. The novel's institutions resemble formal systems only in a limited respect: each uses its own assumptions to explain the world and has difficulty recognizing what those assumptions exclude. Gödel supplies no theorem about Bledsoe or the Brotherhood; he supplies a precise twentieth-century example of completeness failing in the domain where it had seemed most attainable.

<BioLink id="camus">Albert Camus</BioLink> gives the problem of knowledge an existential form. The **absurd** arises from the confrontation between the human demand for intelligibility, justice, and purpose and a world that does not answer that demand. The absurd belongs neither to the mind nor to the world alone; it exists in their relation. Camus therefore refuses to treat the absence of guaranteed meaning as a proof that life lacks value.

In *The Myth of Sisyphus*, a condemned figure pushes a stone up a hill while knowing that it will fall again. Camus locates dignity in lucid persistence: Sisyphus acts without a promise that the universe will reward or complete his labor. At the end of *The Stranger*, Meursault opens himself to "the benign indifference of the universe." Camus's revolt preserves consciousness and action without inventing a cosmic explanation.

<BioLink id="eilenberger">Wolfram Eilenberger</BioLink>'s *Time of the Magicians* describes philosophy after World War I as a search for foundations conducted by thinkers who no longer agreed about what a foundation could be. The result was neither a common doctrine nor a general surrender of knowledge. Philosophers reconstructed logic, language, experience, history, and value through competing methods.

### Connection to *Invisible Man*

Bledsoe, the factory doctors, Brother Jack, and Ras do not lack explanations. Each possesses a vocabulary that determines in advance what counts as evidence and which human purposes matter. Their failure comes from explanatory closure. Facts that do not fit become errors, betrayals, or material to be manipulated.

The narrator's underground room contains 1,369 electric lights powered by electricity he has diverted from Monopolated Light and Power. The excess of illumination does not produce a final theory. It gives him the material condition for writing, remembering, and revising the sequence of explanations he once accepted. Like Camus's absurd figure, he acts without receiving a guarantee that his account will reconcile him to the world. Like the Gödel analogy, his narration searches for what each system could not state in its own terms.

## The Individual and Authority

*Why do people submit to institutions, and what forms of responsibility survive inside them?*

The twentieth century produced fascist and Communist regimes that joined mass participation to surveillance, propaganda, terror, and concentrated state power. Their rise cannot be explained by Nietzsche's secularization or Freud's theory of aggression alone. Economic crisis, war, imperial ambition, racial ideology, party organization, administrative systems, and political choices all mattered. Freud's *Civilization and Its Discontents* contributes one psychological account: social order redirects aggression and desire without eliminating them, producing both collective life and recurring discontent.

<BioLink id="arendt">Hannah Arendt</BioLink> analyzes totalitarianism as a political form that exploits isolation and uprootedness. Modern mass society can leave people without durable civic relationships or a secure place in a common world. Ideological movements promise belonging, historical purpose, and a complete explanation. Terror remains essential, but domination also recruits people who want the movement's identity and certainty.

Arendt's later account of the banality of evil shifts attention from demonic motive to failures of thought and judgment. Adolf Eichmann helped organize the deportation of Jews to ghettos and killing centers through administrative work. Arendt's controversial interpretation described a functionary whose clichés and careerism prevented serious judgment about what he was doing. "Banality" does not make the crime ordinary or innocent. It identifies the danger of performing destructive work through routine procedure while refusing personal responsibility.

Arendt's positive alternative is public action among plural persons who appear before one another as speakers and agents. Freedom requires a shared world in which people can judge, disagree, make promises, and begin something new. Private reflection can prepare such action, but permanent withdrawal cannot replace it.

Black American debates about authority developed under different political conditions. <BioLink id="washington">Booker T. Washington</BioLink>'s 1895 Atlanta Exposition address proposed economic advancement, industrial education, and strategic accommodation within segregation. Du Bois challenged that program's acceptance of disfranchisement and unequal civil status, arguing for higher education, political rights, and organized protest. Their disagreement concerned strategy, leadership, education, class, and the terms on which Black citizenship could be claimed.

*Invisible Man* engages both legacies without treating them as equal versions of the same mistake. The Founder and the college evoke Washington and Tuskegee, but Dr. Bledsoe should not be identified with Washington himself. Bledsoe converts institutional leadership into personal domination, protects his standing with white benefactors, and destroys the narrator's future to preserve an image of order. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s satire tests what can happen when an institution's public mission becomes inseparable from the leader's private power.

The Brotherhood provides a different form of authority. After expulsion, industrial injury, medical violence, and unemployment, the narrator is isolated in a city that does not know him. The organization gives him a name, salary, apartment, discipline, audience, and collective "we." Arendt's analysis clarifies the attraction of ideological belonging and the danger of surrendering judgment to a theory that claims to know history's direction.

The comparison has limits. The Brotherhood is a fictional American organization shaped by Ellison's engagement with the Communist left; it is not equivalent to a totalitarian state. Its control operates through hierarchy, doctrine, racial condescension, reassignment, and the treatment of Harlem as material for strategy. Tod Clifton's death does not function as an "occasional exercise of totalitarian violence" by the Brotherhood. His killing by a police officer exposes both state violence and the organization's inability to value the person whom its theory had converted into a function.

Ras the Exhorter recognizes the Brotherhood's racial blindness and the persistence of white power. His critique contains truths the Brotherhood suppresses, but his nationalism hardens race into destiny and turns political opposition toward violence. The narrator moves between organizations that identify real injuries while demanding complete allegiance to one interpretation.

Sartre's bad faith clarifies the personal temptation inside these institutions. Bledsoe treats his office as permission to do whatever preserves the office. Brother Jack treats doctrine as if it acts through him and removes the need for judgment. The narrator repeatedly speaks through roles that promise significance. Existential freedom does not mean that he can escape consequences at will; it means that no title, command, or theory can assume responsibility in his place.

### Connection to *Invisible Man*

The underground room suspends the narrator's participation in public institutions. It allows him to examine the roles he accepted and the injuries those roles concealed. Withdrawal remains provisional. In the epilogue, he considers the social responsibility of returning to the world and asks whether his voice may speak on "the lower frequencies" for others. The novel closes before reflection becomes action.

Arendt and Sartre illuminate different sides of that unresolved ending. Sartre insists that refusal is still a choice for which the narrator remains responsible. Arendt insists that freedom becomes political when a person appears among others, speaks, judges, and acts within a plural world. Ellison adds the historical difficulty each formulation must face: appearing in public does not guarantee that a Black speaker will be seen as the person who has appeared.

## Looking back: Inherited questions under new pressure

Modernism does not replace a sequence of settled answers with uncertainty. Earlier periods had already contested authority, selfhood, knowledge, freedom, providence, and social order. Medieval thinkers disputed the relation between reason and revelation. Renaissance self-fashioning remained constrained by rank, religion, gender, and patronage. Baroque writers made instability and hiddenness central problems. Enlightenment thinkers disagreed about reason, sympathy, empire, and inequality. Romantic writers tested the powers and dangers of inwardness, imagination, and will.

Modernism inherits those unresolved arguments under conditions that change their scale. Industrial war, racial classification, mass politics, psychoanalysis, relativity, formal logic, and new literary forms make the coherent person and comprehensive system harder to sustain. The result is a plurality of reconstructions rather than one Modernist creed.

## Looking forward: From hibernation to responsibility

The questions opened here continue through decolonization, the civil rights movement, Black existentialism, feminism, postcolonial theory, and later critiques of universal reason. Who has been permitted to represent "the human"? When does a universal claim conceal the experience of the group with the power to define it? How can people act collectively without making difference disappear?

Ellison leaves those questions in motion. The narrator has learned to distrust identities assigned by institutions, but he cannot live indefinitely through refusal. His final address reaches beyond solitary authenticity toward democratic relation: a voice formed by one history may carry meanings that other people can hear without becoming identical to him.
