# Philosophical Context: The High Middle Ages

## Introduction

For many thinkers in Latin Christian Europe, human freedom operated within an order that human beings had not created. God established the universe, gave creatures their natures, and revealed the final purpose of human life. Human reason could investigate that order, but reason did not stand outside it. Political and ecclesiastical authority also claimed a place within divine government, although medieval writers disputed the role and limits of earthly authority.

*Hamlet* inherits these assumptions in unstable form. The ghost invokes purgatory, divine judgment, kingship, and filial duty; Hamlet responds with doubt, testing, and delay. The play repeatedly asks whether inherited structures still deserve belief when they no longer produce certainty.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do human beings stand in the order of things?*

<div class="plate-pair left">
<div class="pair-text">

Later scholars use **Great Chain of Being** to describe a family of hierarchical ideas that medieval and early modern thinkers inherited from Plato, Aristotle, and Neoplatonism. God stands beyond creation; angels possess spirit without matter; human beings join rational soul to mortal body; animals, plants, and minerals occupy progressively lower forms of life and matter. Medieval writers did not present one universally fixed diagram under this name, but many understood creation as graded, purposeful, and complete. Human dignity came from creation in God's image; human limitation came from sin, mortality, and dependence on grace.

The Venerable <BioLink id="bede">Bede</BioLink>'s *Ecclesiastical History* gives that position a more fragile form. Recounting King Edwin of Northumbria's council on conversion, Bede has one counselor compare human life to a sparrow that crosses a lit hall in winter and passes out the far door. The hierarchy names where a human being stands; the sparrow measures how briefly anyone stands there.

<BioLink id="boethius">Boethius</BioLink>'s *Consolation of Philosophy* addresses the same vulnerability through the **Wheel of Fortune**. Boethius had served at the summit of government under Theodoric before an accusation of treason led to imprisonment and execution. In the dialogue he wrote while awaiting death, Lady Philosophy tells him that Fortune's nature is to turn. Wealth, office, reputation, health, and security occupy unstable positions on her wheel; wisdom begins by refusing to treat temporary possession as permanent right.

Hamlet invokes this tradition when he asks the Player to recite the speech that cries, "Out, out, thou strumpet Fortune" and calls on the gods to "break all the spokes and fellies from her wheel." Boethius teaches acceptance of Fortune's turning; the speech Hamlet chooses demands that the wheel be destroyed in a cry of anguished frustration at Fortune's injustice.

<BioLink id="augustine">St. Augustine</BioLink>'s *Confessions* moves the problem inward. Trapped in what he calls a "cauldron of unholy loves," Augustine intellectually recognizes the truth of God, but he cannot make his will follow it. His habits and desires pull against grace until the command "take up and read" directs him to Scripture. Continence tells him, "Cast yourself fearlessly on Him. He will not pull back and let you fall." Augustine's freedom reaches completion through utter dependence on God.

### Connection to *Hamlet*

In the graveyard, Hamlet performs a memento mori with Yorick's skull. He follows Alexander and Caesar into clay, stopping at physical decay without offering the expected consolation of resurrection or heaven. Laertes later imagines Ophelia as a "ministering angel," and Hamlet soon accepts that "there is special providence in the fall of a sparrow." The sequence moves from the body's reduction to dust toward an incomplete return to providence. Bede's sparrow passes through the hall because a religious teaching promises knowledge beyond its doors; Hamlet reaches the edge of that promise without recovering its certainty.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do human beings know what they know, and how far can knowledge take them?*

Medieval Latin philosophy drew upon **revelation**, truths disclosed through Scripture and Christian teaching, and **authority**, arguments inherited from writers whose work had survived. **Scholasticism** tested the relations among those authorities through formal questions, objections, replies, and distinctions. <BioLink id="aquinas">Aquinas</BioLink>'s *Summa Theologiae* exemplifies the method: each article states a problem, presents the strongest objections, gives a counterposition, and answers the objections individually.

The authorities themselves came through several intellectual cultures. From the twelfth century onward, translators working in places including Toledo rendered Arabic versions of Greek texts and Arabic philosophical works into Latin. Avicenna and Averroes shaped European readings of Aristotle; Maimonides influenced Aquinas and later Christian thinkers. Latin scholasticism developed through this multilingual inheritance.

*The Cloud of Unknowing* places a limit on discursive reason. Its anonymous author argues that the intellect cannot comprehend God's essence; love can direct the soul toward a reality that concepts cannot contain. "Leave your intellectual and your imaginative skills strictly alone," the author instructs. The "cloud" names the boundary at which analysis gives way to contemplative practice.

<PullQuote attribution="The Cloud of Unknowing, c. 1375">
  God is always quite unable to be comprehended by the first faculty, that of intelligence, but he is totally and perfectly comprehensible by the second, the power of love.
</PullQuote>

<BioLink id="adelard">Adelard of Bath</BioLink>'s *Natural Questions* demonstrates another form of medieval reasoning. Adelard asks how a spherical earth can remain suspended in space and answers through Aristotelian principles: each element moves toward its natural place, and heavy matter seeks the universe's center. The argument proceeds logically from premises that define a finite, geocentric cosmos. The companion summary of Aristotelian physics makes those premises explicit: four terrestrial elements, incorruptible celestial ether, and a division between changing earth and perfect heavens.

<div class="plate-pair left vcenter">
<div class="pair-text">

<BioLink id="alhaytham">Ibn al-Haytham</BioLink>, working in Cairo during the eleventh century, joined mathematical reasoning to controlled observation in his studies of optics. He instructed investigators to challenge what they read and to suspect their own judgment while doing so. His work demonstrates that systematic observation did not begin in Renaissance Europe; later Latin science developed partly through methods and texts produced in the Islamic world.

<BioLink id="ockham">William of Ockham</BioLink> exposed a fracture inside the Christian synthesis. Aquinas's **natural theology** reasoned from creation toward knowledge of its Creator. Ockham emphasized God's absolute freedom: God could have created an order radically different from the one human beings observe. Creation therefore cannot disclose God's nature as securely as Aquinas proposed. A theologian defending divine omnipotence helped separate the study of nature from claims about God two centuries before Galileo.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern social and spiritual life?*

Medieval resistance usually appealed to a higher authority: Scripture, divine law, the proper order of the Church, or a ruler's failure to fulfill a divinely sanctioned office. <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> presents surrender to God as liberation from a divided will, yet submission to divine authority could authorize resistance to human officials.

<div class="plate-pair right vcenter">
<div class="pair-text">

<BioLink id="hildegard">Hildegard of Bingen</BioLink> repeatedly used visionary authority to confront ecclesiastical superiors. When church officials demanded that an allegedly excommunicated man be exhumed from her community's graveyard, Hildegard insisted that he had died reconciled to the Church. She then removed the grave's identifying marks so that officials could not find the body. Her defiance did not reject religious authority; it rested on her claim to understand divine and sacramental truth more accurately than the officials who opposed her.

John Wycliffe argued that Scripture possessed greater authority than office and that a churchman living in mortal sin could not exercise legitimate spiritual power merely by holding a title. During the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, John Ball used the old question "When Adam delved and Eve span, who then was the gentleman?" to challenge hereditary rank through the equality of humanity before the Fall. Medieval hierarchy generated arguments about the conditions under which hierarchy remained legitimate.

### Connection to *Hamlet*

The ghost demands revenge through several forms of authority at once: the claim of a father upon a son, a murdered king upon a subject, and a suffering soul upon the living. Other obligations pull against the command. Christian teaching restricts private vengeance; Claudius possesses the public authority of a crowned king; the ghost's spiritual identity remains uncertain. Even after the play-within-the-play strengthens the accusation against Claudius, it does not determine whether private vengeance is lawful.

Hamlet's delay therefore cannot be reduced to a Renaissance mind refusing a command that a medieval mind would automatically obey. The play stages a collision among inherited duties that no accepted institution can adjudicate. Hamlet must decide which authority speaks legitimately before he can decide what obedience requires.

## Looking Forward: The Full Arc

*Hamlet* records the failure of inherited structures to align belief, duty, and action. A ghost speaks from a disputed afterlife; the prince tests revelation through theater; the graveyard reduces Alexander and Caesar to dust; the final act returns uncertainly to providence. The play replaces neither belief with skepticism nor obedience with autonomy. Hamlet moves between those positions without securing either one.

Each thread continues past the period. <BioLink id="pico">Pico della Mirandola</BioLink> preserves the scale from matter to divinity in 1486, but he also gives the human creature freedom to move within it, turning an assigned place into a field of moral self-creation. <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink> and other early modern investigators intensify the demand for demonstration and mathematical proof, inheriting older practices of argument while rejecting particular authorities and cosmological premises. <BioLink id="luther">Luther</BioLink> appeals to Scripture and conscience against ecclesiastical office and uses print to carry a local dispute to a wide public, turning conflicts over legitimate Christian authority into competing churches, confessions, and political settlements.
