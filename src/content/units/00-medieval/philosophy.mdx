# Philosophical Context: The High Middle Ages

## Introduction

This unit does not have a core literary text. Its purpose is different: it establishes the intellectual world that everything else in this course reacts against, revises, or struggles to leave behind. To understand what the Renaissance is doing, you need to know what it was departing from. To understand why <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> trembles between two infinities, you need to know the ordered, finite cosmos he has lost. To understand why <BioLink id="nietzsche">Nietzsche</BioLink>'s madman says God is dead, you need to understand the world in which God was alive.

The medieval intellectual world is often caricatured as simply ignorant — the "Dark Ages," a period of superstition before science arrived. This is deeply unfair. Medieval thinkers were sophisticated, rigorous, and working within a framework that was internally coherent and, on its own terms, profoundly beautiful. That framework rested on a few core commitments: that the universe was created and governed by God; that human beings occupied a specific place in a divinely ordered hierarchy; that the deepest truths were accessible through revelation and faith rather than through unaided reason; and that the proper posture of the human soul was one of obedience, humility, and surrender to a reality that exceeded its comprehension.

As you read, notice that these commitments do not produce a single, uniform worldview. <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> agonizes over his inability to surrender; the author of *The Cloud of Unknowing* insists that God can only be reached through a kind of radical intellectual darkness; Adelard of Bath reasons his way through a question about gravity in a way that looks surprisingly like early science. The medieval world contained more variety and more tension than it is usually given credit for. But the foundational assumptions — God at the center, humanity in its place, knowledge from above — remain consistent, and they are precisely what the Renaissance will begin to challenge.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

The medieval answer is clear, and it is given not as a philosophical argument but as a description of reality: human beings are creatures of God, made in his image but fallen through sin, positioned in a Great Chain of Being that extends from God and the angels at the top through humanity in the middle down to animals, plants, and inanimate matter at the bottom. Your place in this chain is not something you chose or can change. It is given. Your task is not to reinvent yourself (as <BioLink id="pico">Pico</BioLink> will later propose) but to fulfill the role you have been assigned — to live in accordance with your nature and, through faith and obedience, to work toward salvation.

<BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink>'s *Confessions* dramatizes what this looks like from the inside. <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> does not describe a process of self-discovery in the modern sense — he is not trying to figure out who he is. He already knows who he is: a sinner who needs God. The drama of the *Confessions* is the drama of surrender — the agonizing difficulty of submitting his will to a truth he already recognizes. His inner conflict is not between competing identities or values (as it might be for a modern character) but between his fallen nature, which clings to worldly pleasures, and grace, which calls him toward God. "Cast yourself fearlessly on him," Continence tells him. "He will not pull back and let you fall." The human position, in <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink>'s account, is one of radical dependence.

The anonymous ballad "The Unquiet Grave" captures the medieval sense of the human position in a different register — not theological but emotional. The living lover sits on the grave of the dead beloved, refusing to let go. The dead woman speaks from the grave to tell him that his grief is futile: "The stalk is withered dry, my love, / So will our hearts decay; / So make yourself content, my love, / Till God calls you away." Human life is transient. The proper response to that transience is not rebellion or despair but acceptance — content yourself, and wait for God.

**Looking forward:** <BioLink id="pico">Pico della Mirandola</BioLink> will explode this framework in 1486. His God does not assign human beings a fixed place; he gives them the freedom to become whatever they choose. The contrast with <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> could not be sharper. Where <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink>'s human being needs to surrender the will, <BioLink id="pico">Pico</BioLink>'s human being needs to exercise it. Where the medieval ballad counsels acceptance of mortality, the Renaissance will pursue glory, knowledge, and the extension of human power over nature. The medieval baseline is essential because without it, the Renaissance revolution has nothing to push against.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The medieval framework for knowledge rests on two pillars: **revelation** (what God has disclosed through Scripture and the Church) and **authority** (what the ancient thinkers, above all Aristotle, have established through reason). The relationship between faith and reason was debated intensely — Aquinas devoted enormous intellectual energy to reconciling Aristotle with Christian theology — but the hierarchy was generally clear: reason serves faith. Where the two conflict, faith takes precedence, because God's knowledge is infinite and ours is not.

The *Cloud of Unknowing* pushes this hierarchy to its mystical extreme. The anonymous monk argues that God cannot be reached by the intellect at all — only by love. "God is always quite unable to be comprehended by the first faculty, that of intelligence, but he is totally and perfectly comprehensible by the second, the power of love." The path to God requires not more knowledge but less — a deliberate embrace of intellectual darkness, a "cloud of unknowing" between yourself and the divine. "Leave your intellectual and your imaginative skills strictly alone." This is not anti-intellectualism; it is a claim that the deepest reality exceeds the capacity of the mind and can only be approached through the heart's surrender.

Adelard of Bath's *Natural Questions* offers an interesting counterpoint. Writing in the twelfth century, Adelard reasons through the question of how the earth is supported in space using what looks like proto-scientific reasoning — chains of logical deduction from first principles. But notice that his reasoning still operates within the Aristotelian framework: elements have "natural places," objects tend toward the center, and the whole argument assumes a finite, ordered cosmos with a fixed middle point. The reasoning is rigorous, but the intellectual horizon is bounded by inherited authority.

The summary of Aristotelian physics makes those boundaries explicit: four elements with natural places, celestial bodies made of incorruptible ether, a clear distinction between earthly corruption and heavenly perfection. This is the cosmology that <BioLink id="copernicus">Copernicus</BioLink>, <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink>, and <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink> will demolish — but in the medieval period it is not a hypothesis; it is the structure of reality.

**Looking forward:** The Renaissance will replace authority with observation. <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink>'s rebuke — "come with arguments or demonstrations, and bring us no more texts and authorities" — is a direct rejection of the medieval epistemological order. <BioLink id="descartes">Descartes</BioLink> will try to rebuild knowledge from scratch, trusting nothing that cannot survive radical doubt. The medieval confidence that knowledge has stable foundations (God, Scripture, Aristotle) will give way to the exhilarating and terrifying discovery that those foundations can be questioned — and that questioning them changes everything.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

In the medieval world, the question almost does not arise — or rather, it arises only in the form of temptation. The proper relationship between the individual and authority (divine, ecclesiastical, political) is obedience. Not blind obedience — Aquinas and others develop sophisticated accounts of when and how earthly authority can be challenged — but the default posture is one of submission. The soul's task is to align itself with God's will, not to assert its own.

<BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink>'s *Confessions* is the great narrative of this alignment. The entire arc of the book moves toward the moment of surrender — the moment when <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> stops resisting and allows God to claim him. The passage in your reading captures the agonizing final stages: <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> knows what he should do, he is almost there, but his will is divided against itself. The "trifles of trifles" — his old habits, pleasures, attachments — pull him back. What finally breaks the deadlock is not a rational argument but an act of grace: he hears a child's voice saying "take up and read," opens the Bible, and the conversion is accomplished. The individual does not overcome authority; the individual is overcome *by* authority — and this is presented as liberation, not subjugation.

The *Cloud of Unknowing* reinforces this posture. The monk instructs his student to "beat constantly against the cloud of unknowing" — to persist in the practice of self-emptying prayer, not as an exercise of will but as a surrender of will. "It doesn't matter how many virtues a man acquires, without this true love they are bound to be warped and thus imperfect." The virtues themselves are insufficient without the foundational act of turning the soul toward God. Individual effort matters, but only as preparation for a submission that transcends effort.

**Looking forward:** <BioLink id="luther">Luther</BioLink>'s "Here I stand" is the inversion of <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink>'s surrender. Where <BioLink id="augustine">Augustine</BioLink> submits his individual conscience to divine authority as mediated by the Church, <BioLink id="luther">Luther</BioLink> sets his individual conscience *against* the Church — insisting that the institution has corrupted the very truth it claims to guard. This is the crack that will eventually split the medieval world open. Once individual conscience can legitimately oppose institutional authority, the entire framework of obedience begins to shift. <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> will try to rebuild absolute authority on secular grounds; <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> will argue for authority by consent; <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> will reimagine the social contract; and by the time we reach <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>, the narrator of *Invisible Man* will be fleeing every form of institutional authority, searching for a self that no system can contain.

## Looking Forward: The Full Arc

This unit exists to give students a baseline — not a straw man to be knocked down, but a coherent and in many ways admirable intellectual world whose assumptions the rest of the course will trace, challenge, transform, and in some cases mourn the loss of. The medieval certainties — a meaningful universe, a knowable human place within it, a trustworthy path to truth, a legitimate structure of authority — are not stupid or naive. They represent answers to permanent human questions. What makes the subsequent periods compelling is not that they simply reject those answers but that they struggle with the consequences of losing them. <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s vertigo, Hamlet's paralysis, Ahab's rage, the invisible man's underground exile — all of these are, in different ways, responses to the absence of the certainties the medieval world once provided.
