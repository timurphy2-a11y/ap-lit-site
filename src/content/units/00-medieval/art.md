## Introduction

Medieval painting operates according to a logic that can seem alien to modern eyes, but becomes coherent once you understand its assumptions. These painters were not failing to achieve realism; they were not interested in realism. Their goal was to make the invisible visible — to depict spiritual truths that transcend the physical world. Everything about the way medieval paintings look follows from this commitment.

This is the artistic baseline for the entire course. Every subsequent period we study — Renaissance, Baroque, Enlightenment, Romantic, Modern — defines itself partly against the medieval assumptions laid out here. Understanding what medieval painters believed about the world, and how those beliefs shaped the physical choices they made with paint and gold leaf, is essential to understanding why later artists made different choices.

## Figure & Space

*How is the human figure placed within the pictorial space?*

Medieval figures exist against flat backgrounds, often shimmering gold leaf. Gold was not a color choice; it represented divine light, the radiance of heaven. There is no horizon line, no vanishing point, no attempt to place figures in a measurable physical space. Instead, size indicates importance: Christ and the Virgin are larger than saints, who are larger than donors. This is called **hieratic scale**, and it tells you that this culture organizes reality by spiritual rank, not by how things look to a human eye standing in a particular spot.

The absence of measurable space is not a failure of technique. It is a theological statement. In a universe governed by divine order, the physical world is secondary to the spiritual one. Placing figures in a realistic, navigable three-dimensional space would imply that physical location matters — that where a figure stands is more important than what it *means*. Medieval painters rejected that premise. Their paintings are maps of spiritual reality, not windows onto the physical world.

## Light & Shadow

*How does the painting use light — its source, quality, and emotional effect?*

Light in medieval painting is uniform and symbolic rather than naturalistic. It does not come from a particular direction or cast shadows. Figures are evenly lit because they exist in a spiritual realm where darkness has no purchase. The gold ground itself *is* light — not reflected light from a window, but the intrinsic radiance of the sacred.

This flatness of illumination mirrors the theological certainty of the medieval worldview: there are no shadows because there is no ambiguity. The world is ordered, its meaning is clear, and the task of the painter is to make that clarity visible. Compare this to what Baroque painters will do with light — selectively illuminating, plunging vast areas into darkness, using shadow to create mystery and doubt — and you can feel the intellectual distance between the two eras.

## Brushwork & Surface

*What is the physical character of the paint itself?*

Individual brushwork is suppressed in favor of precise, anonymous craftsmanship. **Tempera** paint (pigment mixed with egg yolk) was applied in thin, careful layers. The artist's personality was not the point; fidelity to a received tradition was. Painting was closer to devotional labor than personal expression. The smooth, icon-like surfaces reflect a world in which the individual artist's hand mattered far less than the sacred truth being transmitted.

This is worth lingering on. In every subsequent period we study, the question of whether the artist's individual hand should be visible becomes increasingly important — until, by the time we reach Modernism, the artist's gesture *is* the subject. The medieval starting point, where the maker disappears into the making, is the opposite extreme.

## What to Notice

In the Martini *Annunciation* above, notice the gold background: it is not a sky but a symbol of divine presence. The angel Gabriel and the Virgin Mary are evenly lit from no identifiable source — there are no cast shadows anywhere. The figures seem to float rather than stand on solid ground, and the space between them is symbolic (the vase of lilies, the inscribed words) rather than architectural.

In the Wilton Diptych and the Duccio *Maestà* below, notice how size indicates spiritual importance rather than distance from the viewer. Every surface is smooth, precise, and anonymous — you cannot detect an individual artist's personality in the brushwork. These are devotional objects, not personal expressions.

## Looking Forward

The Renaissance will shatter nearly every assumption described here. Where medieval painters suppressed the individual in favor of the sacred, Renaissance painters will place the individual human being at the center of a rationally organized, physically measurable world. Where medieval gold grounds declared that physical space is irrelevant, Renaissance perspective will declare that it is everything. Where medieval brushwork effaced the maker, Renaissance technique will celebrate the artist's rational skill. The revolution is total — and understanding what it replaced is the only way to feel its force.
