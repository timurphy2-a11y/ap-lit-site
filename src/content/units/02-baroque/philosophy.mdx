# Philosophical Context: The Baroque

## Introduction

The Baroque inherits the Renaissance's daring and pays for it. Everything the previous period set in motion — the new science, the Reformation, the celebration of individual freedom — has, by the seventeenth century, produced consequences that are exhilarating, terrifying, and often catastrophic. The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) devastates Central Europe in the name of religious truth. The English Civil War ends with the public beheading of a king. <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink> is silenced. The universe revealed by the telescope turns out to be inconceivably larger than anyone had imagined, and the comfortable old picture of Earth at the center of a small, orderly cosmos has been permanently destroyed.

The thinkers you will encounter in this unit are all, in different ways, responding to this atmosphere of grandeur and crisis. They share an awareness that the stakes are enormous — that the questions being asked are not academic but existential, with consequences measured in wars, executions, and the reorientation of humanity's place in the universe. This is the intellectual world in which <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> writes *Paradise Lost*: a world where freedom is real but dangerous, knowledge is powerful but insufficient, and the relationship between the individual and authority has become a matter of life and death.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

If <BioLink id="pico">Pico della Mirandola</BioLink> placed the human being at the center of creation and celebrated our freedom to become anything, <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> — writing a century and a half later — places us between two abysses and asks whether we can bear it. His "Two Infinities" passage in the *Pensées* is one of the great set pieces of European philosophy: the universe expands outward beyond comprehension, the microscopic world contracts inward beyond perception, and the human being is suspended in the middle, too small to grasp the infinite, too large to grasp the infinitesimal. "What is a man in the infinite?" <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> asks. "A nothing compared with the infinite, an all compared with the nothing, a middle point between nothing and all."

This is not despair — or not only despair. <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s other great insight is that our very awareness of our wretchedness is what makes us great. "Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking reed." A universe that crushes a human being is not nobler than the human being it crushes, because the universe does not know what it is doing. We do. Our dignity consists entirely in thought — and thought is what reveals to us how small we are. This is a distinctly Baroque paradox: grandeur and misery are not opposites but two faces of the same condition.

<PullQuote attribution="Blaise Pascal, Pensees, 1670">
  Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking reed. The entire universe need not arm itself to crush him. A vapour, a drop of water suffices to kill him. But, if the universe were to crush him, man would still be more noble than that which killed him, because he knows that he dies and the advantage which the universe has over him; the universe knows nothing of this.
</PullQuote>

<BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>, approaching the question from a very different angle, offers an equally unsettling portrait. In his account of the "state of nature," human beings are fundamentally equal — but equal in a grim way. Anyone can kill anyone else. Without a common power to keep them in awe, people exist in "continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> does not celebrate human nature; he anatomizes its dangers. We are creatures whose freedom, left ungoverned, produces not <BioLink id="pico">Pico</BioLink>'s self-creating magnificence but universal war.

**Connection to *Paradise Lost*:** <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> is in direct conversation with both of these thinkers. His poem places human beings in a universe of staggering scale — Satan's journey through Chaos, the vast distances between Heaven, Hell, and Earth — that rivals <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s two infinities. And his central characters embody <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s paradox: Adam and Eve are at once the crown of creation and pitifully vulnerable, magnificent in their freedom and devastating in their capacity to misuse it. The poem insists, against <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>, that human beings are capable of genuine goodness — but it also shows, with unflinching honesty, how freedom makes catastrophe possible.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The Renaissance had opened the floodgates of empirical inquiry — <BioLink id="copernicus">Copernicus</BioLink>, <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink>, <BioLink id="descartes">Descartes</BioLink>. The Baroque does not close them. <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink>'s *Principia Mathematica* (1687) represents the supreme achievement of the scientific revolution: a mathematical framework that explains the motions of everything from falling apples to orbiting planets. The universe, it turns out, operates according to laws that human reason can discover and express with precision. This is an astonishing triumph of knowledge.

But the Baroque thinkers are also acutely aware of what knowledge cannot do. <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>, himself a brilliant mathematician and physicist, insists that reason has limits. The "Two Infinities" passage is, among other things, an argument about epistemology: we are constitutionally incapable of grasping either extreme of reality. "Our intelligence holds the same position in the order of the intellect as our body in the extent of nature." We can know the middle of things — but the foundations and the ultimate reaches are hidden from us. And the deepest truths, <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> argues, are not accessible to reason at all: "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know." God is perceived by the heart, not by the intellect.

<BioLink id="cavendish">Margaret Cavendish</BioLink>'s *The Blazing World* offers a different angle on knowledge and its limits — and on who gets to pursue knowledge in the first place. Her protagonist becomes empress of a fantastical world and immediately reorganizes its intellectual life, assigning different species of hybrid creatures to different branches of inquiry. The passage is playful, but it raises serious questions about the relationship between power and knowledge, about who controls the institutions of learning, and about whether the structures we build to organize knowledge might also constrain it.

**Connection to *Paradise Lost*:** The poem is structured around a series of conversations about knowledge and its proper limits. In Book VIII, the angel Raphael warns Adam not to inquire too deeply into the mechanics of the cosmos — "be lowly wise," he counsels. The question of what human beings are meant to know, and what knowledge is dangerous, runs through the entire poem. The Fall itself is, in one reading, an epistemological catastrophe: Eve eats from the Tree of *Knowledge*. <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> does not condemn knowledge as such — he was himself one of the most learned men in Europe — but he insists that knowledge pursued without wisdom, humility, and love becomes destructive. This is <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s argument translated into narrative.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

This is the thread where the Baroque is at its most intense and most divided, because the seventeenth century has just lived through the most dramatic confrontation between individual conscience and sovereign authority in European history: the English Civil War and the execution of Charles I.

<BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>'s *Leviathan*, published in 1651 — two years after the king's beheading — is written in the shadow of that catastrophe. His argument is that the only alternative to the war of all against all is the total surrender of individual judgment to a sovereign power. You give up your right to govern yourself; in exchange, the sovereign provides order, safety, and the possibility of civilized life. The sovereign's authority is absolute, and to resist it is to invite a return to the chaos of the state of nature. This is not a cheerful argument, but it is a powerful one, and it was forged in the experience of actual civil war.

<BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> is <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>'s great antagonist on this question — and *Paradise Lost* can be read as, among many other things, a sustained refutation of the Hobbesian worldview. <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> had served the Commonwealth that executed the king. He believed that political authority must be earned, not merely imposed, and that obedience is only meaningful when it is freely given. His God does not rule through terror (that is Satan's mischaracterization); he rules through love, and he insists that his creatures be free to disobey. "Not free, what proof could they have given sincere / Of true allegiance, constant faith, or love?" The entire architecture of *Paradise Lost* depends on the idea that freedom — real freedom, including the freedom to fall — is not the problem to be solved (as <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> would have it) but the precondition of everything that matters.

<BioLink id="lanyer">Emelia Lanyer</BioLink>'s "Eve's Apology in Defense of Women" adds another dimension to this thread. Writing in 1611 — the first book of original poetry by a woman published in England — <BioLink id="lanyer">Lanyer</BioLink> challenges the patriarchal authority that blamed Eve (and by extension all women) for the Fall. Her argument is daring: if Eve sinned out of love and a desire for knowledge, Adam sinned out of weakness despite having received God's command directly. The question of who has authority to interpret the foundational stories of a culture, and whose voice gets heard in that interpretation, is as much a part of the Baroque intellectual landscape as <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>'s political theory.

**Connection to *Paradise Lost*:** The poem stages the individual-authority question at the highest possible stakes. Satan rebels against divine authority and frames his rebellion as a fight for freedom — "Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven." But <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> makes clear that Satan's version of freedom is self-deception: his "liberty" leads only to enslavement to his own rage and pride. Adam and Eve, by contrast, are genuinely free within a structure of loving authority — until they choose to break it. The poem asks: what does legitimate authority look like? What does genuine obedience look like? And what happens when free beings make the wrong choice? These are <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink>'s questions, answered in a way <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> would never have accepted.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From the Renaissance:** The Renaissance celebrated human freedom, empirical discovery, and individual conscience. The Baroque inherits all three — but in a darker key. <BioLink id="pico">Pico</BioLink>'s self-creating human becomes <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink>'s thinking reed, trembling between infinities. <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink>'s triumphant empiricism becomes <BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink>'s magnificent but impersonal mathematical universe, in which the human significance that the Renaissance assumed is no longer guaranteed. <BioLink id="luther">Luther</BioLink>'s individual conscience, which defied the Pope, has produced a century of religious warfare. The Baroque does not repudiate the Renaissance — it takes the Renaissance seriously enough to confront its consequences.

**Toward the Enlightenment:** The Enlightenment will attempt to resolve the Baroque's tensions by doubling down on reason. Where <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> said the heart has its reasons that reason cannot know, <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> will say: dare to use your own understanding. Where <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> demanded submission to absolute authority, <BioLink id="locke">Locke</BioLink> will argue for government by consent and natural rights. Where the Baroque saw grandeur and terror in the new science, the Enlightenment will see progress and the promise of a better world. The emotional temperature drops; the drawing room replaces the battlefield. But the questions the Baroque raised — about whether reason is sufficient, about whether freedom leads to flourishing or to ruin — do not go away. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>, writing in the Enlightenment's twilight, will explore them with a precision that the Baroque thinkers, for all their brilliance, never achieved.
