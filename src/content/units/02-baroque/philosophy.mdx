# Philosophical Context: The Baroque

## Introduction

Seventeenth-century thought developed under pressure from religious division, civil war, changing cosmologies, and arguments over the authority of reason. Its thinkers did not agree on a single solution. <BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> grounded order in sovereign power; <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> exposed the limits of reason and the instability of human justice; <BioLink id="cavendish">Cavendish</BioLink> challenged the confidence and institutions of experimental science; <BioLink id="lanyer">Lanyer</BioLink> redistributed responsibility in the story of the Fall. Each returned to a common problem: human beings possess judgment and freedom, yet those powers can produce error, conflict, and domination.

*Paradise Lost* joins the problem of political liberty to the theological problem of free will. <BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink>'s creatures act within an order they did not create. Their choices remain genuinely their own, and moral responsibility depends upon that freedom.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do human beings stand in the order of things?*

The Renaissance often represented human beings through their capacities for reason, self-formation, and achievement. <BioLink id="pascal">Pascal</BioLink> retains those capacities while placing them against scales that expose their limits. In the *Pensées*, humanity occupies no secure center. The universe extends beyond comprehension; matter can be divided toward an equally inaccessible minuteness. Human beings stand between these two infinities, unable to grasp either limit or locate themselves through scale alone.

<PullQuote unit="02-baroque" attribution="Blaise Pascal · Pensées · published 1670">
  "Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking reed."
</PullQuote>

<div class="plate-pair left vcenter">
<div class="pair-text">

Pascal's "thinking reed" holds grandeur and misery together. A human body is fragile enough to be destroyed by a vapor or a drop of water. Thought nevertheless gives human beings a dignity the physical universe does not possess: the universe may crush a person without knowing that it has done so. Human greatness consists in the consciousness of weakness, not mastery over nature.

</div>

<BioLink id="hobbes">Hobbes</BioLink> begins from vulnerability of another kind. In the **state of nature**, his name for a condition without a recognized authority able to settle disputes, individuals possess roughly equal capacities to threaten one another. Even a physically weaker person may kill a stronger one through alliance or surprise. Competition, fear, and disagreement about honor or necessity make conflict possible even when no one desires continual war. The state of nature describes what private judgment becomes when no common power can make agreements dependable; it does not require the premise that human beings are innately wicked.

Like Machiavelli, Pascal also distrusts the justice created by custom and political borders. In one fragment, a speaker asks why another person may kill him merely because he lives "on the other side of the water" and his ruler has a quarrel with the speaker's ruler. The same killing becomes murder on one side of the river and heroism on the other. In a striking reframing of Machiavelli's river of Fortuna, Pascal's river exposes the gap between "real" justice and arbitrary conventions merely enforced as justice.

Hobbes and Pascal therefore begin with related instability and move in different directions. Hobbes seeks a public authority capable of ending private conflict. Pascal doubts that political force can become universal justice merely by securing obedience.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do human beings know what they know, and how far can knowledge take them?*

The seventeenth century changed both the instruments and the ambitions of natural inquiry. Telescopic observation revealed mountains on the moon, the phases of Venus, and moons orbiting Jupiter. These discoveries challenged inherited divisions between an imperfect earth and perfect, unchanging heavens. They did not immediately establish one uncontested cosmology. Geocentric, heliocentric, and hybrid models continued to compete.

<div class="plate-pair right vcenter">
<div class="pair-text">

<BioLink id="milton">Milton</BioLink> encountered that controversy directly. During his Italian travels in 1638 or 1639, he visited <BioLink id="galileo">Galileo</BioLink> near Florence while the astronomer lived under house arrest. Galileo is the only contemporary whom Milton names in *Paradise Lost*. The epic repeatedly compares distant or uncertain vision to objects seen through the "optic glass," turning the telescope into an image of both enlarged knowledge and persistent limitation. The instrument extends sight; it does not guarantee interpretation.

<BioLink id="newton">Newton</BioLink>'s *Principia* (1687) gave the period's mathematical natural philosophy an influential synthesis. The same laws of motion and gravitation could explain terrestrial and celestial movement. The achievement joined observation, mathematics, inherited problems, and the work of many earlier investigators. Its reach remained specific: mathematical success in natural philosophy did not make the same method sufficient for every kind of truth.

</div>

Pascal understood mathematical demonstration from within. He made significant contributions to geometry, probability, and the study of pressure and vacuum. His critique gives disciplined thought a proper domain and a limit. The **heart** in the *Pensées* names an immediate faculty through which first principles and religious truth may be apprehended without discursive proof; it does not mean emotion alone.

Pascal's distinction between knowledge and love sharpens that limit. Knowledge that God exists is not identical to love of God, and neither intellectual assent nor fear produces saving faith by itself. The problem is moral and spiritual as well as epistemological: a mind may recognize a truth without ordering its desires toward that truth.

The idea of the **hidden God** gives this limitation theological force. <BioLink id="luther">Luther</BioLink> had distinguished the God revealed through Christ and Scripture from the divine will inaccessible to human speculation. Pascal likewise argues that God supplies signs sufficient for sincere seekers without making divine presence so overwhelming that belief becomes compulsory. Hiddenness prevents human reason from converting God into an object it can master.

<BioLink id="cavendish">Margaret Cavendish</BioLink> challenges confidence from another direction. *The Description of a New World, Called the Blazing World* (1666) combines fiction, satire, natural philosophy, and utopian experiment. Its Empress questions groups of specialist investigators and reorganizes their intellectual institutions. Cavendish defended a vital, self-moving conception of matter and criticized mechanical accounts of nature. She also attacked the tendency of instruments and learned societies to multiply appearances without producing agreement. Her imagined world gives a woman authority largely denied to her in the institutions she examines, yet the Empress's power also makes the work an experiment in intellectual rule rather than a simple fantasy of liberation.

    <p>In Book VIII, Raphael advises Adam to be "lowly wise" and to confine thought to knowledge relevant to human life and duty. The warning does not condemn inquiry.     <p>The Fall turns on the use and ordering of knowledge rather than the desire for knowledge alone. Satan uses partial truths to detach knowledge from trust, responsibility, and love. Knowledge does not save either character because recognition of the good does not guarantee love of it.</p>

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern political and spiritual life?*

The English Civil War made sovereignty an immediate question. Hobbes argued that peace required individuals to authorize a common power whose decisions could not be divided among competing authorities. The sovereign could be a monarch or an assembly, but its authority had to remain undivided. Subjects retained the right to resist direct threats to their lives because self-preservation was the purpose for which political authority had been created. Hobbesian obedience is therefore extensive without becoming the literal surrender of every inward judgment or natural liberty.

The Reformation had already made freedom a theological problem. In *On Free Will*, <BioLink id="erasmus">Erasmus</BioLink> defended a limited human capacity to respond to divine grace. Luther answered in *The Bondage of the Will* (1525) that fallen human beings could not choose salvation through their own power. For Luther, grace saves rather than assists an independently free will. <BioLink id="calvin">Calvin</BioLink> placed similar emphasis on divine election and the inability of fallen humanity to merit salvation.

Milton inherits this controversy and develops a distinctive defense of creaturely freedom. In Book III of *Paradise Lost*, God declares that angels and human beings were created with the capacity to choose good or evil for themselves.

<PullQuote unit="02-baroque" attribution="John Milton · Paradise Lost, Book III · 1667">
  "So will fall,<br />
  He and his faithless progeny: whose fault?<br />
  Whose but his own? Ingrate, he had of me<br />
  All he could have; I made him just and right,<br />
  Sufficient to have stood, though free to fall."
</PullQuote>

Foreknowledge does not compel the actions foreknown. Without the possibility of refusal, obedience would have no moral meaning and praise would belong to necessity rather than to the creature.

The speech also responds to the problem of the hidden God. Milton's God explains his justice in forensic language because the poem anticipates the charge that omnipotence and foreknowledge make God responsible for the Fall. This does not establish that Milton wrote *Paradise Lost* directly against Luther or Calvin. It places the poem within the Reformation argument over grace, freedom, and responsibility, then shows Milton taking a position that diverges from strict accounts of the bound will and predestination.

<BioLink id="lanyer">Aemilia Lanyer</BioLink> revises the Fall from another direction. Her *Salve Deus Rex Judaeorum* (1611) was the first substantial English poetry collection published by a woman with an explicit claim to literary authorship and patronage. In "Eve's Apology in Defence of Women," Pilate's wife asks why women should bear continuing blame for Eve's act while men excuse Adam and condemn Christ.

Lanyer does not need to make Eve faultless to challenge the traditional distribution of responsibility. Adam possesses greater strength, receives the command directly, and knowingly accepts the fruit. Eve is deceived; Adam chooses with fuller knowledge. Lanyer turns Genesis into an argument about the interpretive authority that allowed men to assign guilt to women while minimizing male choice.

    <p>Milton's God, Satan, and human pair each define authority differently. Satan calls all subordination tyranny, yet organizes Hell through hierarchy, intimidation, and deceit. His rhetoric treats freedom as the absence of any superior claim, then makes other beings instruments of his will.</p>
    <p>Adam and Eve begin under an authority that gives one prohibition and leaves a wide field for judgment, labor, conversation, and love. The command creates the possibility of obedience, but freedom creates the possibility of refusal. After the Fall, authority changes: accusation replaces mutual counsel, desire becomes domination, and political hierarchy enters the marriage.</p>
    <p>The poem distinguishes forms of rule rather than opposing free individuals to all authority. Authority may protect a shared good, demand responsible obedience, or conceal domination behind the language of liberty. Freedom may sustain love and judgment or become the excuse by which appetite refuses every limit.</p>

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

<div class="forward">
<div class="lbl">Looking back: Freedom after the Reformation</div>

The Baroque inherits Renaissance arguments about human capacity without inheriting a single Renaissance confidence. Pico's freedom to ascend or descend becomes, in Milton, freedom to stand or fall. Galileo's challenge to inherited cosmology becomes a debate among models that observation alone has not fully resolved. Luther's appeal to scriptural authority develops into competing confessions that each claim fidelity to revealed truth.

The seventeenth century therefore intensifies problems already present in the Renaissance. Individual judgment can resist corrupt institutions and produce new knowledge; it can also fracture agreement. Political authority can prevent civil conflict; it can also demand obedience to injustice. Theological freedom can preserve responsibility; it can also appear to limit divine sovereignty.

</div>

<div class="forward">
<div class="lbl">Looking forward: Public reason and limited government</div>

Enlightenment thinkers inherit these conflicts and change the proposed solutions. Natural philosophers seek methods that can produce publicly testable agreement across confessional divisions. Political writers ask whether authority can protect peace while remaining limited by rights, consent, or law. Arguments for toleration attempt to reduce the political consequences of religious disagreement.

The transition rearranges the relationships among faith, reason, authority, and freedom by changing the institutions through which claims are justified. Locke will revise Hobbes's social contract by making government conditional and limited; later thinkers will ask whether reason itself can supply principles shared across differences of revelation and tradition. The Baroque leaves them a problem rather than a completed program: how can fallible and divided people create an order that does not destroy the freedom it exists to protect?

</div>
