# Philosophical Context: Romanticism

## Introduction

Romanticism begins as a reaction and becomes a revolution. What it reacts against is the Enlightenment's confidence that reason is the supreme human faculty — that the world can be understood, organized, and improved through rational analysis. What it replaces that confidence with is harder to summarize, because Romanticism is, almost by definition, suspicious of neat summaries. But the core claim is something like this: the most important truths about human experience — about love, death, beauty, suffering, the encounter with what is vast and incomprehensible — cannot be reached by reason alone. They require imagination, feeling, and a willingness to confront what exceeds our capacity to understand.

This is the intellectual world of <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink>'s fiction. *Moby-Dick* is a novel that contains chapters on cetology, philosophy, and the technical details of the whaling industry — it takes knowledge seriously — but its deepest subject is something that resists knowledge entirely: the white whale, blank and inscrutable, onto which Ahab projects all his fury and meaning, and which remains, finally, unknowable. "Bartleby, the Scrivener" pushes this confrontation with inscrutability into a claustrophobic Wall Street office. *Benito Cereno* stages it as a political and racial nightmare. In all three, <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink> places his characters before something they cannot comprehend and watches what happens.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

The Enlightenment placed the human being at the center of a knowable world, equipped with reason and capable of progress. Romanticism decenters us — not by diminishing human dignity (the Romantics are intensely interested in human experience) but by insisting that what makes us most fully human is not our rationality but our capacity for feeling, imagination, and the confrontation with what is larger than ourselves.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *Confessions* — the first modern autobiography — announces this shift with its extraordinary opening declaration: "I have entered upon a performance which is without example." <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> claims radical uniqueness. He is not presenting himself as an exemplar of rational virtue; he is presenting himself as *himself*, in all his contradictions, weaknesses, and passions. "I know my heart, and have studied mankind; I am not made like any one I have been acquainted with, perhaps like no one in existence." The Enlightenment valued the universal (what all rational beings share); <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> values the particular, the irreducibly individual. And he locates the self not in reason but in feeling: "An infinity of sensations were familiar to me, without possessing any precise idea of the objects to which they related — I had conceived nothing — I had felt the whole."

<BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink>'s *Nature* takes this inward turn and projects it outward, onto the American landscape. Standing on bare ground, his head "bathed by the blithe air, and uplifted into infinite space," <BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink> experiences a dissolution of the boundary between self and world: "I become a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all." This is the Romantic sublime — the experience of being overwhelmed by something so vast that the ordinary self disappears, replaced by a kind of ecstatic unity with nature. It is precisely what <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> and <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink> would have warned against: the abandonment of rational self-possession in favor of visionary transport.

<BioLink id="schiller">Schiller</BioLink>, in his *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man*, offers a more philosophical account of the same territory. His concept of the "play drive" — the synthesis of the sensuous and the rational — argues that human beings are most fully themselves not when they are reasoning and not when they are feeling, but when the two are held in balance through aesthetic experience. Art is not a luxury; it is the activity through which we become whole.

**Connection to *Moby-Dick*:** <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink>'s novel is populated by characters who embody different versions of the Romantic human position. Ahab is the Romantic visionary pushed to his destructive extreme — a man who insists that the universe must mean something, who projects his rage and suffering onto the white whale and pursues it to annihilation. Ishmael is the Romantic observer — contemplative, self-aware, drawn to the sublime, capable of the kind of ecstatic merging with the world that <BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink> describes ("meditation and water are wedded for ever"). Queequeg embodies a natural dignity and wholeness that the Western characters have lost. The novel asks: what does it mean to be human in a universe that may not care whether we exist?

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The Romantic answer is not that reason is useless — <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> insists that imagination and reason are complementary — but that reason alone is radically insufficient. <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink>'s *Defence of Poetry* makes the case with precision: "Reason is the enumeration of qualities already known; imagination is the perception of the value of those qualities, both separately and as a whole. Reason respects the differences, and imagination the similitudes of things." Reason analyzes; imagination synthesizes. Reason tells you what something is made of; imagination tells you what it means.

<PullQuote attribution="Percy Bysshe Shelley, A Defence of Poetry, 1821">
  Reason is the enumeration of qualities already known; imagination is the perception of the value of those qualities, both separately and as a whole. Reason respects the differences, and imagination the similitudes of things.
</PullQuote>

This is why the Romantics elevate the poet above the scientist and the philosopher. Not because poets know more facts, but because they perceive connections and significances that analytical reason misses. Poetry, <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> argues, "lifts the veil from the hidden beauty of the world, and makes familiar objects be as if they were not familiar." It defamiliarizes — and in that defamiliarization, something essential about reality becomes visible that was hidden by habit and convention.

<BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink> makes a parallel argument about nature itself. The problem, he says, is that "few adult persons can see nature." We look at the landscape and see property lines, economic value, familiar objects. The poet — or the child, or the person whose "inward and outward senses are still truly adjusted to each other" — sees something else: the living unity beneath the surfaces, the "occult relation between man and the vegetable." This is not anti-rational; it is a claim that rationality, operating alone, produces a diminished picture of the world.

**Connection to *Moby-Dick*:** The novel is, among other things, an encyclopedia — of whaling, of cetology, of maritime law and custom. Ishmael compulsively classifies, measures, and analyzes. And yet the whale escapes every system of classification. The whiteness of the whale — the subject of one of the novel's most famous chapters — resists interpretation: it can signify purity or terror, the divine or the void, everything or nothing. <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink> dramatizes the Romantic epistemological crisis: knowledge proliferates, but meaning recedes. The more Ishmael knows about whales, the less he understands about *this* whale. Reason has reached its limit; what lies beyond is the territory of the sublime.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

Romanticism is, in many of its forms, a literature of rebellion. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> defies the conventions of autobiography by exposing his own vices and weaknesses. <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> declares poets "the unacknowledged legislators of the world" — claiming for the visionary imagination an authority that supersedes political power. The Romantic artist is, almost by definition, an outsider: someone who sees truths that society refuses to acknowledge and who pays the price for that vision.

<BioLink id="marx">Marx</BioLink> and Engels, writing in 1848, translate this Romantic energy into political revolution. Their analysis of capitalism as a system that reduces all human relationships to "naked self-interest" and "callous cash payment" is, among other things, a Romantic critique of Enlightenment rationality applied to economics. The bourgeoisie has rationalized everything — and in doing so has destroyed every form of human connection that cannot be expressed as a transaction. "All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned." The *Manifesto* calls for the overthrow of a system that has made the world knowable and profitable at the cost of making it inhuman.

**Connection to *Moby-Dick*, "Bartleby," and *Benito Cereno*:** All three <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink> texts explore the relationship between individuals and the systems that govern them. The *Pequod* is a commercial enterprise — a floating factory — and its crew is a global labor force yoked to Ahab's private obsession. The novel asks what happens when a system (capitalism, the whaling industry, the ship's hierarchy) is commandeered by an individual will that has no interest in the system's stated purposes. "Bartleby" inverts this: a man within the most rationalized of environments (a Wall Street law office) simply refuses to participate. "I would prefer not to" is the most quietly devastating act of individual resistance in American literature. And *Benito Cereno* reveals that the apparent order aboard a Spanish slave ship conceals a reality that the American captain — a man of good will but limited perception — cannot read. Authority, in all three texts, is revealed to be fragile, potentially fraudulent, and dependent on the willingness of individuals to go along with it.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From the Enlightenment:** The Enlightenment trusted reason, valued social order, and believed in progress. Romanticism does not simply reject these commitments — <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink>, after all, insists that reason and imagination are both necessary — but it dramatically expands the range of what counts as knowledge and what counts as a legitimate human response to the world. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s heroines correct their errors through rational self-examination; Ahab is destroyed by the force of his own vision. <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> said "dare to know"; the Romantics say "dare to feel, dare to imagine, dare to confront what you cannot understand." The emotional temperature rises; the drawing room gives way to the ocean.

**Toward Modernism:** But the Romantics' faith in the individual imagination, in the sublime encounter with nature, in the visionary artist as unacknowledged legislator — all of this will be tested by the catastrophes of the twentieth century. What happens to the Romantic self when it encounters not the whale but the trenches of the Western Front? What happens to the individual when the structures of authority become totalitarian? What happens to imagination when the unconscious — <BioLink id="freud">Freud</BioLink>'s discovery — turns out to be not a wellspring of visionary truth but a tangle of repressed desires and childhood wounds? Modernism inherits Romanticism's intensity but loses its confidence. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s invisible man is, in a sense, a Romantic individual — unique, perceptive, passionately alive — trapped in a world that has no category for him.
