# Philosophical Context: Romanticism

## Introduction

Romanticism is not a single doctrine and does not begin by discarding the Enlightenment. Its writers inherit the demand for freedom, criticism of tradition, and confidence in independent judgment. They challenge narrower accounts of human beings as primarily rational and of nature as an object fully available to classification and use. Reason remains necessary, but it is joined by imagination, feeling, memory, embodiment, language, and aesthetic experience as equally valuable elements of the human experience.

This is the intellectual world of <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink>'s fiction. *Moby-Dick* takes factual knowledge seriously: it contains cetology, maritime history, philosophy, theology, and the technical practices of whaling. Yet its forms of knowledge remain partial. The whale can be measured, classified, hunted, rendered into oil, and interpreted as symbol without being exhausted by any of those acts. Ahab responds to that limit by forcing every appearance into one private meaning. Ishmael responds by multiplying perspectives and allowing knowledge to remain provisional.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do human beings stand in the order of things?*

Romanticism gives new authority to individual experience without treating every feeling as true. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *Confessions* announces an unprecedented project of self-revelation. He will present a particular person in his contradictions, failures, desires, and sensations rather than offer an exemplary life cleansed for public instruction.

<PullQuote unit="04-romanticism" attribution="Jean-Jacques Rousseau · The Confessions · composed c. 1765–1770">
  I have entered upon a performance which is without example, whose accomplishment will have no imitator. I mean to present my fellow-mortals with a man in all the integrity of nature; and this man shall be myself.
</PullQuote>

Rousseau's claim is larger than autobiography. The individual life becomes a possible source of knowledge because sensations and emotions possess histories that abstract accounts of human nature can miss. Yet the self revealed in the *Confessions* is not transparent even to itself. Memory selects and reshapes experience; confession can become performance; the desire to appear wholly sincere can expose new forms of self-deception.

<div class="plate-pair werther-pair">
<div class="pair-text">

<BioLink id="goethe">Goethe</BioLink>'s *The Sorrows of Young Werther* (1774) turns emotional intensity into narrative form. Werther treats the force of his response to nature, art, and Lotte as evidence of a depth that ordinary society cannot accommodate. Albert, Lotte's fiancé and later husband, is steady, employed, and bound to practical obligations. Werther borrows Albert's pistols and uses them to end his life. The practical man's instrument becomes part of the visionary's self-destruction.

The novel does not simply endorse Werther. His suffering is real, but so are his self-dramatization, disregard for limits, and inability to imagine a future not organized around his desire. Readers nevertheless made him an emblem of exceptional sensibility. Reports of imitative dress and suicide surrounded the novel, although the number and direct causation of such deaths remain uncertain. The episode demonstrates a problem Romantic heroism repeatedly confronts: intense feeling may deepen perception, but it does not by itself establish moral authority.

<BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink>'s *Nature* gives inward experience a different direction. Standing beneath an open sky, he becomes a "transparent eye-ball": "I am nothing; I see all." The ordinary boundary between observer and landscape seems to dissolve within a larger unity. This is one form of the Romantic sublime, an experience in which scale or power exceeds ordinary comprehension and expands the imagination.

The sublime was not a Romantic rejection of <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink>. Kant had made it a central philosophical category: the mind encounters what imagination cannot adequately contain and becomes aware of powers not reducible to sensory magnitude. Romantic writers inherit and transform that problem. Some seek spiritual unity in nature; others discover estrangement, terror, or an inhuman power that refuses consolation.

<BioLink id="darwin">Darwin</BioLink> alters the human position from another direction. *On the Origin of Species* (1859) explains adaptation through variation, inheritance, competition, and natural selection rather than through fixed species arranged toward a predetermined summit. Human beings are not the goal of the mechanism, although Darwin did not pretend that natural selection settled every theological or philosophical question about purpose. Published eight years after *Moby-Dick*, *Origin* belongs to the novel's later intellectual horizon rather than its sources. It gives new scientific force to a question Melville had already made urgent: what if nature does not organize itself around human meaning?

    <p>Ahab concentrates recognizable traits of the Romantic and Byronic hero: radical individuality, suffering converted into authority, contempt for prudence, defiance of an apparently hostile order, and movement toward self-destruction. Melville does not merely take an admirable visionary "too far." He places the Romantic will in command of an industrial institution and gives its private revelation consequences for other lives.</p>
    <p>The comparison between Werther and Ahab clarifies the structure without making the characters equivalent. Albert and Starbuck are practical counterfigures who recognize obligations the visionary refuses. Starbuck knows that the *Pequod* belongs to owners, that its purpose is to fill its hold with oil, and that its crew expects to return alive. Ahab uses the authority, discipline, and equipment of that commercial voyage to conduct a war no owner or sailor authorized.</p>
    <p>Ishmael offers an alternative form of Romantic subjectivity. He is inward without treating inwardness as sovereignty. He seeks relief from isolation through work, friendship, storytelling, and attention to a world that repeatedly exceeds him. His bond with Queequeg is not a recovery of supposedly "natural" wholeness. It is a relationship across differences in language, religion, culture, and experience, sustained by acts of trust. Ishmael survives because he can revise an interpretation, accept uncertainty, and depend upon another person.</p>

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do human beings know what they know, and how far can knowledge take them?*

The Romantic answer is not that reason is useless. <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink>'s *Defence of Poetry* distinguishes analytical thought from the imagination that perceives relations, values, and wholes:

<PullQuote unit="04-romanticism" attribution="Percy Bysshe Shelley · A Defence of Poetry, composed 1821">
  Reason is the enumeration of qualities already known; imagination is the perception of the value of those qualities, both separately and as a whole. Reason respects the differences, and imagination the similitudes of things.
</PullQuote>

Shelley calls the faculties complementary. Reason discriminates and orders; imagination discovers relations and makes unfamiliar possibilities available to thought. His defense of poetry is a particular Romantic argument, not a movement-wide declaration that poets possess higher factual knowledge than scientists or philosophers. It proposes that moral and political change depends upon the capacity to imagine lives, connections, and values not yet contained in an established vocabulary.

Emerson similarly argues that habit and use can narrow perception. A landscape becomes property, commodity, route, or resource. The poet attends to relations that those categories conceal. Such perception does not eliminate analysis. It raises questions about what a system of classification makes visible, what it leaves out, and what interests shaped its boundaries.

<BioLink id="feuerbach">Ludwig Feuerbach</BioLink> gives this problem a critical turn. In *The Essence of Christianity* (1841), he argues that human beings attribute idealized forms of human reason, love, and will to God, then experience those projected qualities as the attributes of an independent being. Projection produces alienation: people become subordinate to powers drawn from their own nature because they no longer recognize those powers as their creation.

    <p>The novel distinguishes three acts that can otherwise blur together: knowing facts about an object, interpreting its significance, and forcing that interpretation upon others. Feuerbach supplies an interpretive vocabulary for the second and third, not evidence of direct influence upon Melville. Ahab does not merely discover a stable meaning in the whale. He gathers suffering, rage, metaphysical doubt, and a demand for purpose, attaches them to an external object, and then experiences that object as the power governing his life. The whale becomes the apparent author of the meaning Ahab has assigned to it.</p>
    <p>The "pasteboard masks" speech makes the interpretive violence explicit:</p>
    <PullQuote unit="04-romanticism" attribution="Herman Melville · Moby-Dick, Chapter 36 · 1851">

His conclusion cannot be disproved by the whale's silence, because silence itself becomes evidence of concealment. Interpretation has stopped being revisable, and because he commands the ship, a meaning that answers a private need acquires material force over other people.</p>

    <p>Ishmael's cetology operates differently, and his knowledge remains corrigible. He classifies whales through an elaborate system that is learned, comic, useful, and openly incomplete, and he knows that his categories are constructions. The novel can therefore respect empirical knowledge without pretending that classification exhausts its object. His chapters do not demonstrate that reason has failed and must give way to the sublime. They show knowledge continuing under conditions of uncertainty.</p>
    <p>The whale's whiteness intensifies the problem. White can signify innocence, holiness, beauty, terror, vacancy, or the combination and absence of color. No single symbolic system secures the meaning. Ahab treats ambiguity as an enemy to defeat; Ishmael makes ambiguity the occasion for comparison. One demands a final answer. The other constructs an archive of partial ones.</p>

Between a projected authority and an indifferent nature, the novel refuses both easy consolation and easy nihilism. The absence of a guaranteed cosmic meaning does not erase the human meanings created through friendship, labor, narration, and responsibility.</p>

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern political and social life?*

Romanticism often gives the outsider a privileged position. The artist, exile, wanderer, rebel, or visionary may perceive what conventional society has taught itself not to see. Yet no outsider stands wholly beyond institutions. Books require publishers and readers; travel depends upon money, borders, and empire; the solitary artist participates in a commercial culture even while criticizing it.

<BioLink id="destael">Germaine de Staël</BioLink> makes that tension visible. Her novels and critical works join inward feeling to questions of gender, nation, religion, and political liberty. Napoleon's government treated her influence as a political threat, restricted her movements, and ordered the destruction of the 1810 French printing of *On Germany*. Published abroad in 1813, the work introduced many French and British readers to German literature, philosophy, and the distinction between classical and Romantic art. De Staël's career shows Romantic individualism operating through salons, correspondence, translation, exile, and transnational intellectual networks rather than solitary inspiration alone.

<BioLink id="marx">Marx</BioLink> and Engels analyze the material institutions beneath modern claims of freedom. Their intellectual path runs through Hegel and Feuerbach rather than through a simple conversion of Romantic feeling into revolution. Hegel had described forms of alienation within the development of consciousness and social life. Feuerbach relocated alienation in humanity's projection of its powers onto religion. Marx applies the concept to labor and property: workers create objects they do not own, surrender control over the activity of production, and confront the products and institutions of their own work as powers belonging to someone else.

*The Communist Manifesto* (1848) describes the bourgeoisie as revolutionary because it repeatedly transforms production, social relations, and the globe itself. Its achievement is inseparable from disruption and exploitation. "All that is solid melts into air" does not mourn a stable preindustrial world that can simply be restored. It describes a system compelled to remake inherited structures while reducing more relationships to exchange and "callous cash payment."

<BioLink id="dostoevsky">Dostoevsky</BioLink> supplies a later counterargument from within the revolt against rational systems. In *Notes from Underground* (1864), the narrator attacks the belief that scientific knowledge and enlightened self-interest could make human conduct predictable. People may reject an apparently beneficial order simply to prove that they remain free. Yet the Underground Man is not presented as a triumphant Romantic rebel. His relentless analysis becomes paralysis; his claim to superior consciousness damages every possibility of mutual relationship.

This makes him a productive comparison with Bartleby, but not Bartleby's twin. Both figures occupy rationalized offices and interrupt ordinary work through refusal. Their refusals remain radically ambiguous. They may expose the limits of the institution, defend an irreducible self, express suffering that other people fail to recognize, or become forms of isolation that cannot create an alternative community.

Across all three works, authority depends upon interpretation, performance, and compliance. Its outward forms can conceal purposes radically different from those an observer assumes.</p>

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

<div class="forward">
<div class="lbl">Looking back: Children and critics of the Enlightenment</div>

Romanticism inherits the Enlightenment's commitment to freedom and its suspicion of authority protected only by custom. Romantic writers revise these arguments rather than replacing reason with passion. They give greater weight to the particular life, historical culture, organic relation, aesthetic form, and experiences that resist conceptual mastery. Their internal disagreements matter. Emerson's moments of unity with nature, <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink>'s moral imagination, Goethe's self-consuming hero, <BioLink id="schiller">Schiller</BioLink>'s aesthetic education, de Staël's historical and transnational criticism, and Melville's skeptical multiplicity cannot be reduced to one doctrine.

The deepest conflict is not reason against feeling. It concerns what should happen when feeling, imagination, and rational analysis produce different claims. Ahab converts suffering into certainty and certainty into command. Ishmael allows knowledge and imagination to correct one another without expecting either to eliminate uncertainty.

</div>

<div class="forward">
<div class="lbl">Looking forward: The Romantic self under pressure</div>

The nineteenth century tests Romantic individualism before the catastrophes of the twentieth. Modernism will inherit this pressured self rather than simply abandon Romantic intensity. Its writers retain the inward voice, the visionary image, symbolic density, and distrust of convention while losing confidence that the individual imagination can create wholeness. Scientific naturalism, imperial violence, mass politics, urban anonymity, and new accounts of unconscious motivation further destabilize the sovereign self.

Melville already stands at that threshold. Ahab anticipates the dangerous will that converts a private interpretation into collective destiny. Bartleby anticipates the individual whose refusal can expose a system without escaping it. Ishmael anticipates the modern narrator who survives through fragments, borrowed voices, unstable classifications, and relationships that do not require final agreement.

</div>
