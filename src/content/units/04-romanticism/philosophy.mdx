# Philosophical Context: Romanticism

## Introduction

Romanticism begins as a reaction and becomes a revolution. Against the Enlightenment's confidence that reason is the supreme human faculty, it develops the countervailing belief that the most important truths about human experience can only be accessed by imagination, feeling, and a willingness to confront that which exceeds our capacity to understand.

This is the intellectual world of <BioLink id="melville">Melville</BioLink>'s fiction. *Moby-Dick* is a novel that takes knowledge seriously (it contains chapters on cetology, philosophy, and the technical details of the whaling industry), but its deepest subject resists knowledge entirely: the white whale, blank and inscrutable, onto which Ahab projects all his fury and meaning, and which remains, finally, unknowable. "Bartleby, the Scrivener" pushes this confrontation with inscrutability into a claustrophobic Wall Street office. *Benito Cereno* stages it as a political and racial nightmare.

## The Human Position

*What is a human being, and where do we stand in the order of things?*

The Enlightenment placed the human being at the center of a knowable world, equipped with reason and capable of progress. Romanticism decenters us — not by diminishing human dignity but by insisting that what makes us most fully human is not universal rationality but our capacity for feeling and imagination.

<BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink>'s *Confessions* announces this shift with its extraordinary opening declaration: "I have entered upon a performance which is without example." <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> presents himself as *himself*, in all his contradictions, weaknesses, and passions. "I know my heart, and have studied mankind; I am not made like any one I have been acquainted with, perhaps like no one in existence." He locates the center of selfhood not in reason but in feeling: "An infinity of sensations were familiar to me, without possessing any precise idea of the objects to which they related — I had conceived nothing — I had felt the whole."

<BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink>'s *Nature* takes this inward turn and projects it onto the American landscape. Standing on bare ground, his head "bathed by the blithe air, and uplifted into infinite space," <BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink> experiences a dissolution of the boundary between self and world: "I become a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all." This is the Romantic sublime — the experience of being overwhelmed by something so vast that the ordinary self disappears, replaced by ecstatic unity with nature. It is precisely what <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> and <BioLink id="pope">Pope</BioLink> would have warned against: the abandonment of rational self-possession in favor of visionary transport.

### Connection to *Moby-Dick*

Ahab is the Romantic visionary pushed to his destructive extreme: a man who insists the universe must mean something, who projects his rage and suffering onto the white whale and pursues it to annihilation. Ishmael is the Romantic observer, contemplative and self-aware, capable of the ecstatic merging with the world that <BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink> describes ("meditation and water are wedded for ever"). Queequeg embodies a natural dignity and wholeness that the Western characters have lost. The novel asks what it means to be human in a universe that may not care whether we exist.

## Knowledge and Its Limits

*How do we know what we know, and how far can knowledge take us?*

The Romantic answer is not that reason is useless — <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> insists that imagination and reason are complementary — but that reason alone is radically insufficient. <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink>'s *Defence of Poetry* makes the case with precision: "Reason is the enumeration of qualities already known; imagination is the perception of the value of those qualities, both separately and as a whole. Reason respects the differences, and imagination the similitudes of things."

<PullQuote attribution="Percy Bysshe Shelley, A Defence of Poetry, 1821">
  Reason is the enumeration of qualities already known; imagination is the perception of the value of those qualities, both separately and as a whole. Reason respects the differences, and imagination the similitudes of things.
</PullQuote>

This is why the Romantics elevate the poet above the scientist and the philosopher. Not because poets know more facts, but because they perceive connections and significances that analytical reason misses. Poetry, <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> argues, "lifts the veil from the hidden beauty of the world, and makes familiar objects be as if they were not familiar." In that poetic defamiliarization, something essential about reality becomes visible that was hidden by habit and convention.

<BioLink id="emerson">Emerson</BioLink> makes a parallel argument about nature itself. The problem, he says, is that "few adult persons can see nature." We look at the landscape and see property lines, economic value, familiar objects. The poet sees something else: the living unity beneath the surfaces, the "occult relation between man and the vegetable." This is not anti-rational; it is a claim that rationality, operating alone, produces a diminished picture of the world.

### Connection to *Moby-Dick*

The novel is, among other things, an encyclopedia of whaling, cetology, and maritime custom. Ishmael compulsively classifies, measures, and analyzes. Yet the whale escapes every system of classification. The whiteness of the whale resists interpretation: it can signify purity or terror, the divine or the void. Knowledge proliferates, but meaning recedes. The more Ishmael knows about whales, the less he understands about *this* whale. Reason has reached its limit; what lies beyond is the territory of the sublime.

## The Individual and Authority

*What is the relationship between the person and the structures that govern them?*

In many of its formulations, Romanticism is a literature of rebellion. <BioLink id="rousseau">Rousseau</BioLink> defies the conventions of autobiography by exposing his own vices and weaknesses. <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> declares poets "the unacknowledged legislators of the world," claiming for the visionary imagination an authority that supersedes political power. The Romantic artist is, almost by definition, an outsider: someone who sees truths that society refuses to acknowledge and who pays the price for that vision.

<BioLink id="marx">Marx</BioLink> and Engels, writing in 1848, translate this Romantic energy into political revolution. Their analysis of capitalism as a system that reduces all human relationships to "naked self-interest" and "callous cash payment" is, among other things, a Romantic critique of Enlightenment rationality applied to economics. The bourgeoisie has rationalized everything — and in doing so has destroyed every form of human connection that cannot be expressed as a transaction. "All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned." The *Manifesto* calls for the overthrow of a system that has made the world knowable and profitable at the cost of making it inhuman.

### Connection to *Moby-Dick*, "Bartleby," and *Benito Cereno*

The *Pequod* is a commercial enterprise, and its crew is a global labor force yoked to Ahab's private obsession: the novel asks what happens when a system is commandeered by an individual will that has no interest in the system's stated purposes. "Bartleby" inverts this: a man within the most rationalized of environments simply refuses to participate. "I would prefer not to" is the most quietly devastating act of individual resistance in American literature. *Benito Cereno* reveals that the apparent order aboard a slave ship conceals a reality the American captain cannot read. In all three, authority proves fragile, potentially fraudulent, and dependent on the willing compliance of individuals.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward

**From the Enlightenment:** The Enlightenment trusted reason, valued social order, and believed in progress. Romanticism does not simply reject these commitments — <BioLink id="shelley_pbs">Shelley</BioLink> insists that reason and imagination are both necessary — but it dramatically expands what counts as knowledge and what counts as a legitimate human response to the world. <BioLink id="austen">Austen</BioLink>'s heroines correct their errors through rational self-examination; Ahab is destroyed by the force of his own vision. <BioLink id="kant">Kant</BioLink> said "dare to know"; the Romantics say dare to feel, dare to imagine, dare to confront what you cannot understand.

**Toward Modernism:** The Romantics' faith in the individual imagination, the visionary artist, and the sublime encounter with nature will be severely tested by the catastrophes of the twentieth century. What happens to the Romantic self when it encounters not the white whale but the trenches of the Western Front? What happens to imagination when Freud's unconscious turns out to be not a wellspring of visionary truth but a tangle of repressed desires and childhood wounds? Modernism inherits Romanticism's intensity but loses its confidence. <BioLink id="ellison">Ellison</BioLink>'s invisible man is a Romantic individual — unique, perceptive, passionately alive — trapped in a world that has no category for him.
