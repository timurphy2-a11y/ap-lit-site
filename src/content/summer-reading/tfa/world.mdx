<SectionOpener text="tfa" eyebrow="THINGS FALL APART" heading="The World of the Novel" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

*Things Fall Apart* is set in the late nineteenth century in Igboland — the southeastern region of present-day Nigeria, in the territory of the lower Niger River. The novel follows Okonkwo, a man of considerable standing in the village of Umuofia, across the final years of his world's existence as an autonomous, self-governing community. By the time the novel ends, that world has been enclosed by British colonial administration. The novel was published in 1958, two years before Nigerian independence.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">

The Igbo homeland was not a political nation. Before European colonization, the Igbo lived in hundreds of autonomous village communities, connected by shared language, cultural practices, and religious beliefs, but without a single centralized government or ruler. Each village was a sovereign republic, governing itself through councils of elders, age grades, and a meritocratic title system.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">

This political organization was not a failure to develop centralized governance. It was a fully developed alternative to it. The significance of this distinction becomes clear when the British arrive and discover they have no chiefs to co-opt. The story of what happens next — how the British imposed the fiction of chiefs on a society that had none — is one of the historical through-lines this site traces.

</ProseBlock>

<TermCard text="tfa" term="Umuofia">

The novel's primary setting — one of nine related villages that form a loose confederation. Umuofia is renowned for its military strength and for the power of its *agadi-nwayi*, the spirit of the old woman. It is "never beaten" in war, and its Oracle holds authority over the surrounding region. For the novel's purposes, it is representative: what happens to Umuofia is what happened across Igboland.

</TermCard>

<SectionTerminator text="tfa" />

<SectionOpener text="tfa" eyebrow="THE WORLD ACHEBE BEGINS WITH" heading="Before the Missionaries" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

*Things Fall Apart* does not start with the arrival of missionaries or the establishment of colonial administration. It opens with Okonkwo throwing Amalinze the Cat in a wrestling match. The story begins inside a fully functioning, internally coherent world — its governance, its cosmology, its economy, its social obligations — before any colonial presence has appeared.

</ProseBlock>

<PullQuote text="tfa" attribution="— *Things Fall Apart*, Chinua Achebe">

"Age was respected among his people, but achievement was revered. As the elders said, if a child washed his hands he could eat with kings."

</PullQuote>

<ProseBlock align="right">

This temporal choice constitutes a deliberate literary argument. Readers of *Things Fall Apart* spend the majority of the novel inside a world that works before they see that world disrupted. By the time the missionaries appear, readers already understand what is being disrupted from the inside — not as outsider observers of an alien society, but as people who have been given access to a civilization's internal logic.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">

Chinua Achebe was explicit about why this mattered. He had grown up reading British novels about Africa — including Joyce Cary's *Mister Johnson* — in which Africa existed as backdrop for European narratives and Africans existed without interiority, without history, without fully realized culture. He had been taught, through colonial education, to identify with the white characters in those novels, not the African ones. *Things Fall Apart* was his correction: a novel in which an African civilization is the subject, not the setting, and in which that civilization's destruction is tragedy rather than progress.

</ProseBlock>

<SectionOpener text="tfa" eyebrow="THE STRUCTURAL FOUNDATION" heading="The Yam Economy" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

In Umuofia, the yam is not a crop — it is the organizing principle of social life. Yam farming determines the agricultural calendar, defines masculine status and identity, structures the relationship between wealth and labor, and anchors the ritual year to the earth. To understand how Umuofia works, you have to understand what the yam does.

</ProseBlock>

<SideBySide gap="md">
<div>

<ProseBlock align="left">

Yam cultivation in Igboland was primarily men's work, and it was hard work. The farming season required clearing land, mounding soil, planting, staking, weeding, and harvesting — all labor-intensive, all structured by the agricultural calendar. The size of a man's harvest was a direct index of his standing. "Yam stood for manliness," Achebe writes, "and he who could feed his family on yams from one harvest to another was a great man indeed."

</ProseBlock>

</div>
<div>

<ProseBlock align="left">

The yam economy also structured relations of dependency. A young man without land or resources could approach a more established farmer for seed yams to plant on a share-cropping arrangement. This is the relationship Okonkwo has with Nwakibie at the start of the novel; he borrows 800 seed yams and works under the arrangement until he can establish himself. The terms of that dependency — its obligations, its honor, its risks — shape Okonkwo's character as much as his own ambition does.

</ProseBlock>

</div>
</SideBySide>

<TermCard text="tfa" term="obi">

In Umuofia, a man's compound — his obi, his barns, his wives' huts, the space his family inhabits — is the physical expression of everything he has built. Okonkwo's compound is substantial: three wives, barns full of yams, a reputation established through wrestling, war, and consistent agricultural achievement.

</TermCard>

<TermCard text="tfa" term="harmattan">

The harmattan is the wind of the dry season — the season of rest between harvests, of wrestling matches and storytelling, of the activities that fill the time when the fields lie fallow. It is the season in which the novel begins. The agricultural calendar marks time in *Things Fall Apart* as clearly as the clock marks time in a Western novel; knowing where the community is in its farming year tells the reader what kind of time it is.

</TermCard>

<SectionOpener text="tfa" eyebrow="THE IGBO WEEK" heading="Sacred Time" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

Igbo communities organized time on a four-day week — not the seven-day week of the Western calendar but a cycle of four market days, each named and each carrying distinct associations: Eke, Orie, Afor, and Nkwo. Villages near each other held their markets on different days so that traders could move between them across the week.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">

The Week of Peace is the most sacred period in the Igbo agricultural calendar — the seven days before planting during which the earth goddess Ala prohibits all conflict. No one in Umuofia is to speak harshly, let alone raise a hand. The earth must not be shown anger before it is asked to grow.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">

Okonkwo beats his wife Ojiugo during the Week of Peace — not because he does not know what the Week is, but because his furious, controlling temperament cannot contain itself when he discovers she has gone to braid her hair without preparing the afternoon meal. He is punished by the priest of Ala, Ezeani, who tells him: "You have committed a great evil." The punishment is not merely social; it is cosmological. Okonkwo has shown the earth goddess contempt at the moment she most demands reverence.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">

The Week of Peace episode is the novel's first demonstration of how Igbo sacred time works, and of how Okonkwo's defining flaw operates within it. He is not ignorant of the law. He simply cannot govern himself. This is the crack in the foundation of Igbo culture that the novel will spend the rest of its pages widening.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">

Sacred time in Igboland is not merely a religious concept; it is also an administrative one. The calendar structures when fields are cleared, when yams are planted, when the egwugwu emerge to administer justice, when marriages can be arranged, when wars can be fought. The Igbo week is the infrastructure of community life. Achebe immerses the reader in it before any outside force arrives, so that when that infrastructure is disrupted — when the colonial administration and the church impose their own calendar — the reader already knows what is being replaced.

</ProseBlock>
