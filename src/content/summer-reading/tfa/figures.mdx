<ProseBlock align="center">

The figures on this page are not characters in *Things Fall Apart* — they are the people whose decisions shaped the historical world the novel inhabits, and the writer who made that world visible on the page.

</ProseBlock>

Chinua Achebe was born Albert Chinụalụmọgụ Achebe on November 16, 1930, in Ogidi, in the Onitsha area of colonial Nigeria. His father was a catechist for the Church Missionary Society, and Achebe grew up in a household that was Christian but surrounded by the traditional Igbo culture his father had left. He was educated in both worlds simultaneously — a formation that gave him the vantage point of someone who could see both from the inside.

He attended Government College, Umuahia and then University College, Ibadan, where he studied English. It was at Ibadan, reading the British novels that constituted the English literature curriculum, that he discovered his purpose as a writer. Joyce Cary's *Mister Johnson* crystallized for him what was wrong with Western writing about Africa. He recognized that the entire tradition of European literature about Africa — including Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*, which he would critique directly in his 1975 essay "An Image of Africa" — rendered Africa as backdrop for European moral and psychological dramas, without granting Africans the status of full human beings with interiority, history, and culture.

*Things Fall Apart* was his response: a novel in which an African civilization is the subject, in which an African man's tragedy is rendered with the seriousness and complexity of any tragedy in the Western literary tradition, and in which the colonial disruption of that civilization is witnessed from the inside rather than narrated from above.

In "The Novelist as Teacher" (1965), Achebe wrote that his goal was "to help my society regain belief in itself and to put away the complexes of the years of denigration and self-abasement." He wrote for two audiences simultaneously: Western readers who needed to understand that African civilizations had "poetry and dignity," and African readers who needed to recover pride in a past that colonial education had taught them to regard as primitive.

*Things Fall Apart* was published in 1958 by Heinemann in London, two years before Nigerian independence. It has been translated into more than fifty languages and has sold over twenty million copies. Achebe followed it with *No Longer at Ease* (1960), *Arrow of God* (1964), and *A Man of the People* (1966) — together a panoramic account of Nigerian experience from the pre-colonial past through the postcolonial present.

In 1967, when the southeastern Igbo homeland broke away to form the Republic of Biafra, Achebe chose to stay and serve as a roving cultural ambassador, raising international awareness of the famine produced by the Nigerian government's blockade. He addressed the war's full horror directly only in *There Was a Country: A Personal History of Biafra* (2012), published a year before his death. A 1990 car accident left him partially paralyzed; he moved to the United States, teaching at Bard College and then at Brown University. He received the Man Booker International Prize in 2007, and died on March 21, 2013, in Boston, at eighty-two.

Frederick Lugard was the British military officer and colonial administrator who built the governing framework of colonial Nigeria. He served in the British Army in India, Egypt, and East Africa before being appointed High Commissioner of Northern Nigeria in 1900.

His greatest contribution to British colonial policy — the doctrine of indirect rule — was designed to govern a vast territory with minimal British administrative presence, using existing local authority structures rather than deploying expensive British officials throughout the interior. In the hierarchical Islamic north, with its established emirate system, this worked reasonably well. In the acephalous Igbo south, where no paramount chiefs existed to co-opt, it produced the warrant chief system: the British selected arbitrary individuals, issued them written warrants granting colonial authority, and created a system that was corrupt by design. Warrant chiefs had no traditional legitimacy and depended entirely on British backing, which they frequently used to enrich themselves and settle personal scores.

Lugard amalgamated the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria and the Protectorate of Southern Nigeria into a single Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria in 1914 — an administrative convenience whose consequences persist to the present day. The borders he consolidated enclosed peoples who had no historical relationship to each other, creating a "Nigeria" whose unity was a British administrative fiction. He served as Governor-General until 1919, then returned to Britain, where he continued to write and advocate for indirect rule as colonial policy.

</KeyFigureCard>

Joyce Cary was a British novelist who served as a colonial administrator in Nigeria from 1913 to 1920. His 1939 novel *Mister Johnson* — set in colonial Nigeria and featuring a Nigerian protagonist who enthusiastically embraces British colonial culture — was widely praised in Britain as a sympathetic and humanizing portrait of an African character.

Achebe identified *Mister Johnson* as the immediate provocation for writing *Things Fall Apart*. He had read it as a student and found it deeply condescending. In Cary's novel, Mister Johnson's devotion to his white employer and his ultimate willingness to be killed by him were intended to be touching; to Achebe, they represented the precise failure of imagination he wanted to correct: the inability to grant an African character a full interior life, genuine agency, and the right to be the subject rather than the object of his own story.

Cary does not appear in *Things Fall Apart*. But the tradition he represents — British literature about Africa written by people who had lived in Africa but could not see it on its own terms — is exactly what Achebe was writing against.

</KeyFigureCard>

The Church Missionary Society was founded in London in 1799 by evangelical Anglicans. It became the dominant Protestant missionary organization in West Africa, establishing its first permanent base in Nigeria at Onitsha in 1857 and extending its reach through Igboland over the following decades.

In *Things Fall Apart*, the missionaries who arrive in Umuofia are not specifically identified as CMS, but the pattern of their arrival — first Mr. Brown's patient approach, building a church and a school; then the stricter Mr. Smith's confrontational posture — broadly reflects the mixed character of missionary enterprise in Igboland. The CMS trained indigenous clergy, established schools, and produced the first written materials in Igbo languages. These contributions were real. So were the institutional disruptions they enabled.

The school that Mr. Brown builds in the novel is not a neutral gift. It is the mechanism through which Igbo children will be equipped to serve in the colonial administration — the means through which the governance structures the egwugwu represent will be staffed by people trained to work around them. Mission schools and colonial administration were inseparable in practice, whatever the missionaries' stated intentions.

</KeyFigureCard>

Thomas John Dennis was a British Anglican missionary who came to Nigeria in 1894 and spent the rest of his life there. He learned Igbo, published a grammar and a literacy primer, and undertook the labor of translating the entire Bible into a language he had adopted as his own. He died in 1917 when the ship carrying him home to England was torpedoed by a German submarine. A suitcase containing a draft of his Igbo-English dictionary washed up on a beach in Wales; a fisherman found it, dried the pages, and returned them.

The Igbo language has many dialects, some so distinct that speakers can barely communicate across dialects. When Dennis set out to translate the Bible, he needed a single written form usable across all of them. Working with a committee of Igbo-speaking assistants from 1906, he assembled what he called "Union Igbo" — a standardized written dialect constructed by combining elements from multiple regional varieties. The Union Igbo Bible appeared in 1913. Because the missionaries controlled the schools, Union Igbo became the standard written form taught throughout Igboland.

In a 1994 interview with *The Paris Review*, Achebe was direct: "There is a problem with the Igbo language. It suffers from a very serious inheritance which it received at the beginning of this century from the Anglican mission... The standard version cannot sing. There's nothing you can do with it to make it sing. It's heavy. It's wooden. It doesn't go anywhere."

The living Igbo dialects Achebe grew up hearing were orally rich; the written Igbo available to him was a composite that belonged to no organic oral tradition. Achebe's decision to write in English was not simply a concession to global readership — it was also a response to the fact that the written form of his first language had been shaped into something with no inherent music or cultural grounding. Dennis Memorial Grammar School in Onitsha, one of Nigeria's most distinguished secondary schools, is named in his honor.
