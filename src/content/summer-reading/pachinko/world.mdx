<ProseBlock align="left">
*Pachinko* spans eighty years and four generations. It begins in 1910 — the year Japan annexed Korea — and ends in 1989, at the peak of Japan's economic boom. A family that starts in a Korean fishing village ends in a Tokyo skyscraper.

{/* SECTION 2 — Where the Novel Begins: Yeongdo, Busan */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE STARTING POINT" heading="Yeongdo, Busan" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
The novel opens in Yeongdo, a small island attached to the port city of Busan on Korea's southeastern coast — the closest point on the peninsula to Japan. In 1910, Yeongdo was a fishing community: tight, poor, and largely self-sufficient. Sunja's family runs a boarding house there, feeding and sheltering the men who pass through.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">
The Korea Sunja's family inhabits is already a colonized country. Japan annexed Korea in 1910, the year the novel begins. Land had been surveyed and redistributed; languages were under pressure; the basic economic conditions of Korean rural life were being reorganized to serve Japanese interests. Sunja's poverty is the product of a specific historical process — the novel traces its consequences rather than its mechanisms.
</ProseBlock>

<SectionTerminator text="pachinko" />

{/* SECTION 3 — The Crossing: Busan to Shimonoseki */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE ROUTE" heading="Busan to Shimonoseki" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
Busan mattered geographically because it was the city Koreans crossed from when they came to Japan. The ferry route from Busan to Shimonoseki — roughly 200 kilometers across the Korea Strait — became one of the most heavily traveled corridors in twentieth-century Asia.
</ProseBlock>

{/* SECTION 5 — Where the Novel Moves: Osaka and Tokyo */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE GEOGRAPHY" heading="Osaka and Tokyo" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
After Sunja and Isak arrive in Japan, the novel's geographic center shifts to Osaka — specifically to the Ikaino neighborhood, a working-class district in Osaka's Ikuno ward that became home to the largest concentration of Koreans in Japan.
</ProseBlock>

<TermCard text="pachinko" term="Ikaino" original="猪飼野">
The name encoded what Japanese society thought of the people living there. Korean migrants settled in Ikaino because discrimination in housing blocked them from living elsewhere: Japanese landlords refused to rent to Koreans, and the community built its life in the space that remained. Korean markets, churches, community organizations, and pachinko parlors all took root in Ikaino. It is where Sunja and Kyunghee sell kimchi from a cart, where Isak ministers and is eventually arrested, where Noa and Mozasu grow up.
</TermCard>

<ProseBlock align="right">
The novel's later sections move to Tokyo, where Solomon works in the 1989 chapters. Tokyo represents the generational distance from Ikaino: Solomon is trilingual, internationally educated, and operates in the world of global finance. The move from Ikaino to Tokyo across three generations is one of the novel's measures of what the Zainichi community built — and of how much remains unchanged regardless of these achievements.
</ProseBlock>

<SectionTerminator text="pachinko" />

{/* SECTION 6 — The Four Generations */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE STRUCTURE" heading="Four Generations" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
*Pachinko* is organized around four generations of Sunja's family, each of which inhabits a different legal and historical moment.
</ProseBlock>

<SideBySide gap="md">
<div>
<ProseBlock align="left">
**Issei (first generation): Sunja and Isak.** Born in Korea, arrived in Japan as adults. They are legally Japanese subjects under colonial rule, but socially and economically Korean and marginal. Korea is home; Japan is where they work and survive.
</ProseBlock>
</div>
<div>
<ProseBlock align="left">
**Nisei (second generation): Noa and Mozasu.** Born or raised primarily in Japan. They are Korean by heritage and legal status but have grown up in the only country they know. Noa's driven attempt to assimilate fully, and Mozasu's practical embrace of the pachinko industry, constitute the novel's central pairing.
</ProseBlock>
</div>
</SideBySide>

<ProseBlock align="center">
**Sansei (third generation): Solomon.** Educated in America, fluent in Korean, Japanese, and English, professionally mobile — and still unable to acquire full belonging in Japan. The novel's 1989 sections test whether a generation that has done everything right by the system's standards can escape the condition the system assigned at birth.
</ProseBlock>

{/* SECTION 7 — The Title */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE METAPHOR" heading="Pachinko" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
A pachinko machine is a vertical pinball device, played in large, loud parlors across Japan. Steel balls cascade down through a maze of pins; the player has minimal control over where they land. Winning is largely a matter of chance: the machine is calibrated to produce a few winners and many losers. It skirts Japanese law through a prize-exchange mechanism that has always operated in a legal gray area.
</ProseBlock>

<PullQuote text="pachinko" attribution="— Min Jin Lee, on the novel's organizing metaphor">
Life is like trying to win a game which may have been designed to make the player lose.
</PullQuote>

<ProseBlock align="right">
The Zainichi Korean community came to dominate the pachinko industry not by preference but by exclusion. Barred from public employment and most formal private-sector work, they entered an industry Japanese society disdained and stayed when others left. The parlor is a livelihood chosen from foreclosed alternatives, converted through decades of labor into genuine success in a space the dominant culture refuses to respect.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">
The novel's own answer to that metaphor arrives not from the author but from a character who has watched the game long enough to understand it.
</ProseBlock>

<PullQuote text="pachinko" attribution="— Pachinko, Min Jin Lee">
Pachinko was a foolish game, but life was not.
</PullQuote>
