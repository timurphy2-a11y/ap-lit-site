>
  <SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE STARTING POINT" heading="Yeongdo, Busan" background="tinted" />

  <Beat align="left">
    The novel opens in Yeongdo, a small island attached to the port city of Busan on Korea's southeastern coast — the closest point on the peninsula to Japan. In 1910, Yeongdo was a fishing community: tight, poor, and largely self-sufficient. Sunja's family runs a boarding house there, feeding and sheltering the men who pass through.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="right">
    The Korea Sunja's family inhabits is already a colonized country. Land had been surveyed and redistributed; languages were under pressure; the basic economic conditions of Korean rural life were being reorganized to serve Japanese interests. Sunja's poverty is the product of a specific historical process — the novel traces its consequences rather than its mechanisms.
  </Beat>
</ScrollyZone>

  <SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE ROUTE" heading="Busan to Shimonoseki" background="cream" />

  <Beat align="left" activates="route-base">
    Busan mattered geographically because it was the city Koreans crossed from when they came to Japan.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="left" activates="route-line">
    The ferry route from Busan to Shimonoseki — roughly 200 kilometers across the Korea Strait — became one of the most heavily traveled corridors in twentieth-century Asia.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="right" activates="year-1905">
    In 1905, the route opened as Japan gained protectorate control. The Korea Strait became a managed colonial corridor.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="right" activates="volume-1930">
    At its 1930 peak, 400,000 Koreans entered Japan in a single year — 80,000 to 150,000 crossing annually through the late 1920s and 1930s.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="center" activates="inspection">
    Travelers were required to prove they carried at least ten yen, demonstrate Japanese-language ability, and confirm employment.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="center" activates="ikaino-marker">
    Those who passed inspection arrived in Osaka and built a community in the Ikaino neighborhood — the largest concentration of Koreans in Japan. Despite the requirements, the crossings did not slow; scheduled ships could not keep up with demand.
  </Beat>
</ScrollyZone>

<ScrollyZone
  field="cascade"
  tempo="driving"
  ground="tinted"
  text="pachinko"
>
  <SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE HISTORY" heading="1876 to 1989" background="tinted" />

  <Beat align="left">
    *Pachinko* spans the entire arc of Japan's colonial and postcolonial relationship with Korea — from the first forced opening of Korean ports to the year the novel ends, two years before Japan's bubble economy collapsed.
  </Beat>

  <div class="sr-cascade-entries">
  <div class="sr-zone-cascade-progress"></div>

  <CascadeEntry date="1876">
    Japan forces Korea to open its ports through an unequal treaty, beginning a half-century of expanding Japanese dominance.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1905">
    Following victory in the Russo-Japanese War, Japan compels Korea to become a protectorate, stripping it of independent foreign policy.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1910" highlight={true}>
    Japan formally annexes Korea. The treaty is signed not by the Korean emperor but by his prime minister, Yi Wan-yong — remembered in Korea as its most reviled collaborator. Thirty-five years of colonial rule begin. *The novel starts here.*
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1919">
    The March First Movement: an estimated two million Koreans participate in nationwide demonstrations for independence. Japan crushes the protests, killing thousands. Isak's older brother Sameol dies in connection with these events.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1923">
    The Great Kantō Earthquake devastates Tokyo and Yokohama. In the chaos that follows, rumors spread that Koreans have poisoned water supplies; Japanese vigilante groups and police massacre several thousand Koreans living in Japan.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1930s" highlight={true}>
    Agricultural crisis and colonial dispossession accelerate Korean emigration. 400,000 Koreans enter Japan in 1930 alone. Sunja and Isak arrive in Osaka, joining a community of several hundred thousand Koreans concentrated in Ikaino.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1940">
    The *sōshi-kaimei* name-change policy requires all Koreans to register Japanese-style family names. Officially voluntary; in practice, those who refuse lose access to employment, schools, and services.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1945">
    Japan surrenders. Colonial rule ends. Approximately two million Koreans are living in Japan; most eventually repatriate, but roughly 600,000 remain.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1950–1953">
    The Korean War devastates the peninsula and divides it permanently. Return to Korea becomes still less viable for those who stayed in Japan.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1952" highlight={true}>
    The San Francisco Peace Treaty takes effect. Japan formally recognizes Korean independence — and simultaneously strips Zainichi Koreans of Japanese nationality, making them legally stateless overnight.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1965">
    Japan and South Korea normalize relations. Zainichi Koreans affiliated with South Korea gain "special permanent residency" — a legal status that provides security but falls short of citizenship.
  </CascadeEntry>

  <CascadeEntry date="1989" highlight={true}>
    The novel's final sections. Japan is at the peak of its bubble economy. Solomon works at a Japanese branch of a British bank in Tokyo. *The novel ends here.*
  </CascadeEntry>

  </div>
</ScrollyZone>

  <SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE GEOGRAPHY" heading="Osaka and Tokyo" background="cream" />

  <Beat align="left" activates="osaka-marker">
    After Sunja and Isak arrive in Japan, the novel's geographic center shifts to Osaka — specifically to the Ikaino neighborhood, a working-class district in Osaka's Ikuno ward that became home to the largest concentration of Koreans in Japan.
  </Beat>

  <Beat
    align="center"
    variant="termcard"
    word="猪飼野"
    romaji="Ikaino"
    gloss="a field where pigs are raised"
  >
    The name encoded what Japanese society thought of the people living there. Korean migrants settled in Ikaino because discrimination in housing blocked them from living elsewhere. Korean markets, churches, community organizations, and pachinko parlors all took root in Ikaino. It is where Sunja and Kyunghee sell kimchi from a cart, where Isak ministers and is eventually arrested, where Noa and Mozasu grow up.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="right" activates="tokyo-marker">
    The novel's later sections move to Tokyo, where Solomon works in the 1989 chapters. The move from Ikaino to Tokyo across three generations is one of the novel's measures of what the Zainichi community built — and of how much remains unchanged regardless of these achievements.
  </Beat>
</ScrollyZone>

  <SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE METAPHOR" heading="Pachinko" background="cream" />

  <Beat align="left">
    A pachinko machine is a vertical pinball device, played in large, loud parlors across Japan. Steel balls cascade down through a maze of pins; the player has minimal control over where they land.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="right">
    Winning is largely a matter of chance: the machine is calibrated to produce a few winners and many losers.
  </Beat>

  <Beat align="center">
    The Zainichi Korean community came to dominate the pachinko industry not by preference but by exclusion. Barred from public employment and most formal private-sector work, they entered an industry Japanese society disdained and stayed when others left.
  </Beat>
