{/* SECTION 1 — Annexation and Dispossession */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="HOW JAPAN TOOK KOREA" heading="Annexation and Dispossession" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
Korea did not fall quickly. Japan dismantled it methodically over three decades: forced treaties in 1876, protectorate status in 1905, formal annexation in 1910. At each stage, Korean sovereignty was stripped while the appearance of an independent state was maintained long enough for the next concession to be extracted.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">
Formal annexation came on August 29, 1910. The treaty was signed not by Emperor Sunjong but by Prime Minister Yi Wan-yong, who served as Japan's collaborator and became the most reviled figure in modern Korean history. Japan's Resident-General had suppressed all Korean newspapers in the days before signing to prevent public knowledge of what was being negotiated. Korea was renamed *Chōsen* and governed by a Governor-General — always a Japanese military officer appointed directly by the Japanese Emperor.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">
The annexation's legal validity has been disputed ever since. When Japan and South Korea normalized relations in 1965, the compromise language declared all pre-1910 treaties "already null and void" — deliberately ambiguous about whether they were invalid from the start or merely rescinded. That ambiguity remains a live political dispute: a disagreement, fundamentally, over whether Japan owes a full accounting of what it did.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">
The colonial economy's most consequential instrument was the **Land Survey of 1910–1918**. The survey required all landowners to formally register their land under a new Japanese property rights system. Korean farmers whose families had cultivated land for generations under customary tenure arrangements lost that land when they failed to register correctly. Village commons and clan-owned land — belonging to no individual and impossible to register — were expropriated and transferred cheaply to Japanese buyers.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">
By the 1930s, approximately 70% of Korean agricultural workers had been reduced to tenant status, paying rents of 50–70% of their harvest to Japanese and Japanese-allied Korean landlords. The combination of land dispossession, rack-renting, and agricultural crisis drove the migration that created the Zainichi community. The approximately two million Koreans who emigrated to Japan during the colonial period were not voluntary migrants in any meaningful sense.
</ProseBlock>

{/* SECTION 2 — Postwar Rupture: The San Francisco Moment */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="1952" heading="From Subjects to Stateless" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
Japan's treatment of the Zainichi community after 1945 is one of the most precisely documented cases of systematic legal dispossession in modern history. The mechanism was not a single dramatic act of exclusion but a series of administrative decisions — each one narrowing the community's legal standing until, in 1952, the process reached its logical conclusion.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">
In December 1945, Korean voting rights were revoked on the grounds that Koreans were not Japanese. In 1947, the Alien Registration Ordinance classified Koreans as foreigners — despite the fact that they were still technically Japanese nationals. The Japanese government maintained both positions simultaneously: Koreans were too foreign to vote, but too Japanese to establish their own ethnic schools.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">
The San Francisco Peace Treaty took effect in April 1952. Nine days before it came into force, the Director-General of the Civil Affairs Bureau in the Ministry of Justice issued a circular notice to officials, announcing that all Koreans — including those residing in Japan — were to lose their Japanese nationality. No legislation was passed. No individual choice was offered. Overnight, 600,000 people became legally stateless. (Iwasawa, *International Law, Human Rights, and Japanese Law*, Oxford University Press, 1998, pp. 130–31.)
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">
The practical consequences were immediate and comprehensive: all public-sector employment closed to non-citizens; medical insurance, pension, and public housing restricted to citizens. In 1952, 61% of Zainichi Koreans were unemployed. The largest recorded occupation — 6.6% of the community — was day laboring. The pachinko industry was one of the few economic margins left open.
</ProseBlock>

{/* SECTION 3 — The Legal Arc, 1945–1992 */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="FORTY-SEVEN YEARS" heading="The Legal Arc, 1945–1992" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
The legal status of the Zainichi community did not stabilize after 1952 — it remained in flux for four more decades, improving incrementally through political campaigns the community organized and sustained over generations. Each milestone in the table below is a reform that was fought for; none of them resolved the fundamental condition.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">
The school closure crisis of 1948 is the period's most dramatic episode. After liberation in 1945, the Zainichi community rebuilt Korean-language schooling from nothing: within three years, 600 ethnic schools were operating across Japan, enrolling approximately 60,000 children. In January 1948, the Japanese government ordered them closed. On April 24, approximately 15,000 students, teachers, and parents assembled near the Hyogo prefectural office in Kobe. Hundreds entered the building and forced the governor to revoke the order.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">
General MacArthur's GHQ declared a state of emergency. Around 1,700 people were arrested; 136 faced military trial. Students sent postcards addressed directly to MacArthur demanding the release of their teachers. Those postcards survive in the U.S. National Archives — the physical record of a community fighting, from the margins, for the right to educate its own children.
</ProseBlock>

{/* SECTION 4 — The Pachinko Industry */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="EXCLUSION AS FOUNDATION" heading="The Pachinko Industry" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">
The pachinko industry's relationship to the Zainichi community is a direct consequence of the discrimination described above — and a precise illustration of how systematic exclusion shapes economic life. Zainichi Koreans did not choose pachinko. They entered it because the system left them few other options, and stayed because, when others left, they could not.
</ProseBlock>

<TermCard text="pachinko" term="pachinko" original="パチンコ">
Gambling is formally prohibited in Japan. Pachinko circumvents this through a prize-exchange mechanism: players win tokens or prizes that are then exchanged for cash at separate kiosks nearby. This quasi-legal structure made Japanese investors reluctant to commit to the industry. When a 1954 regulatory change caused a significant contraction — the number of parlors collapsed from 50,000 to 9,000 — Japanese owners could leave for other work. Zainichi Koreans, excluded from most formal employment, had no such exit. They stayed. As the industry recovered and expanded, Korean ownership grew dramatically.
</TermCard>

<ProseBlock align="right">
By the 1990s, pachinko had become a 19 trillion yen annual industry — roughly twice the export revenues of Japan's entire car industry. Zainichi Koreans accounted for approximately 90% of sales.
</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">
The stigma attached to pachinko — its association with gambling, quasi-legality, and Korean identity — was produced by the same discrimination that made Korean participation necessary. In *Pachinko*, this dynamic runs through every generation: Mozasu's success in the industry is real and hard-won; what it cannot buy is the respect the dominant culture withholds on principle.
</ProseBlock>

{/* SECTION 5 — Chongryon and Mindan */}

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="A COMMUNITY DIVIDED" heading="Chongryon and Mindan" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="center">
After 1945, the Zainichi community faced a choice it had not made and could not avoid: Cold War geopolitics divided the Korean peninsula and forced the diaspora to take sides.
</ProseBlock>

<SideBySide gap="md">
<div>
<ProseBlock align="left">
**Mindan** — Korean Residents Union in Japan, founded 1946. Affiliated with South Korea. Mindan provided legal assistance, community services, Korean-language education, and political representation to Zainichi Koreans aligned with the Republic of Korea. After the 1965 normalization treaty, Mindan-affiliated Koreans gained a pathway to special permanent residency.
</ProseBlock>
</div>
<div>
<ProseBlock align="left">
**Chongryon** — General Association of Korean Residents in Japan, founded 1955. Operating as North Korea's *de facto* diplomatic representative in Japan. In the 1950s and 1960s, when North Korea's economy outperformed South Korea's, Chongryon was the dominant Zainichi organization. It ran Korean-language schools, provided community loans, and organized the mass repatriation campaign of 1959–1984 that sent approximately 90,000 people to North Korea — most of whom found conditions far worse than promised and were unable to leave.
</ProseBlock>
</div>
</SideBySide>

<ProseBlock align="left">
For a Zainichi Korean family in the 1950s and 1960s, affiliation with one organization or the other determined which schools their children attended, which banks would lend to their businesses, and what legal assistance they could access. The division was not of the community's making: it was imposed by Cold War geopolitics on people who, in most cases, simply wanted to continue their lives in the country where they already lived.
</ProseBlock>

<TermCard text="pachinko" term="Zainichi Kankoku/Chōsenjin" original="在日韓国・朝鮮人">
The term *Zainichi* (在日) — literally "residing in Japan" — became the community's common self-designation across both political affiliations. Its neutrality was the point: it named a condition (living in Japan) rather than a political loyalty (South or North Korea) or a legal status (alien). In the novel, the word carries the weight of everything the community built in the space between those categories.
</TermCard>

<ProseBlock align="right">
Hansu's dismissal of both organizations in the novel — his observation that neither group's leadership ever actually intends to return to Korea — reflects something Min Jin Lee found repeatedly in her research: the Zainichi community was often clear-eyed about the gap between organizational political rhetoric and the reality of people's lives. The organizations were necessary. They were also, for many in the community, beside the point.
</ProseBlock>
