<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="KOREAN IDENTITY" heading="Living a Double Life" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

To be Korean in Japan between 1910 and 1989 was to live a double life — not by choice, but by necessity. At home, one language; in public, another. One name on the family register, another on the workplace form. The inner life conducted in Korean; the external world navigated in Japanese. *Pachinko* is a novel about what this doubling does to people across generations: what it costs, what it preserves, and what it destroys.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">

Japan's colonial administration did not simply govern Korea; it attempted, in stages, over thirty-five years, to replace Korean identity with Japanese identity. The early colonial period restricted Korean-language instruction and controlled Korean-language publications. The March First Movement of 1919 — in which two million Koreans demonstrated for independence — was crushed with mass arrests and killings.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">

The middle period brought modest relaxation without changing the fundamental terms of colonial subordination. The final decade reversed even these concessions. The *naisen ittai* ideology — "Japan and Korea as One Body" — declared that Koreans and Japanese were racially and culturally unified, which meant that Koreans must become Japanese. Korean-language instruction was discontinued entirely. Koreans were required to attend Shinto shrines and perform rituals of loyalty to the Japanese emperor. Then came the policy that runs through *Pachinko* like a fault line.

</ProseBlock>

<SectionTerminator text="pachinko" />

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THREE PRESSURES" heading="Language, Names, Religion" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

The colonial assault on Korean identity worked through three specific dimensions of daily life simultaneously — language, names, and religion. Each operated through different mechanisms. Together, they constituted what historians describe as a policy of ethnocide: the systematic destruction of national identity, not by killing people but by forcing them to become Japanese, on paper and in public.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="right">

**Language.** Japanese became the mandatory language of education from the start of colonial rule. Korean-language instruction was progressively restricted through the 1920s and 1930s. In 1938, the same year Japanese authorities banned Korean-language instruction from schools entirely, a three-part oath was introduced for Korean children to recite every morning:

</ProseBlock>

<PullQuote text="pachinko" attribution="— Oath of the Imperial Subjects, 1938; required daily recitation in all Korean schools. Translation from Robinson, Korea's Twentieth-Century Odyssey, p. 94.">

*We are subjects of imperial Japan.*
*We will work together to loyally serve the emperor.*
*We will train ourselves with discipline and become strong subjects.*

</PullQuote>

<ProseBlock align="left">

**Names.** In 1940, the *sōshi-kaimei* policy required every Korean household to register a Japanese-style family name. It was officially described as voluntary. In practice, Koreans who refused lost access to employment, schools, hospitals, and government services. By mid-1940, roughly 80% of Korean households had registered Japanese names — most of them under coercion.

</ProseBlock>

<TermCard text="pachinko" term="sōshi-kaimei" original="創氏改名">

The policy produced the central daily negotiation of the Zainichi experience. A Korean name, in this world, is a liability — it marks the bearer as Zainichi, triggering discrimination in hiring, housing, lending, and daily social interaction. *Sōshi-kaimei* made the Korean name a document that had to be hidden, and the Japanese name a performance that had to be sustained.

</TermCard>

<ProseBlock align="right">

**Religion.** In 1938, the Kyōwakai (Mutual Assistance Society), Japan's compulsory organization for Koreans living in Japan, began enforcing mandatory attendance at Shinto shrines as acts of loyalty to the Japanese emperor. Christian pastors who refused to perform the shrine rituals or who continued ministering to their congregations in Korean were arrested. This is the charge that results in Isak's arrest and imprisonment — not sedition, not political organizing, but the refusal to subordinate his faith and his community to colonial ritual.

</ProseBlock>

<SectionTerminator text="pachinko" />

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE COMMUNITY" heading="Zainichi" background="cream" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

The Zainichi community emerged from colonial economic disruption — the land dispossession and agricultural collapse that impoverished Korean farmers and drove approximately two million Koreans to emigrate to Japan in search of work. By 1945, roughly two million Koreans were living in Japan. Most eventually repatriated, but about 600,000 remained, unable or unwilling to return to a divided, devastated peninsula.

</ProseBlock>

<PullQuote text="pachinko" attribution="— Min Jin Lee, describing the challenge of making the case for *Pachinko*'s subject">

"Who cares about 600,000 people."

</PullQuote>

<ProseBlock align="right">

Lee's statement is not resignation; it is the obstacle she set out to overcome. The Zainichi community was large enough to constitute one of Japan's most significant ethnic minority populations, small enough to be invisible to the broader world, and systematically excluded from any opportunity to tell its own story. *Pachinko* is, explicitly, an act of correction.

</ProseBlock>

Sunja and Isak carry their Korean identities intact — names, language, food, faith — because their lives are lived primarily within the Korean community of Ikaino. The *tsūmei* question barely touches them. What they carry from Korea is still real; the place they left still exists in memory.

<ProseBlock align="left">

**Nisei (second generation):** Born or raised in Japan. Korea is a language spoken at home, a set of foods and customs, an identity category on a legal document. Japan is the only country they know, yet Japan stripped them of Japanese nationality in 1952, making them people without a state. Noa's response is total assimilation. Mozasu's is pragmatic acceptance. Neither resolves the structural condition.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">

**Sansei (third generation):** Solomon. Educated abroad, trilingual, professionally mobile. The generation that has done everything that the system ostensibly rewards. The name question has not disappeared; it has taken a new, subtler form. When the land deal falls apart because of who Solomon is rather than what he has done, the distance between his education and his circumstances becomes the novel's final measurement of the impact of structural exclusion.

</ProseBlock>

<SectionOpener text="pachinko" eyebrow="THE EVERYDAY TEXTURE" heading="Two Names, Two Lives" background="tinted" />

<ProseBlock align="left">

The *tsūmei* — the Japanese passing name — is the daily mechanism through which Zainichi Koreans navigated a world that excluded them. Not a dramatic act of identity, but a persistent, low-grade performance: the name used at work, at school, at the bank, with the landlord. The name that kept the Korean name hidden.

</ProseBlock>

<TermCard text="pachinko" term="tsūmei" original="通名">

The *tsūmei* is not a pseudonym and not a renunciation. It is a survival tool. Every Zainichi Korean character in *Pachinko* navigates the space between their Korean name and their *tsūmei* — a navigation that is not dramatic but daily, habitual, and exhausting. A choice of name signals power, intimacy, danger, and belonging all at once. For Noa, the Japanese name becomes the self he is constructing; for Mozasu, it is simply the name he uses at work; for Solomon, it is both and neither.

</TermCard>

<ProseBlock align="right">

Inside the Ikaino community, the Korean name was simply a marker of true identity. Outside it — in the landlord's office, at the school gate, in the factory hiring hall — the calculation began. The doubling was not confined to language or name. It extended to food, to custom, to the decision of whether or not to tell someone where you were from. Min Jin Lee has described interviewing Zainichi Koreans who had spent their entire adult lives concealing their identity from Japanese colleagues, neighbors, and in some cases from their own children.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="left">

What the novel renders, in a way that no historical account can capture, is the texture of this concealment across a lifetime. Sunja selling kimchi from a cart in a neighborhood where she is surrounded by other Koreans is one kind of experience. Noa, reading English literature at a Japanese university, constructing an elaborate Japanese persona over years of methodical self-erasure, is another. Solomon, managing the performance across three languages and two continents, is a third. The *tsūmei* is the same institution in all three cases; what it costs is different every time.

</ProseBlock>

<ProseBlock align="center">

Lee spent decades researching this material, conducting hundreds of interviews across Japan. Nearly every Zainichi Korean she interviewed had some connection to the pachinko industry. The industry was not a choice; it was one of the few spaces the exclusion left open. That a disreputable industry entered through necessity became, through generations of labor, a community's economic foundation — this is the novel's central symbol of what survival under exclusion actually looks like.

</ProseBlock>
