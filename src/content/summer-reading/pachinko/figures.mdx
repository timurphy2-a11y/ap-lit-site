<ProseBlock align="center">

The figures on this page are not characters in *Pachinko* — they are the people whose decisions shaped the world the novel's characters inhabit.

</ProseBlock>

Yi Wan-yong signed the Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty on August 22, 1910 — not because he was the Korean emperor, but because Japan installed him in a position to sign it. His signature on the treaty's first article — declaring that Korea's emperor "completely and permanently" ceded all sovereignty to Japan — made him the most despised figure in modern Korean history. The Korean word for collaborator, *chinilpa*, is inseparable from his name.

Yi's significance for *Pachinko* is structural rather than explicit. He never appears in the novel. But his act is the premise of everything that follows: the annexation he signed into existence is the historical fact that sends Sunja's family into poverty, drives her migration to Japan, and sets in motion the eighty-year history the novel traces. He is also a reminder that colonial history required Korean collaborators as much as Japanese administrators — a moral complexity that runs through the novel even when it is not named directly.

</KeyFigureCard>

<KeyFigureCard

  summary="Yu Gwan-sun was arrested at seventeen for organizing independence demonstrations after the March First Movement, died in prison at eighteen, and became the symbolic martyr of the Korean independence movement."
  id="figure-yu"
>

Yu Gwan-sun was seventeen when Japanese authorities arrested her for organizing independence demonstrations in her home province after the March First Movement's outbreak in 1919. She had already been expelled from her school in Seoul when Japan closed schools in response to the protests. She organized local demonstrations, was arrested, tried before a military tribunal, and sentenced to three years in Seodaemun Prison. She continued to protest inside the prison on the anniversary of March 1, 1920, was tortured in response, and died on September 28, 1920, at eighteen. Prison records listed the cause of death as "illness."

She became the symbolic martyr of the Korean independence movement, a figure comparable in Korean national memory to Joan of Arc. She is now commemorated as a national hero in South Korea.

Her significance for *Pachinko* is specific: the world Sunja's family inhabits in the novel's early sections is defined by what happened to people like Yu Gwan-sun — the violent suppression of the March First Movement, the arrests of Christian leaders, the crushing of organized resistance. Isak's brother Sameol dies in connection with these events.

</KeyFigureCard>

Minami Jirō governed Korea during the most aggressive phase of cultural suppression — the period the novel's characters experience directly. Under his administration: Korean-language instruction was discontinued entirely; Korean-language newspapers were shut down; the *naisen ittai* ideology (Japan and Korea as one body) was implemented as official policy; Shinto shrine worship was enforced; and the *sōshi-kaimei* name-change campaign was launched in 1940.

He was convicted of war crimes by the Tokyo Trials in 1948 and sentenced to life imprisonment. He was paroled in 1954 and died the following year.

He is the administrative architect of the system that Isak is arrested for resisting. When Isak refuses to bow at a Shinto shrine and is taken by Japanese police, the policy he is refusing is Minami's policy.

</KeyFigureCard>

<KeyFigureCard

  accent="pachinko"
  summary="Lee first encountered the story that became <em>Pachinko</em> at nineteen, at a lecture about Koreans in Japan. She spent nearly thirty years researching Zainichi Korean communities, conducting hundreds of interviews, before the novel was published in 2017."
  id="figure-lee"
>

Lee was born in Seoul, emigrated with her family to the United States as a child, grew up in New York, attended Yale University and Yale Law School, and practiced briefly as a lawyer before turning to writing. She encountered the story that became *Pachinko* at nineteen, at a Yale lecture by an American missionary working with Koreans in Japan. It stayed with her for nearly thirty years.

She spent much of that time working on what became the novel — first as a short story, then as an early draft focused on a single generation, then as a complete restart after moving to Tokyo with her husband in 2007. Living in Japan gave her direct access to the community she was writing about. She conducted hundreds of interviews — with Zainichi Koreans across multiple generations, pachinko industry workers and owners, community organizers, and historians. Nearly every family she interviewed had some connection to the pachinko industry at some point.

Lee has described her motivations with unusual directness. Describing the writing of *Pachinko* as an act of activism, she has drawn explicit connections between the Zainichi experience and the experience of minorities elsewhere who exist in societies that claim not to see race while organizing themselves around it. The novel was a finalist for the National Book Award for Fiction in 2017.

</KeyFigureCard>

<KeyFigureCard
  name="Mindan &amp; Chongryon"
  eyebrow="The Two Associations"
  accent="pachinko"
  summary="Mindan and Chongryon divided the Zainichi Korean community along Cold War lines — South Korea versus North Korea — determining which schools families sent children to, which banks would lend to their businesses, and what legal status they could claim."
  id="figure-associations"
>

Mindan (Korean Residents Union in Japan, founded 1946) is affiliated with South Korea. It provided legal assistance, community services, Korean-language education, and political representation to Zainichi Koreans aligned with the Republic of Korea. After the 1965 normalization treaty, Mindan-affiliated Koreans gained a pathway to special permanent residency.

Chongryon (General Association of Korean Residents in Japan, founded 1955) operates as North Korea's de facto diplomatic representation in Japan. In the 1950s and 1960s, when North Korea's economy outperformed South Korea's, Chongryon was the dominant Zainichi organization. It ran Korean-language schools, provided community loans, and organized the mass repatriation campaign of 1959–1984 that sent approximately 90,000 people to North Korea — most of whom found conditions far worse than promised and were unable to leave.

For a Zainichi Korean family in the 1950s and 1960s, affiliation with one organization or the other determined which schools your children attended, which banks would lend to your business, and what legal assistance you could access. The division was imposed not by Zainichi Koreans themselves but by Cold War geopolitics that split the Korean peninsula and forced the diaspora to choose sides.

Hansu's dismissal of both organizations in the novel — his observation that neither group's leadership ever actually intends to return to Korea — reflects something Lee found in her research: the Zainichi community was often clear-eyed about the gap between organizational political rhetoric and the reality of people's lives.
